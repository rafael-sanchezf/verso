# Attribute genome: how Pandora chose the next song, and what Verso should take from it

Research compiled 2026-10-29 for VER-303, after Rafa asked that day for research on "how pandora did song recommendations and how we can optimize it for our use (different attributes, getting the closest next song, multi-dimensional pythagoras theorem)", adding "i think we NEED that for our rec engine". Four research threads fed it: Pandora's Music Genome Project, read from its patent, Tim Westergren's sworn 2014 testimony, the 2011 S-1 and Pandora-authored papers; the attribute vocabularies that exist for films and games and the terms they come under; whether a language model can build a genome, how reliably and at what cost; and the math of distance and of learning attribute weights from a ranked list, with the researchers' own worked examples, simulations and pgvector timings on the local Supabase image. It builds on the engine as designed and built ([docs/design/recommendation-engine.md](../design/recommendation-engine.md)) and on the research behind it ([recommendation-engine.md](recommendation-engine.md)), and does not repeat what they settle: the community prior, layer 1's content profile (an idf-weighted sum of binary features scored by cosine), the leave-one-out harness `recommendation_loo`, the prediction written at every insertion into `prediction_calibration` under `model_version` content-v1, and the blend. "We NEED that" is treated as a hypothesis, under the repo's rule that an engine layer ships only if it beats the one before it in the harness. Nothing in this report is decided. Numbers marked as the researchers' own are their simulations, arithmetic or measurements, not published results.

## Summary

Pandora's Music Genome Project gave every song a vector of a few hundred "genes", traits a trained musician scored from 0 to 5 in about 15 minutes a song, and its patent picks the next song by weighted squared Euclidean distance. That is Rafa's "multi-dimensional Pythagorean theorem": the hypotenuse rule extended to every gene, with the square root dropped and each squared gap multiplied by a weight. With several seed songs, each gene is weighted by the inverse of the seeds' variance on it, so shared traits decide the station. The weights were hand-set; thumbs, a per-pair veto and collaborative filtering came later, and by 2013 the genome was one of more than 50 algorithms, credited mainly with cold start and the long tail. It fell short on cost (low millions of songs in 25 years), on unpublished rater noise, and on songs that match on paper but not in audience. The 2026-09-24 verdict that a genome is out of reach no longer holds: Claude Haiku 5.5 could score all 69,183 games for $8 to $42, though every attribute needs validating against human scores and the TMDB and Twitch terms must be cleared first. The math needs three changes for Verso: graded attributes get their own weighted Euclidean term beside today's cosine on tags; a person is scored by closeness to their own loved items or a few prototypes, never their average, since two clusters of taste cancel on a graded axis; and personal weights come from the ranked list, shrunk toward everyone's, at about one weight per three to five ranked items. "We NEED that" is unproven, but Verso sits where Pandora said the genome earns its keep. The cheapest test is a pilot on the games already ranked, run in the harness against today's scorer and against IGDB's free themes and keywords.

## Pandora scored each song on a few hundred traits, by hand, from 0 to 5

A gene is one musical trait that an analyst scores for one song. The patent defines each as "a number between 0 and 5", with fractional values limited to half steps, and allows a scaling function because gene values are not linear ([US 7,003,515 B1, filed 2002-05-16, granted 2006-02-21](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en)). Pandora-authored work in 2025 confirms the 0-to-5 annotation and explains that many genes run from incidental to dominant: 0 means absent, a low score a minor role, a high score the lead role ([Oramas et al., MGPHot, TISMIR 8(1), 2025](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.236)). Two weaker sources disagree on the scale: Westergren's sworn testimony says most traits get 1 to 5 by prevalence ([CRB Web IV, Westergren written direct testimony, para 26, 2014](https://www.crb.gov/rate/14-CRB-0001-WR/statements/Pandora/7_Written_Direct_Testimony_of_Timothy_Westergren_PUBLIC_pdf.pdf)), and Òscar Celma's thesis, citing Pandora staff in 2007, reports 0 to 10 ([Celma, PhD thesis, section 3.4, 2008](https://mtg.upf.edu/static/media/PhD_ocelma.pdf)). The patent and the 2025 paper are the strongest sources, and they agree.

The count of genes has never been one number. The patent gives about 150 for rock and pop, 350 for rap, about 400 for jazz and 300 to 500 for world and classical. Pandora's own statements drift over the years: "up to 480" in the 2011 S-1 ([Pandora Media S-1, 2011-02](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001230276/000119312511032963/ds1.htm)), "up to 450" on a 2013 conference slide by Pandora's Eric Bieschke ([Bieschke, MLconf slides, 2013-11](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/eric-bieschke-slides/28431664)), 148 to 450 by genre in the 2014 testimony, "up to 400" on the corporate page read 2026-10-29 ([Pandora: About the Music Genome Project](https://www.pandora.com/corporate/mgp.shtml)), and "hundreds" in the 2025 paper. The count varies because the vocabulary varies: there are five separate genomes (Pop/Rock, Jazz, Hip-Hop/Electronica, World and Classical), built by Westergren with the Stanford musicologist Nolan Gasser, each with its own genes on instrumentation, tempo, form, melody, harmony and lyrics ([CRB Web IV, paras 7-8, 26](https://www.crb.gov/rate/14-CRB-0001-WR/statements/Pandora/7_Written_Direct_Testimony_of_Timothy_Westergren_PUBLIC_pdf.pdf)). A distance needs vectors of equal length, so a jazz song and a rap song are not directly comparable, and the patent describes no bridge between genomes. When Pandora moved into podcasts in 2018 it built a separate Podcast Genome of more than 1,500 attributes rather than extend music's ([TechCrunch, 2018-11-13](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/13/pandora-brings-its-genome-technology-to-podcast-recommendations/amp/), read through a search summary). One genome per medium is Pandora's own pattern, and it matches Verso's rule 24, "same loop, own product".

The full gene list is a trade secret. The 2025 paper released 58 genes in seven families for 21,320 Billboard Hot 100 tracks, and their names show the mix: Vocal Grittiness and Vocal Nasality; Harmonic Sophistication; Swing Feel, Backbeat and Danceability; Electric Guitar and Synthesizer presence; Aural Intensity and Live Recording; Focus on Riffs; and lyric tone as Angry, Sad, Love/Romance and Explicit ([TISMIR 2025](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.236), relayed by a page summary). Some genes are presence or intensity of an instrument, some are perceptual qualities, and some are judgments of style. The judged ones are where two analysts are most likely to disagree.

The analysts hold a four-year degree in music theory, composition or performance, pass an entrance exam, and train by scoring the same song and comparing results until they agree ([Pandora MGP page](https://www.pandora.com/corporate/mgp.shtml); [CRB Web IV, fn 5](https://www.crb.gov/rate/14-CRB-0001-WR/statements/Pandora/7_Written_Direct_Testimony_of_Timothy_Westergren_PUBLIC_pdf.pdf)). A simple pop song took "roughly 15 minutes" under oath in 2014; the 2025 paper says a few minutes for simple tracks and about 30 for complex ones; older Pandora copy quoted by Celma said 20 to 30. The team was never large: thirty around 2008, about 20 in 2011, 25 in 2013 and thirty again under oath in 2014, by the sources above. Throughput was about 15,000 songs a month around 2008, which Celma called a clear scalability problem. The catalog grew from more than 800,000 songs in February 2011 (S-1) to "over 1.5 million" in 2014 ([MLconf 2014 abstract](https://mlconf.com/sessions/pandora-is-best-known-for-the-music-genome-project/)), under 2 million in 2017 against 30 million or more at on-demand rivals ([The Outline, 2017-01-11](https://theoutline.com/post/877/the-music-genome-project-isn-t-done)), and "millions" by 2025.

Quality control was partial. Pandora describes "redundant analysis" and monthly review of a share of each analyst's songs, and its 2025 paper calls it impractical to analyze every song twice; Wikipedia puts the double-scored share at about 10% ([Wikipedia: Music Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_Genome_Project)). No inter-analyst agreement figure has ever been published, so how noisy a single gene score is remains unknown. The dollar cost of the technology is redacted in the public testimony (para 30), and no cost per song is public. The researchers' own arithmetic from a weak job-board listing of $50,000 to $62,000 a year ([Rise job listing, via search summary](https://app.joinrise.co/jobs/music-analyst-1-1cab)) gives about $6 to $8 of analyst salary per song at 15 minutes, before review, tooling and overhead. The binding cost was never money per song. It was calendar time: about 25 years to reach low millions of tracks.

## The "multi-dimensional Pythagorean theorem" is Euclidean distance, weighted gene by gene

Rafa's phrase names the right idea. Put two games on a chart with difficulty across and coziness up. The gap in difficulty and the gap in coziness are the two legs of a right triangle, and the straight-line distance between the games is the hypotenuse: the square root of the sum of the two squared gaps. Add a third attribute and the rule still holds, with a third squared gap under the root; with 400 genes there are 400 squared gaps under one root. That is Euclidean distance, and the patent writes it in exactly this form, as the square root of the sum over all n genes of each squared difference ([US 7,003,515 B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en)). The word "Pythagorean" does not appear in the passages the researchers retrieved.

The patent then makes two moves. It drops the square root, because the root never changes which song is closest, and it multiplies each squared gap by a per-gene weight w_i, so the distance between song S and candidate T is the sum over genes i of w_i × (s_i − t_i)², where s_i and t_i are the two songs' scores on gene i. It also allows a scaling function f applied to each score before differencing, and a general form, the sum of w_i × g(|s_i − t_i|), with g(x) = x² as the usual case and x³ as an alternative that punishes large gaps harder. The engine "sorts the results to yield an adjustable number of closest matches"; there is no threshold. Worked, with the researchers' style of example: a seed game scored (difficulty 5, coziness 0, story weight 2) and a candidate scored (4, 1, 2) have gaps of 1, 1 and 0, so the Euclidean distance is the square root of 2, about 1.41, and the patent's squared distance is 2. If the weight on difficulty is 3 and the others 1, the weighted distance is 3 × 1 + 1 × 1 + 1 × 0 = 4, and a candidate that misses on difficulty now sits twice as far away as one that misses equally on coziness.

The weights are where the patent's real ideas sit. For a single seed song, its vector is compared with every song's vector using weights a music team set "through empirical work": hand-tuned, not learned. For a seed group (an artist's songs, an album, or "the songs that a given end user likes"), the patent reduces the group to a center vector μ, the mean of each gene across the seeds, and a deviation vector σ, each gene's standard deviation across the seeds. Each gene is then weighted by the inverse of its variance, 1 / σ_i², so the distance from the group to a candidate t is the sum over genes of (1 / σ_i)² × (μ_i − t_i)². In statistics this is a diagonal Mahalanobis distance: genes on which the seeds agree count heavily, and genes on which they vary count little. Because a gene with zero spread would get infinite weight, and because the patent concedes "some noise in the rated gene values", it adds a floor: the weight becomes w_i × 0.25 / (σ_i² + 0.25), where 0.25 is half a scale point squared, a constant the patent says "may be modified". Worked: three seed songs score 4, 4 and 5 on tempo (variance 0.22 across the three) and 1, 3 and 5 on vocal register (variance 2.67). The floored weights are 0.25 / 0.47 = 0.53 for tempo and 0.25 / 2.92 = 0.086 for vocals, so tempo counts about six times as much; without the floor the ratio would be twelve. A candidate that matches the seeds' mean tempo exactly but sits two points off their mean vocals is at distance 0.086 × 2² = 0.34; one that matches their mean vocals but sits two points off their mean tempo is at 0.53 × 2² = 2.1, six times farther. The station is defined by what the seeds share.

The patent anticipates a taste with more than one center. For a group whose songs fall into clusters, it keeps two or more center-and-deviation pairs, so a match fits one cluster rather than the empty space between them, and it returns "a few from each cluster according to cluster size" ([US 7,003,515 B1, claims 13 and 17](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en), paraphrased in the researchers' notes). It also describes "focus traits", the distinguishing aspects of a song fired by rules (a gene above a threshold, a gene marked as a definer, or a group of genes meeting a test); when a user picks one, the engine raises the weights of the genes behind it. It says the weighting vector "can also be manipulated for each end user" but gives no algorithm for learning it, and it contains no thumbs-up or thumbs-down mechanism. Recommendation from taste alone, with no seed, is contemplated but not specified. The patent, filed 2002-05-16 by Glaser, Westergren, Stearns and Kraft, is listed as expired on Google Patents.

The live system matched the patent's outline. Pandora staff described it to Celma around 2007 as "simple weighted Euclidean distance" over nearest neighbors, with extra weight on important genes such as genre, and artist similarity computed from specific songs rather than an artist's average ([Celma thesis, section 3.4](https://mtg.upf.edu/static/media/PhD_ocelma.pdf)). Under oath in 2014, Westergren called it "a nearest-neighbor calculation" of how geometrically close one song is to another, adjusting in real time with each input and blind to popularity by design ([CRB Web IV, paras 30-31](https://www.crb.gov/rate/14-CRB-0001-WR/statements/Pandora/7_Written_Direct_Testimony_of_Timothy_Westergren_PUBLIC_pdf.pdf)). The arithmetic is a few lines: a weighted sum of squared gaps, sorted, top k. What is worth copying is the treatment of weights: inverse-variance weighting of a seed set, a floor against noisy scores, a separate center for each cluster of taste, and a way for the user to say which traits matter.

## Thumbs came later, and by 2013 the genome was one of more than 50 algorithms

The genome alone was never the whole product for long. By the 2011 S-1, algorithms combined the genes with individual and collective listener feedback, and listeners had given more than three billion thumbs in 2010 ([S-1, 2011](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001230276/000119312511032963/ds1.htm)). Bieschke's 2013 slides put the count at more than 30 billion thumbs and the system at more than 50 algorithms, with the advice to combine techniques that "seek orthogonality" and the note that simple item-to-item recommenders win at scale ([Bieschke, MLconf slides, 2013-11](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/eric-bieschke-slides/28431664)). A Pandora scientist described the genome in 2014 as "the basis" of the content-based side of a dynamic ensemble ([MLconf 2014 abstract](https://mlconf.com/sessions/pandora-is-best-known-for-the-music-genome-project/)). Later counts of about 70 or more than 75 algorithms rest on secondary sources, one with a breakdown that does not sum to its own total.

The sworn 2014 account is the clearest. Westergren described three parts: the genome's nearest-neighbor match; a "collective intelligence" layer that uses thumbs, thumbs-down and skips to correct pairs that "do not appeal to the same audience", for example dropping song B from stations seeded with song A when listeners respond badly; and collaborative filtering for listeners who thumb, recommending what similar listeners liked ([CRB Web IV, paras 29, 33-34](https://www.crb.gov/rate/14-CRB-0001-WR/statements/Pandora/7_Written_Direct_Testimony_of_Timothy_Westergren_PUBLIC_pdf.pdf)). He credited the genome with what the other two cannot do: recommend new songs and serve new listeners "without any data", and give obscure music a level field. Changes were tested on small groups against 140 metrics (paras 32, 35). The feedback layer is a per-pair veto list on top of the genome, not a re-weighting of genes, which is the cheapest and lowest-risk way to fold behavior into an attribute match.

Pandora also changed what it optimized. Bieschke's slides track a move from the share of thumbs that were up (skewed toward extremes) to total listening hours (skewed toward heavy listeners) to the rate at which listeners return, with relevance, accuracy, diversity, novelty and surprise as deeper measures ([Bieschke slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/eric-bieschke-slides/28431664); [third-party notes of the talk](https://gist.github.com/mjamesruggiero/7553429)). Thumbprint Radio, a station built from a listener's own thumbs-up history, launched 2015-12-14 ([Business Wire, 2015-12-14](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20151214005277/en/3666533/Pandora%C2%A0Unleashes-Thumbprint-Radio%C2%A0%E2%80%93-Uniquely-Personal-Station-Inspired), read through search results). Modes, launched 2019-03-19, let a listener switch a station between the default, Deep Cuts, Discovery, Crowd Faves, Artist Only and Newly Released, which was the product answer to stations that all sound the same ([Engadget, 2019-03-19](https://www.engadget.com/2019-03-19-pandora-modes-stations-feature.html)). Pandora-authored research trained audio models to predict expert genes ([Prockup et al., ISMIR 2015](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2015/paper/000276.pdf)), and a later community post describes predicting tags for songs no human has analyzed ([Pandora Community, read through a summary](https://community.pandora.com/t5/Community-Blog/The-Future-of-the-Music-Genome-Project-Unlocking-the-Long-Tail/ba-p/176424)), even though the corporate page read 2026-10-29 still says the project uses no machine listening. The explanation survives as a "Why this song?" item that lists shared traits while the traits themselves stay proprietary ([Pandora Community thread](https://community.pandora.com/t5/Desktop/How-can-I-view-a-song-s-genome-traits/td-p/70780)). Nothing public shows what share of today's picks come from the genome, or how SiriusXM, which completed its acquisition on 2019-02-01, uses it ([SiriusXM, 2019-02-01](https://investor.siriusxm.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1084/siriusxm-completes-acquisition-of-pandora)).

Read together, Pandora's own account is that attribute distance is the cold-start and long-tail engine and that behavior corrects it. Nobody at Pandora claims the genome alone is enough. That matters for Verso in a specific way: Verso has no crowd yet, decision 24 keeps neighbor predictions off until the harness says otherwise, and the engine is in the regime where Pandora found the genome most useful. Verso also has a better outcome than a thumb: whether a recommended item is later ranked, and in which tier.

## Where the genome fell short: cost, unmeasured noise, and audiences the attributes miss

Cost and speed come first. In 2017 Pandora's analyzed catalog was under 2 million songs against more than 30 million advertised by on-demand services, and the human-rating model was called unscalable by industry norms ([The Outline, 2017-01-11](https://theoutline.com/post/877/the-music-genome-project-isn-t-done)). The music chapter of the Recommender Systems Handbook summarizes expert annotation as accurate but "prohibitively costly to scale" ([Schedl et al., Recommender Systems Handbook ch. 13, 2015](https://www.ifs.tuwien.ac.at/~knees/teaching/rsss2019/RecSysHandbook2015_ch13_musicrec.pdf)). Pandora could live with a small catalog because it ran a radio product, where the listener does not choose the song; an app where people search for a specific game or film cannot.

Noise is second, and it is unmeasured. No inter-analyst agreement has been published in 25 years, only a share of songs is scored twice, and the patent's own variance floor is an admission that single scores are noisy. Among close candidates, a nearest-neighbor ranking is partly decided by that noise.

Third, attributes do not capture audiences. Westergren presented popularity blindness as a virtue and, in the same testimony, said the genome can pair songs that do not share an audience, which is why the thumbs layer exists ([CRB Web IV, paras 6, 31, 33](https://www.crb.gov/rate/14-CRB-0001-WR/statements/Pandora/7_Written_Direct_Testimony_of_Timothy_Westergren_PUBLIC_pdf.pdf)). The genome scores lyric tone (angry, sad, romantic, explicit) but not lyric meaning, and a critic argued that any complete taxonomy of music is impossible and that analysts' own culture shapes which features they notice ([Mandl, The Brooklyn Rail, 2014-11](https://brooklynrail.org/2014/11/music/feature-creeps/), opinion). Complaints that stations grow repetitive after many thumbs-up are anecdotes from users and one trade columnist ([Radio Ink, 2016-12-18](https://radioink.com/2016/12/18/death-by-repetition/)); no systematic study was found. Some of Pandora's playlist rules came from law rather than taste: US non-interactive webcasters may play no more than three songs from one album and four by one artist in any three hours ([17 USC 114, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/114)). That law does not reach Verso, but the idea of a cap per album becomes a cap per franchise below.

The academic record has a hole exactly where Rafa's question sits. No published study puts a Pandora-style expert genome head to head against collaborative filtering for recommendation. The nearest evidence points one way. In Celma's blind test, 288 people rated 5,573 thirty-second clips from three recommenders; on songs they did not know, collaborative filtering from Last.fm averaged 3.03 on a 5-point scale, a hybrid of AllMusic experts' related artists re-ranked by audio similarity 2.77, and pure audio similarity 2.57, while on known songs the three did not differ significantly ([Celma thesis, ch. 7 and Table 7.1](https://mtg.upf.edu/static/media/PhD_ocelma.pdf)). On 1.5 million ratings of 1,000 jazz songs, rating-based similarity found same-artist matches almost twice as well as signal-based content similarity ([Slaney and White, ISMIR 2007](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2007/paper/000479.pdf)). The handbook chapter states the general pattern: audio-content methods "are usually outperformed by collaborative filtering", and content is still preferred for cold start and against popularity bias. On the other side, Pandora's own authors found that 48 expert genes predict a song's genre better than audio features (mean AUC 0.931 against 0.905, where AUC is the chance a classifier ranks a true member above a non-member), though audio won for rap, reggae and disco; audio models recovered rhythmic genes well (swing AUC 0.907) and perceptual ones poorly (a mean R² of 0.266 across continuous timbre genes, with vocal grittiness "not modeled very well at all") ([Prockup et al., ISMIR 2015, Tables 6, 8 and 9](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2015/paper/000276.pdf)). The fair summary: collaborative filtering beats content at predicting what people like once there is data; experts beat machines at describing the items; Pandora's architecture, genome first and behavior on top, fits both.

The automated contrast carries a warning of its own. The Echo Nest, an MIT Media Lab spin-out, derived about a dozen 0-to-1 audio descriptors (danceability, energy, valence, acousticness) for about 30 million songs by signal processing and web text mining, and Spotify bought it in March 2014 for about €50 million ([Wikipedia: The Echo Nest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Echo_Nest); [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/spotify-acquired-echo-nest-just-e50m/)). On 2024-11-27 Spotify closed the audio-features, audio-analysis and recommendations endpoints to new apps, citing a "more secure platform" ([Spotify for Developers, 2024-11-27](https://developer.spotify.com/blog/2024-11-27-changes-to-the-web-api); [TechCrunch, 2024-11-27](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/27/spotify-cuts-developer-access-to-several-of-its-recommendation-features)). Any product built on those scores lost them overnight. A genome is worth building only if Verso owns the scores, and owning them depends on the terms under which the inputs were read, which is the subject of a later section.

## No one licenses a graded genome for games or films

The film world has two real genomes, and neither is available. Netflix paid trained taggers to watch titles and score them from a manual of 24 or 36 pages (reports differ) on content such as goriness, romance and how conclusive the plot is; a script then combined the tags into 76,897 "altgenres", the count Alexis Madrigal scraped for The Atlantic in January 2014 ([Waterloo course page quoting the article](https://poetry-contingency.uwaterloo.ca/netflix-algorithms-humans/); [Pixel Envy, 2014-01-02](https://pxlnv.com/linklog/how-netflix-reverse-engineered-hollywood/)). The scale is reported as one to five, with zero for absence in one example ([Irish Times, 2014-07-10](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/film/trust-busters-and-monkey-boxing-why-netflix-watches-everything-you-watch-1.1860471)); the Atlantic text itself could not be read. In January 2024 Netflix said it would likely drop the percent match in favor of more than 3,000 descriptive tags, with about 30 full-time staff on tags ([MobileSyrup, 2024-01-17](https://mobilesyrup.com/2024/01/17/netflix-content-match-percentages-tags-report/)). None of it has a public feed. The MovieLens Tag Genome is the other: a dense matrix of 0-to-1 relevance scores for about 1,100 tags on 9,734 films (1,084 tags in the 2021 release), predicted by a regression over users' tags, ratings and review text ([GroupLens Tag Genome 2021](https://grouplens.org/datasets/movielens/tag-genome-2021/)). Its accuracy sets a useful ceiling. People who rated the same film-tag pair more than once disagreed by a mean standard deviation of 0.74 points on a 5-point scale (0.71 for objective tags such as "courtroom", 0.87 for subjective ones such as "bad acting"), and the model's mean absolute error was 0.83 points against 1.45 for predicting the average, with a 2021 neural model at 0.81 ([Kotkov, Maslov and Neovius, SIGIR 2021](https://research.abo.fi/ws/files/50027691/3404835.3463019.pdf)). It is research data: GroupLens does not normally permit redistribution, and the MovieLens README the researchers could read bars commercial use without permission ([GroupLens MovieLens](https://grouplens.org/datasets/movielens/); [mirror of the ml-100k README](https://git.camilstaps.nl/university/NWI-IBI008-Data-Mining/tree/Assignment%205/data/MovieLens_README.txt)).

The remaining film vocabularies are binary or closed. Jinni's "Entertainment Genome", a taxonomy built by film professionals and applied to titles by language processing of metadata and reviews ([Wikipedia, Movie Genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movie_Genome)), was reported at about 2,200 genes ([Nocamels, 2010](https://nocamels.com/2010/10/jinni-helps-your-computer-understand-mood-and-sentiment-so-you-can-find-just-the-movie-youre-looking-for/), read through a search summary) and went business-to-business when its consumer site closed in June 2015. Letterboxd's themes and nanogenres come from Nanocrowd, which clusters the emotional language of reviews, and date from March 2022 ([Nanocrowd, 2022-03-01](https://nanocrowd.com/nanocrowd-letterboxd/)); Letterboxd's API is by request only and is not granted for recommendation projects ([Letterboxd API page](https://letterboxd.com/api-beta/), read through a search summary). TMDB's keywords are a free-form crowd vocabulary: the public daily export of 2026-10-29 held 98,126 keyword ids, mixing people, places, plot devices and themes with no grades ([TMDB export](http://files.tmdb.org/p/exports/keyword_ids_10_29_2026.json.gz), observed 2026-10-29).

For games the free vocabularies are all binary. IGDB carries 23 genres, 22 themes (Horror, Comedy, Open World, Survival, Stealth and others), 7 player perspectives, 6 game modes, free-form keywords and per-platform multiplayer records with co-op and split-screen flags and maximums; the taxonomy endpoints return names only, with no strength per game ([IGDB API docs](https://api-docs.igdb.com/); [IGDB contribution guidelines, Themes](https://github.com/twitchtv/igdb-contribution-guidelines/wiki/Themes)). Verso mirrors only the genres today, though the rest come from the same free API under the same terms. Steam has about 429 player-applied tags (observed 2026-10-29), each a binary vote with a count; only a game's top 20 are exposed, and Valve finds similar games by overlap of those 20, with rare tags weighing more than common ones such as "Action" ([Steamworks, Steam Tags](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/tags)). That is very nearly Verso's layer 1: idf-weighted overlap of binary features. The tags come from undocumented store endpoints, and Steam's written API terms of July 2010 license data for end users' personal use ([Steam Web API Terms](https://steamcommunity.com/dev/apiterms)). Quantic Foundry's Gamer Motivation Model has 12 motivations in six pairs (Destruction and Excitement, Competition and Community, Challenge and Strategy, Completion and Power, Fantasy and Story, Design and Discovery), built by factor analysis of more than 1.25 million self-selected survey takers, with test-retest r = 0.73; it describes players, not games, and its per-game audience profiles are sold as custom reports ([Quantic Foundry reference PDF](https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Gamer-Motivation-Model-Reference.pdf)). GameRefinery, now under Liftoff, tracks 260 analyst features on mobile games for paying studios ([GameRefinery, 2020-10-29](https://gamerefinery.com/product-update-october-2020-automated-tags)).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The conclusion is plain. No licensable source offers graded attribute vectors for games or films. Netflix and the Tag Genome were built from paid experts or from large corpora of user tags and reviews that Verso does not have. If Verso wants a genome, it has to score one, and the only ways to do that at catalog scale in 2026 are its own users or a language model.

## Since September, a model can score every game for under $50; validation is the real cost

On 2026-09-24 the earlier research concluded that "hand-built genomes are out of reach for a one-person team" and that Verso's content side should come from IGDB and TMDB metadata ([comparable-products notes, "Content-based precedents"](recommendation-engine-notes/comparable-products.md)). That was a statement about analyst hours, and it still holds for analysts: at Pandora's 20 to 30 minutes an item, the 69,183 games in Verso's IGDB mirror would take 23,000 to 34,600 hours (the researchers' arithmetic). What has changed is the price of a machine scorer, and the evidence on how far to trust one.

On categorical labels with a ground truth, models have matched or beaten crowd workers since 2023. On 6,183 tweets and news articles, zero-shot ChatGPT beat crowd workers by about 25 accuracy points on average and agreed with itself 97% of the time at temperature 0.2, for under $0.003 an annotation ([Gilardi, Alizadeh and Kubli, PNAS 2023, arXiv 2303.15056](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15056)). On graded judgments the picture depends on the task. A 2026 study of six model judges against 12 human raters found the highest pooled agreement on a 0-to-5 scale, an intraclass correlation (ICC, where 1 is perfect agreement on the same scale) of 0.853, against 0.805 on 0 to 10; but per task the 0-to-5 ICC ran from 0.905 down to 0.517, and per model from 0.816 (GPT-4o) down to 0.596 ([Li et al., arXiv 2601.03444, 2026-01-06](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03444v1)). Models rate emotional intensity lower than people do ([Bojic et al., arXiv 2501.02532, 2025-01](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.02532)), pull the scores of later attributes in one output toward earlier ones and favor round numbers ([Stureborg, Alikaniotis and Suhara, arXiv 2405.01724, 2024-05](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01724)), and shift by up to 76 accuracy points under formatting-only prompt changes ([Sclar et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324v2)). On a nuanced "vibe" attribute of short videos, Gemini 2.5 Pro reached F1 81.3 against an expert golden set where crowd raters reached 63.2 ([Long, Wang et al., RecSys 2025, arXiv 2510.06657](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06657v1)). One caution bears directly on explanations: a GPT-5 judge of movie relevance matched human rankings of whole systems closely (Kendall's tau 0.87) while agreeing with humans on individual item pairs only 55% to 57% of the time ([Penha et al., Spotify, arXiv 2511.23312, 2025-11](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.23312v1)). A genome can be good enough to rank lists and still be wrong about a single game named in a reason.

Knowledge falls with popularity and with model size. Asked to recall the 3,883 films of MovieLens-1M, GPT-4o recovered 81% and GPT-4o mini 8%; on the least popular third, 64% and 2% ([Di Palma et al., SIGIR 2025, arXiv 2505.10212](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10212v1)). No equivalent benchmark exists for games, whose long tail is likely worse. Retrieval largely repairs the long tail in question answering, and retrieving only below a popularity threshold cut cost by half ([Mallen et al., ACL 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)). For a small model like Haiku, the catalog text (an IGDB summary, a TMDB overview) is the evidence, not a supplement. Adding metadata to a movie judge's prompt raised its agreement with people slightly, while adding the average rating lowered it ([Penha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.23312v1)), so reception fields belong outside the scoring prompt: the genome should describe the item, not its reviews. The abstention design that follows from these sources is the researchers' inference: skip items below a popularity floor or with too little text, ask the model first for two or three checkable facts (developer, year, platform) and abstain on a mismatch with the catalog record, offer "cannot tell" on every attribute, and flag items where two runs disagree by more than a point.

The measured value of model-written attributes in recommenders is real but indirect. LLMRec had gpt-3.5-turbo write item attributes and user profiles for Netflix and MovieLens data and gained 8.5% in recall at 20, for about $21 of model calls in total ([Wei et al., WSDM 2024, arXiv 2311.00423](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.00423v6)). Closest to Verso, a small fine-tuned model scored items on 12 predefined attributes per domain on a 1-to-10 scale (for games: difficulty, replay value, graphics quality, customization), and concatenating them with sequence-model embeddings raised mean reciprocal rank by 14.4% to 19.7% on Amazon's video-game reviews and 6.6% to 14.1% on movies, with no check on the scores' reliability ([Jia et al., arXiv 2504.11658, 2025-04](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11658v1)). YouTube put one model-labeled attribute into retrieval and measured +0.21% satisfied consumption online ([Long, Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06657v1)). Free-form model tags do worse than structured descriptors ([AgenticTagger, arXiv 2602.05945, 2026-02](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.05945), from a review summary), and every production pipeline found (Amazon, Spotify, YouTube) keeps people in a filter or golden-set role ([Spotify Engineering, 2024-10-21](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2024/10/how-we-generated-millions-of-content-annotations/)). Every one of those gains came from feeding attributes to a learned model trained on plenty of interaction data. None measures Verso's setting: a SQL scorer over one person's list, a reason line, and a cold start. Against text embeddings, no head-to-head under identical conditions exists; language-based profiles reach parity with embedding baselines while staying readable and editable ([Ramos et al., ACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05810v2)), and scored attributes add to embeddings rather than replace them (Jia). The attributes' advantage is that a person's weights over a handful of grouped attributes can be fit from a list of 50 items, and that they produce sentences such as "slower and cozier than your Loved", where an embedding needs a second step to say anything.

What a prompt can copy from Pandora is its rubric: a fixed vocabulary with written definitions; integer scores from 0 to 5 with a described meaning for at least 0, 1, 3 and 5; two or three anchor titles at each end, as Pandora's trainees calibrated on shared songs; an explicit "cannot tell"; a human golden set; and about 10% of items scored twice. An evaluator given a rubric with a description per score and a reference answer reached Pearson 0.897 with human graders across 45 rubrics ([Kim et al., Prometheus, ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08491)), and a small calibration network on top of a model's answer probabilities halved the error against individual human judges ([Hashemi et al., LLM-Rubric, ACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.745)). Scoring attribute groups in separate calls, or shuffling their order between two runs, counters the anchoring effect. Every score row should carry the rubric version, the model id and a prompt hash, so a rubric change re-runs in one batch and compares against the last. What a prompt cannot copy is the listening: Pandora's analysts heard the whole song, and a model reads a paragraph about the game.

Cost is now small. At the prices read on 2026-10-29, Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, Sonnet 5.5 $2 and $10, Opus 5.5 $4 and $20, and the Batch API halves each ([Claude pricing](https://claude.com/pricing); [Batch processing docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/batch-processing)). The researchers' totals, in US dollars at standard and batch prices, for 800 input and 300 output tokens an item:

| Model | 69,183 games | 25,000 films | 12,000 series | 10,000 popular games |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haiku 5.5 | 15.91 / 7.96 | 5.75 / 2.88 | 2.76 / 1.38 | 2.30 / 1.15 |
| Sonnet 5.5 | 318 / 159 | 115 / 57.50 | 55.20 / 27.60 | 46 / 23 |
| Opus 5.5 | 636 / 318 | 230 / 115 | 110.40 / 55.20 | 92 / 46 |

A realistic rubric of 4,000 cached tokens plus 600 tokens of grounding barely moves the Haiku total ($17.30 standard for all games) when the cache hits, and raises it to about $42 standard or $21 batch if every cache read misses, which batches allow. Asking for a one-sentence rationale per attribute (1,500 output tokens) raises Haiku to about $59 for the games, because output tokens dominate the bill. Scoring twice doubles any line. These are the researchers' calculations on assumed token counts; real IGDB summaries were not measured. The dominant cost is no longer the model. It is the human golden set, 100 to 200 items per medium scored by hand to validate each attribute, and the engineering to keep the rubric versioned.

## The data terms decide what may be scored at all

The model costs a few dollars; the inputs may cost a written agreement. TMDB's API terms, last updated 2023-10-20 and read 2026-10-29, list as prohibited in section 1.C any use of the APIs or TMDB content in connection with a machine-learning or AI-based application, "including for training"; making "derivatives" of the APIs or the content; and caching anything for longer than six months ([TMDB API Terms of Use](https://www.themoviedb.org/api-terms-of-use)). Section 2.A says the free API permits no commercial use without a separate written agreement, and names among commercial uses the training or validating of an AI system and use in LLM-based query-response systems. On the researchers' reading, which is not legal advice, sending TMDB overviews or keywords to Claude to produce scores is use "in connection with" an AI application, the stored scores are plausibly derivatives of TMDB content, and Verso's planned paid tier is commercial use in any case, a point [film-tv-data-sources.md](film-tv-data-sources.md) already made on 2026-09-16. The six-month cache cap and deletion on termination would also reach the TMDB rows the scores are keyed on. A film genome grounded on TMDB text therefore waits on a written agreement. The alternatives are grounding text from another source with compatible terms, which was not researched, or the model's own knowledge with no TMDB text in the prompt, which brings back the long-tail problem. One further reading, also an inference: the same clause 1.C plausibly reaches the `suggest` Edge Function already built, which sends film candidates' titles to a model, so the question to TMDB should cover both.

IGDB's own documentation is friendlier. It allows commercial use through a free partnership with user-facing attribution, prefers that data be stored and served locally, lets partners keep data after a partnership ends, and says nothing about AI ([IGDB API docs](https://api-docs.igdb.com/), read 2026-10-29). The binding contract is the Twitch Developer Services Agreement, last modified 2024-12-04, which IGDB names as its terms ([Twitch Developer Services Agreement](https://legal.twitch.com/en/legal/developer-agreement/)). Section VI.A defines "Twitch Data" as data from the API or other program materials, "including any insights derived from that data". Section VI.C bars selling, licensing or monetizing Twitch Data and derived insights, or making them available to "any affiliates or third parties", without prior written permission. Schedule 1 limits caching to 24 hours unless authorized and prohibits selling or distributing metadata, though whether Schedule 1 reaches IGDB, which is documented apart from the Twitch APIs it names, is unclear. The text never mentions artificial intelligence, machine learning or IGDB. Two questions follow: whether sending IGDB summaries to Anthropic makes Twitch Data available to a third party, and whether attribute scores are derived insights. Showing scores to Verso's own users fits VI.B's first listed purpose, improving the end-user experience. Selling aggregate attribute data to studios, which decision 21 allows for de-identified behavior data, would not without permission.

Steam tags are a legal question rather than a technical one: the endpoints that expose them are undocumented, the written Web API terms of July 2010 license data for end users' personal use, and the Steam Subscriber Agreement grants a personal, non-commercial license unless commercial use is expressly allowed ([Steam Web API Terms](https://steamcommunity.com/dev/apiterms); [Steam Subscriber Agreement](https://store.steampowered.com/agreement)). No clause names tags. The MovieLens Tag Genome would make a tempting external check on a model-built film genome, but its commercial use needs GroupLens's permission too. Neither TMDB nor IGDB distinguishes a model provider that only runs inference from one that trains, and Anthropic's own data-use terms for API inputs were not checked in this research.

The order of work follows. The data terms are the first gate, not the last: one email to partner@igdb.com and one to TMDB settle more of this project's risk than any amount of math, and their answers decide which media can have a genome at all.

## Graded attributes need their own distance, beside the tags Verso already scores

Pandora's Euclidean distance and Verso's cosine are not rivals; each is right for one kind of data. The textbook statement is that nearest-neighbor content recommenders use Euclidean distance for structured attributes and cosine for tag or text vectors, because in Euclidean distance a feature small in both items counts the same as one large in both, while cosine rewards only what the two share ([Pazzani and Billsus, The Adaptive Web, 2007](https://cs.fit.edu/~pkc/apweb/related/pazzani07aw.pdf)). Two of the researchers' worked examples show where each one breaks.

Graded attributes first. Four attributes on Pandora's 0-to-5 scale: difficulty, combat intensity, coziness, story weight. The seed is a brutal soulslike, S = (5, 5, 0, 2). Candidate X is another demanding action game, (4, 4, 1, 2); Y has the seed's proportions at half strength, (2.5, 2.5, 0, 1); Z is a cozy farming game, (0, 0, 5, 2).

| Candidate | Euclidean distance | Manhattan (L1) distance | Cosine similarity | Cosine after centering each attribute on the catalog mean of 2.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X, demanding action | 1.73 (closest) | 3.0 (closest) | 0.984 | 0.997 (highest) |
| Y, half-strength copy | 3.67 | 6.0 | 1.000 (highest) | 0.551 |
| Z, cozy farming | 8.66 | 15.0 | 0.101 | −0.974 |

Raw cosine calls Y a perfect match because it measures only direction. On an intensity scale, Y is a game half as hard and half as violent, which is exactly what a soulslike fan does not want. Euclidean and Manhattan distance pick X, and so does cosine once each attribute is centered on the catalog mean, the Pearson-style "centered cosine". Manhattan (L1) distance is the sum of absolute gaps, the distance walked along city blocks rather than the hypotenuse. The rule for graded attributes: weighted Euclidean or L1, or cosine only on centered attributes, never raw cosine.

Binary tags second. Twelve binary tags; a seed with six. P is thinly catalogued, with two tags, both the seed's. R is richly catalogued, with ten tags, five of them the seed's and five not. Euclidean distance prefers P (2.00 against 2.45); cosine (0.577 against 0.645) and Jaccard overlap (0.333 against 0.455) prefer R. R shares five of the seed's six traits and is the better match. Euclidean distance on binary vectors counts every tag the candidate carries and the seed lacks as a mismatch, so it rewards items the catalog knows little about. Layer 1's choice of cosine on idf-weighted binary features is correct and should stay.

Scale and redundancy decide whether a weighted Euclidean distance means anything. It sums squared gaps in raw units, so a 10-point Metacritic gap adds 100 to the squared distance while a full swing on coziness adds 25. Every genome attribute should sit on one anchored 0-to-5 scale, or be standardized to z-scores over the catalog, and runtime, year and community score should stay out of the distance as filters, the prior or terms of their own. Near-duplicate attributes count twice: with difficulty, punishing death and combat intensity as three attributes plus coziness, a game one point softer on all three hard attributes sits at distance 1.73 from the seed, and a game one point cozier sits at 1.00, so "hardness" counts √3 times as much as coziness only because it was written three times (the researchers' example). Mahalanobis distance, d² = Δᵀ Σ⁻¹ Δ with Δ the vector of gaps and Σ the attributes' covariance, treats moves that usually happen together as one unit: with two standardized attributes correlated at 0.9, a step of (1, 1) along the correlation is a Mahalanobis distance d of 1.03, a step of (1, −1) against it 4.47 and a step of (1, 0) 2.29, where plain Euclidean distance gives 1.41 to both diagonal steps. Full whitening on 20 to 60 attributes estimated from the catalog magnifies rarely varying, noisy attributes, so the safer course is to design the list without near-duplicates and, if needed, use a shrunk covariance or Pandora's own diagonal floor.

The number of attributes is not the risk it sounds like. Distances concentrate as dimensions grow, so the nearest point's distance approaches the farthest's, an effect seen at as few as 10 to 15 dimensions on unstructured data ([Beyer et al., ICDT 1999](https://members.loria.fr/MOBerger/Enseignement/AVR/Exposes/beyer.pdf)), and it worsens faster for Euclidean than for Manhattan distance ([Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim, ICDT 2001](https://www.haralick.org/ML/surprise_in_high_dimensions.pdf)). In the researchers' own simulation over 3,000 items, 60 independent attributes put the nearest item at 73% of the median distance (L1: 70%), a thin but usable gap. Real attributes cluster: with 60 attributes driven by 6 underlying factors plus 30% attribute-specific noise, the nearest sits at 54% of the median; holding 6 factors and varying the noise, it sits at 22% with no noise, 38% at 10% noise and 61% at 50%. Scoring noise, not the attribute count, decides contrast. Averaging two or three independent scorings of each attribute buys more contrast than cutting attributes.

The design consequence is that the tags and the genome answer different questions. The tags say what kind of thing an item is; the genome says how much of each quality it has. They should be two terms, each with its own measure (cosine on idf tags, weighted Euclidean or L1 on standardized genes), blended by weights the harness fits, rather than one concatenated vector on which one measure is wrong for half the coordinates.

## A ranked list is many seeds: score against loved items, never their average

A Pandora station starts from one seed, so "closest to the seed" is well defined. A person's ranked list is many seeds, and taste is often in more than one place. Any single summary of it, whether a centroid of loved items, a Rocchio query (the relevance-feedback rule that moves a query toward liked items and away from disliked ones) or the weighted linear profile layer 1 builds today, can land between the clusters or cancel them. The researchers' worked example makes it concrete. Four attributes from 0 to 5: difficulty, combat intensity, coziness, exploration. A user loved three cozy games, (1, 0, 5, 3), (0, 0, 5, 2) and (1, 0, 5, 3), and three soulslikes, (5, 5, 0, 4), (5, 5, 0, 5) and (5, 5, 0, 2), all scored 9; they disliked two middling licensed action games, (3, 3, 2, 3) and (2, 3, 1, 2), scored 3; their mean is 7.5. The centroid of the loved items is (2.83, 2.5, 2.5, 3.17). Four candidates, scored five ways (for distances lower is better, for the rest higher):

| Candidate | Distance to the loved centroid | Linear profile, as layer 1 builds it (cosine) | Distance to the nearest loved item | Distance to the nearer of two prototypes | Rocchio, β = 1, γ = 0.5 (cosine) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1, middling action game (3, 3, 2, 3) | 0.75 (best) | −0.19 | 3.61 | 3.53 | 0.13 |
| C2, cozy game (1, 0, 5, 2) | 4.15 | 0.56 | 1.00 (best, tied) | 0.75 | 0.29 |
| C3, soulslike (5, 5, 0, 3) | 4.15 | −0.44 (worst) | 1.00 (best, tied) | 0.67 (best) | −0.17 (worst) |
| C4, a "cozy soulslike" hybrid (4, 3, 3, 4) | 1.60 | 0.58 (best) | 3.74 | 3.76 | 0.78 (best) |

The centroid recommends exactly the kind of game the user disliked. The linear profile, built as layer 1 builds its profile (each item weighted by its score minus the user's mean, times its attributes centered on the catalog), comes out as (3.0, −4.5, 9.0, 6.0): the two loved clusters point in opposite directions on difficulty, combat and coziness and largely cancel, so the profile is decided by whatever the two disliked games happen to separate, here low coziness, and it scores the soulslike below the disliked game. Rocchio does the same, and both rank first a hybrid that matches neither cluster. Distance to the nearest loved item and to the nearer of two prototypes put both true matches on top and the disliked kind near the bottom. Layer 1 escapes the worst of this today only because its features are binary: `genre:simulator` and `genre:souls-like` are separate coordinates, not two ends of one axis. A graded genome makes cancellation the default, because every graded axis has two ends.

The field reached the same conclusion with learned embeddings. Pinterest's PinnerSage found that a single user embedding "falls short", showing a user interested in painting, shoes and science fiction whose merged vector lands nearest a pin about breakfast; it clusters each user's actions with Ward hierarchical clustering (3 to 5 clusters for a light user, 75 to 100 for a heavy one), represents each cluster by a medoid, a real pin, rather than a centroid, and reports offline and online gains over single-embedding methods ([Pal et al., KDD 2020, arXiv 2007.03634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03634)). Alibaba's MIND gives each user several interest vectors in production ([Li et al., CIKM 2019, arXiv 1904.08030](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08030)). Rocchio "often fails" on multimodal classes and has no theoretical guarantee ([Wikipedia, Rocchio algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocchio_algorithm); [Pazzani and Billsus 2007](https://cs.fit.edu/~pkc/apweb/related/pazzani07aw.pdf)). Pandora's patent already did the multi-center version with its vector pairs per cluster, twenty years earlier.

For Verso, the researchers' proposal is to score each candidate by its similarity to the user's own top-tier items: the maximum similarity to any loved item, or the mean of the top two or three, weighted by how high each loved item sits. This is the content form of item-to-item neighbors, and it writes the most legible reason ("closest to Hollow Knight, which you put in Loved"). Once a user has dozens of loved items, prototypes are cheaper: cluster the loved tier (Ward or k-medoids, with k chosen by a gap in merge distance, typically 1 to 4 for a list of 50), represent each cluster by its medoid game, and share the three picks across prototypes in proportion to cluster size, as both Pandora's patent and PinnerSage do. Disliked and dropped items should push away only locally, as a penalty for being close to one of them, s(c) − γ × max over disliked items j of sim(c, j), with γ starting near 0.3 to 0.5 for the harness to tune, rather than as a subtracted centroid, which in the example above flips the sign of a whole loved cluster. The single linear profile stays as one term, not the whole model.

One seed still has a place. "More like this" from an item page is Pandora's original station: the item is the query, the person's learned weights shape the metric, and the distance rules of the previous section apply unchanged. When a person picks several seeds, Pandora's inverse-variance weights are the natural default, and its focus traits become a chip on the screen ("more like this, for its atmosphere") that raises the weights of the attributes behind it.

## Each person's weights can be learned from the list, shrunk toward everyone's

Pandora's weights were set by a music team and nudged by a user's focus choices. Verso can do better, because a ranked list is a far richer signal than a thumb, but it must first get past an objection already in the design. The engine doc sets aside a per-user Bradley-Terry model on the comparison log because binary insertion never asks a question the current order would contradict, so "the top item never loses and the fit has no unique answer" ([docs/design/recommendation-engine.md](../design/recommendation-engine.md#alternatives-considered-and-set-aside-for-now)). That objection is about one free strength per item. With n strengths, any order is reproducible exactly, every agreeing comparison is perfectly separated, and the likelihood keeps rising as the strengths spread apart.

Attribute weights are a different model, and the objection mostly does not carry over (the researchers' analysis). Each item's score becomes s_i = w · x_i, where x_i is the item's vector of d standardized attributes and w is the person's d weights, shared by every item. For the fit to diverge, some w would have to reproduce every implied pair, which means the person's whole order would have to be a linear function of the attributes. With n ≤ d + 1 items any order is reproducible, so a short list diverges exactly as the doc says. Above that, real taste is not exactly linear in a few attributes, the order cannot be reproduced, and the maximum is finite; any Gaussian prior makes the objective strictly convex and the answer unique at every list length. The same warning applies to sparse binary features: a `developer:` tag carried by one ranked item behaves like a per-item strength, and its weight runs to infinity without a prior, which is why layer 1's rule that a feature must appear on two or more of the user's items is in the right spirit. The weights add no new information about items already ranked; their value is generalization to items not yet ranked, and reasons ("you weight difficulty high and story low").

The model has precedents in three literatures. Ranking SVM learned a linear ranking function from click preference pairs by classifying ordered pairs ([Joachims, KDD 2002, summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2015/08/27/optimizing-search-engines-using-clickthrough-data/)); RankNet put a logistic link on score differences ([Burges et al., ICML 2005](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/learning-to-rank-using-gradient-descent)); and economists and marketers model a full ranking as a sequence of first choices among the items not yet chosen, with utilities linear in attributes, the rank-ordered or exploded logit that is Plackett-Luce with covariates ([Stata manual, rologit](https://www.stata.com/manuals15/rrologit.pdf); [Allison and Christakis](https://humannaturelab.net/sites/default/files/pdf/paper/017-Logit-Models-for-Sets-of-Ranked-Items.pdf)). Conjoint analysis shows the shrinkage works: hierarchical Bayes recovers each respondent's part-worths even with fewer profiles per person than attributes ([Lenk, DeSarbo, Green and Young, Marketing Science 1996](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.15.2.173)), and Sawtooth Software's guidance is about ten or more choice tasks per person for robust individual predictions ([Sawtooth, CBC/HB](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/advanced-analytical-tools/cbc-hierarchical-bayes)).

Concretely, the likelihood is either Plackett-Luce over the whole list, P(order) = the product over positions i of exp(s_(i)) divided by the sum of exp(s_(j)) over the items j at or below position i, which is the proper probability of one full ranking; or a logistic model on every implied pair, P(a above b) = σ(w · (x_a − x_b)) with σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)), which is RankNet with a linear scorer and easy in SQL or a short script. The pairs must be re-weighted so the whole list counts as about n observations rather than n(n − 1)/2: each item appears in n − 1 pairs, so unweighted pairs overstate the evidence about (n − 1)/2-fold, produce overconfident weights and swamp the prior. The comparison log alone (about 200 logged comparisons for a 50-item list against 1,225 implied pairs) throws away what the order already holds. The prior is w ~ Normal(m, τ² I): m is the population's weights (equal weights after standardization, or weights an expert sets, until enough users exist, then the mean of the fitted users, which is empirical Bayes), and τ is the spread between people.

How many ranked items does it take? The researchers simulated it. A person's weights were the population mean plus Normal(0, 0.5²) noise per attribute; the person ranked n items by a utility of which the attributes explained 40% (the rest is taste the attributes do not capture); the model was fit by Newton's method on implied pairs weighted to n; and it was scored on 200 new items by held-out pairwise accuracy, where 0.5 is chance and the oracle, using the true weights, is the ceiling the attributes allow. There were 60 simulated people for d = 8, 40 for d = 24 and 25 for d = 48; the table shows the first two.

| Personal weights d | Items ranked n | Oracle | Population weights only | Per user, almost no prior | Shrunk toward the population |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 5 | 0.683 | 0.589 | 0.539 | 0.593 |
| 8 | 20 | 0.675 | 0.592 | 0.597 | 0.623 |
| 8 | 40 | 0.687 | 0.599 | 0.643 | 0.648 |
| 8 | 80 | 0.690 | 0.590 | 0.666 | 0.670 |
| 24 | 10 | 0.706 | 0.622 | 0.561 | 0.631 |
| 24 | 40 | 0.706 | 0.624 | 0.602 | 0.652 |
| 24 | 80 | 0.701 | 0.622 | 0.645 | 0.663 |
| 24 | 160 | 0.702 | 0.621 | 0.667 | 0.674 |

An unshrunk per-user fit does worse than simply using everyone's weights until the list holds about two to three items per weight. The shrunk fit is never worse than the population weights and recovers about half the gap to the oracle at three to five items per weight (56% at n = 40 for d = 8, 52% at n = 80 for d = 24, 48% at n = 160 for d = 48). A typical Verso list of 20 to 50 games supports about 8 to 15 personal weights, not 40 to 60. So the personal weights should sit on a few grouped dimensions (difficulty, pace, tone, story weight, social play, for instance), with the full genome entering through the population weights and the similarity terms. One practical warning came out of the simulation: a first run that put the prior's mean on the wrong scale made the shrunk fit worse than the prior as lists grew. The prior has to be estimated on the scale the model fits, which empirical Bayes over real users does automatically, and which a hand-set prior can get wrong.

Four things break, each with a fix. The implied pairs are not independent, so weight them to n or use Plackett-Luce, and take uncertainty from leave-one-out over users rather than from pair counts. The order is partly noise, since a re-asked comparison may flip; the logistic link absorbs it, the prior keeps it from becoming a weight, and the tiers can serve as a coarser, more reliable target, fit with an ordinal model, with positions inside a tier at a smaller weight. Taste lies partly outside the attributes: the simulation's ceiling of 0.68 to 0.71 follows from its assumption that attributes explain 40%, and only the harness can measure the real share. Correlated attributes trade weight between themselves; the prior steadies them, and grouping before the personal fit avoids the problem.

Verso's two escape hatches carry information the weight model can use. A "too close" answer is a near-tie, and the Rao-Kupper model handles it with a threshold θ: with Δ = w · (x_a − x_b), P(a above b) = σ(Δ − θ), P(b above a) = σ(−Δ − θ), and the tie takes the rest ([Encyclopedia of Mathematics, paired comparison model](https://encyclopediaofmath.org/wiki/Paired_comparison_model)). At θ = 1 the tie probability is 0.462 when Δ = 0, 0.381 at Δ = 1, 0.222 at Δ = 2 and 0.101 at Δ = 3. A "too close" answer therefore pulls w · (x_a − x_b) toward zero: the attributes on which the two items differ matter little to this person, in that direction. The current equal spacing of positions throws that away, and keeping it costs one θ per user or per medium. "Can't compare" is different. The choice literature finds comparisons hardest when options trade off against each other ([Tversky and Russo, J. Math. Psych. 1969](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/c1ecacdc-75f2-417a-93a0-ac97c1ca1a5d); [Shubatt and Yang, arXiv 2401.17578, v6 2026-10-20](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17578)), which in attribute space means items far apart and in different directions, with neither dominating. The researchers draw three uses from that, all inferences: leave can't-compare pairs out of the weight likelihood, since the person declined to say; treat them as cannot-link constraints when clustering the loved tier into prototypes, so two loved items the person refused to compare land in different clusters ([Wagstaff et al., ICML 2001](https://www.wkiri.com/research/papers/wagstaff-kmeans-01.pdf)); and check in the log whether can't-compare pairs sit farther apart in genome distance than ordinary pairs, which is a free test of whether the genome measures anything real. No study was found that uses a "can't compare" answer this way.

## Three picks that are not one game three times

Nearest-neighbor matching has a known failure: the three closest games to a soulslike fan's taste are often three versions of one experience. Pandora met this partly through law (the per-album and per-artist caps), partly through Modes, and partly by removing songs similar to one a listener thumbs down ([IEEE Spectrum, 2017-08-01](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-machine-learning-is-reinventing-the-way-we-discover-music)). The general tool is maximal marginal relevance (MMR): choose each next item to maximize λ × relevance − (1 − λ) × its highest similarity to the items already chosen, where λ = 1 is plain relevance and lower values buy diversity ([Carbonell and Goldstein, SIGIR 1998](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/ammoffat/sigir98/abstracts/carbonell.html)). Diversity costs measured accuracy and can still win with people: on book data and a study of more than 2,100 people, diversified lists were less accurate and better liked ([Ziegler et al., WWW 2005](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p22.pdf)).

The researchers' worked example: five candidates for a soulslike fan with relevance Dark Souls Remastered 0.95, Dark Souls II 0.90, Lies of P 0.85, Hollow Knight 0.80 and Hades 0.75; the three souls games 0.8 to 0.9 similar to each other and 0.3 to 0.45 to the other two, Hollow Knight and Hades 0.5 to each other. By relevance alone the three picks are Dark Souls Remastered, Dark Souls II and Lies of P. At λ = 0.85 they become Remastered, Dark Souls II and Hollow Knight; at λ = 0.7, Remastered, Hollow Knight and Hades. MMR fixes the set but not the first pick: the remaster of a game the person may already have ranked stays on top, so a relation filter has to run before MMR, not instead of it.

The order of operations the researchers propose: first, exclude any candidate that is an edition, port, remake or remaster of a ranked item, using IGDB's `version_parent`, `parent_game`, `remakes` and `remasters` fields ([game-data-sources.md](game-data-sources.md); [igdb-api-types schema](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DmitryScaletta/igdb-api-types/master/api.json)), unless the person asks for it; second, allow at most one pick per franchise or collection among the three, which is Pandora's statutory cap turned into a product rule; third, run MMR over genome similarity with λ near 0.7 to 0.8, with relevance and similarity both on 0-to-1 scales so that λ means something; fourth, spread the picks across the person's prototypes in proportion to cluster size. Verso's mirror keeps `parent_game` and `version_parent` in each game's `metadata` as raw IGDB ids with no link built from them, and does not mirror remakes, remasters, collections or franchises, so the first two steps need a small catalog change for games; films already store their TMDB collection in `movie_details`.

At most one of three slots should explore, chosen by uncertainty rather than at random. With a posterior over each person's weights, Thompson sampling is natural: draw one set of weights from the posterior, score, and take the best candidate not already picked, which was highly competitive with the state of the art on display ads and news recommendation ([Chapelle and Li, NIPS 2011](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2011/hash/e53a0a2978c28872a4505bdb51db06dc-Abstract.html)). A person with a short list gets varied third picks automatically and a person with a long list gets near-greedy ones. The slot should carry the design's existing "a stretch" label and be logged, since the tier it later lands in is the best evidence about how uncertain the weights are. Serendipity then gets a Verso measure. Ge, Delgado-Battenfeld and Jannach define a serendipitous item as one that is liked and unexpected relative to a primitive baseline recommender ([Ge et al., RecSys 2010](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/recsys/GeDJ10); survey in [Kaminskas and Bridge, ACM TiiS 2016](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/diversity-serendipity-novelty-and-coverage-a-survey-and-empirical/)); in Verso's terms, a pick outside the community prior's top 100 for the same filters that the person later ranks Liked or Loved.

## At 70,000 games, score exactly and skip the vector index

pgvector, the Postgres extension Supabase ships, supports every measure above natively: `<->` for Euclidean distance, `<=>` for cosine distance, `<#>` for negative inner product, `<+>` for L1, plus Hamming and Jaccard on bit vectors. Without an index it searches exactly, with perfect recall; its approximate indexes, HNSW and IVFFlat, trade recall for speed, and with a filter applied after an approximate scan a query can return fewer rows than asked, which version 0.8.0 (2024-10-30) addressed with iterative scans ([pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector); [pgvector CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pgvector/pgvector/master/CHANGELOG.md)). The latest release is 0.8.7, of 2026-10-01. The local Supabase image in this repo (Postgres 17.6) offers pgvector 0.8.2 as an available extension, not installed in Verso's database today; the hosted version was not confirmed.

The researchers timed it on this machine on 2026-10-29 (Apple M1 Pro, the local Supabase Postgres container, pgvector created inside a transaction that was rolled back, synthetic data, top 30 returned):

| Query | Rows scored | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Exact Euclidean, 48 attributes, no index | 70,000 | 27 to 33 ms |
| Exact cosine | 70,000 | 26 to 27 ms |
| Exact per-user weighted Euclidean | 70,000 | 33 to 40 ms |
| The same, after a filter keeping 3% | about 2,100 | 4.8 ms |
| Weighted Euclidean in plain SQL arrays (`real[]`), no pgvector | about 2,100 | 86 ms |
| The same in plain SQL arrays | 70,000 | 2.6 s |
| HNSW index scan | index | 1.1 ms |
| HNSW index build, 70,000 vectors | | 14 to 16 s |

An exact score over the whole games catalog is tens of milliseconds and over a filtered pool a few, well inside a suggestion request, and pgvector's C arithmetic runs 20 to 80 times faster than the same sums over Postgres arrays. For comparison, the existing content scorer ran its full candidate pass over 70,000 games in about 0.4 s with idf counted live ([engine design](../design/recommendation-engine.md#the-first-batch-two-prs-from-main-not-stacked)).

Per-user weights rule out a shared index. A weighted Euclidean distance, the sum of w_k (x_k − q_k)², equals the plain distance between √w ⊙ x and √w ⊙ q (each attribute multiplied by the square root of its weight), so each person's weights define a different space and an index built on the raw vectors does not apply. Algebra rescues it on paper: the weighted distance equals an inner product between a stored augmented vector (x, x²) and a per-user query (−2 w ⊙ q, w), plus a constant, and an exact scan on that inner product returned the same top 30 as the weighted exact search. Through an HNSW index, though, recall at 30 was 0.08 (1 to 5 of the true 30 over ten queries) on structured data where plain unweighted Euclidean through HNSW reached 0.93. The researchers' conclusion: never index personal weighted distance; score it exactly over the filtered pool. A purely linear personal score, s = w · x, could use one shared inner-product index with the weights as the query, but its recall was not measured.

The current design needs no index at all. `suggestion_candidates` scores the 500 best items by prior after its filters, and by the researchers' timings even plain arrays would score 500 genome vectors in about 20 ms (an extrapolation from 86 ms for 2,100). The cost appears with max-similarity to loved items: 20 loved games against 500 candidates is 10,000 distance evaluations, about 0.4 s in plain arrays by the same extrapolation and a few milliseconds in pgvector. So the proposal is pgvector for the arithmetic, with no index: stage 1, hard filters in SQL (medium, platform, services, runtime, not yet ranked); stage 2, exact scores over that pool (idf-tag cosine, genome similarity to loved items, the prior); stage 3, the relation filter and MMR on the top 50 to 100. If an unfiltered "more like this" across all 70,000 games is ever needed, HNSW on the unweighted or population-weighted genome can fetch about 500 for an exact personal rerank, with `hnsw.iterative_scan` on for selective filters: in the researchers' 3% filter test a plain HNSW scan returned 0 rows and the iterative scan returned 30.

## The harness decides, with both scores written on the same insertion

The instruments already exist. `recommendation_loo` and its summary report, per user and medium, the mean absolute errors of the content score, the user's mean and the prior, the tier accuracy and the Spearman correlation; `prediction_calibration` reports the prospective insertion rows by user, medium, `model_version`, list-size band, confidence and source; and `pnpm engine:report` prints both ([engine design](../design/recommendation-engine.md#every-insertion-is-a-prediction-rafa-2026-10-15)). A genome scorer slots in as a second content score, and the question is only how to compare it honestly.

Compare the two scorers paired, on the same held-out items and the same insertions, never as two separate averages. Across five TREC collections, the paired t-test, the bootstrap and the randomization test agreed closely, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank and sign tests missed real differences and flagged false ones ([Smucker, Allan and Carterette, CIKM 2007](https://ciir-publications.cs.umass.edu/getpdf.php?id=744)). Four metrics fit Verso's data: absolute error on the 0-to-10 score; tier hit; pairwise accuracy, the share of the user's other ranked items whose order against the held-out item the scorer gets right (0.5 is chance), which is closest to the question Verso actually asks people and is what the weight model optimizes; and Spearman per list.

Two rules keep the comparison clean, both the researchers' inferences. In leave-one-out, the personal weights must be refit with the held-out item excluded, and the population prior fit on other users only; otherwise the held-out item's own position has already shaped the weights and the harness flatters the genome. At each insertion, both scores should be written into the row's existing `components` jsonb, say `content_v1` and `genome_v1`, computed from the list as it stood and neither read during the insertion, which is the rule the design already sets for the guess. Every insertion then becomes a paired observation of the two scorers on the same item at the same moment, which removes the differences in who used which version and when that a split by `model_version` carries.

The sample sizes are sobering. By the researchers' power arithmetic (two-sided α = 0.05, power 0.8), a paired comparison of absolute errors needs about (2.8 × sd / δ)² items, where sd is the standard deviation of the per-item difference in errors and δ the true gain: with sd = 1.0 point, about 87 items for a gain of 0.3, 196 for 0.2 and 784 for 0.1. For pairwise accuracy only the pairs on which the two scorers disagree carry information; if they disagree on 20% of pairs and one is 4 points more accurate, about 970 pairs in total are needed. Those counts assume independent units, and items within one list are not. With few users the honest test is at the user level: a paired t-test on per-user mean differences detects a standardized per-user effect of about 1.6 with 5 users, 1.1 with 8, 0.9 with 12, 0.66 with 20 and 0.4 with 50. With a dozen testers, the genome has to win for nearly every one of them to show at all. The sd values are guesses until the harness produces a first one.

The decision rule that fits the data Verso will have: report per-user paired differences in error, pairwise accuracy and tier hit, test them with a user-level randomization test or a bootstrap over users, and ship the genome as one weighted term of the blend whose weight the harness fits, never as a replacement. A term that does not help earns a weight near zero without a separate decision. Because accuracy and satisfaction diverge under diversification, MMR and the exploration slot are judged also by the tier their picks later land in. And the can't-compare test from the weights section runs on the comparison log for free, before any user sees a genome-driven pick.

## Worth a pilot, not yet a build

Rafa's "we NEED that" is a hypothesis with real support. Verso lives in the regime where Pandora said its genome earned its keep: no crowd, new items, and, under decision 24, a recommender limited to the community prior and the content profile until neighbors prove themselves. Layer 1's features are coarse. Twenty-three IGDB genres, a developer, a decade and a platform cannot tell a brutal action RPG from a gentle one, or a two-hour melancholy walk from a two-hour comedy; a graded genome can. The same attributes would power filters people ask for ("short and relaxing tonight") and reasons the design wants ("slower and cozier than your Loved"), and the closest published result, model-scored game attributes added to learned recommenders, raised mean reciprocal rank by 14% to 20% ([Jia et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11658v1)). The cost of scoring is now dollars.

The case against is just as concrete. No study pits an expert genome against collaborative filtering; content features usually lose to collaborative filtering once there is data; and Pandora itself layered thumbs, a per-pair veto and collaborative filtering on top within a decade, with weights it never learned from feedback. A single attribute score is coarse: people disagree with each other by about 0.74 points on a 5-point scale, the best model in the Tag Genome work misses by about 0.8, and a judge can agree with people on whole lists while agreeing on single items barely better than chance. How much of a person's taste the attributes explain is unknown; the simulation's ceiling rests on an assumed 40%. With a dozen testers, the harness can only see a large, consistent gain. The genome also adds work that does not end: a rubric to version, a golden set to maintain, a model whose alias may change under it, and two data-terms questions that may not resolve in Verso's favor for films.

A cheaper rival has to be tested alongside it, and this is the researchers' inference. IGDB's themes, player perspectives, game modes and keywords come from the same free API under the same terms as the genres Verso already mirrors. Added to layer 1 as more binary features, they need no model, no rubric and no new terms question. If they capture most of the gain, the graded genome is not needed; if the genome beats them too, the case is made.

The verdict: the need is not shown, and a pilot that would show it is cheap. Score only the games already on testers' lists (a few hundred, since leave-one-out only ever scores ranked items), on a draft rubric of 15 to 20 attributes; have Rafa score about 50 of them by hand as the golden set; and run the harness offline with three arms (content-v1, content-v1 plus IGDB's taxonomies, and content-v1 plus the genome term), each paired per user. Build the full genome only if the third arm wins for most users.

## What this means for Verso

A proposal, not a decision. Each part below names where it would sit in the engine as built, and none of it ships unless the harness says so.

The data terms come first. Before any catalog text reaches a model, one email to partner@igdb.com should ask for written permission on three points: sending IGDB summaries and metadata to a model provider to score attributes; storing those derived scores and showing them to Verso's users; and whether derived scores may appear in the aggregate, de-identified data products decision 21 allows. A second, to TMDB, should ask whether a commercial agreement can cover model processing and derived scores under sections 1.C and 2.A, and whether the existing `suggest` function's film candidates fall under 1.C; the commercial-license email that film-tv-data-sources.md already says is due before pricing can carry it. Steam tags stay out until Valve's terms are clear, and the MovieLens Tag Genome stays out unless GroupLens permits commercial use. The answers decide which media get a genome.

One genome per medium, games first. Pandora built five music genomes and a separate podcast genome, and Verso's rule 24 already says games and films never share a similarity or a prediction. Games go first because IGDB's terms are friendlier and no graded game vocabulary exists anywhere; films follow a written TMDB answer. Each genome is a short list, 15 to 25 attributes, designed without near-duplicates and checked on a scored sample with a correlation matrix, merging attributes that move together. Binary presence features come from the catalog, graded intensities from the model, and length, year, platform, services and reception stay out of the distance as filters or the prior. A draft for Rafa to cut, drawn from Pandora's split between "sonoric" and stylistic traits, Quantic Foundry's twelve motivations, the 12 game and movie attributes in Jia et al., and the dimensions Netflix's taggers reportedly scored:

| Medium | Graded 0 to 5, scored by a model under a rubric | Binary, from the catalog |
|---|---|---|
| Games | Difficulty; pace; combat intensity; low stress (coziness); story weight; exploration and discovery; strategy and planning depth; systems complexity; darkness of tone; humor; tension or horror; creativity and building; replayability; completionist grind | IGDB multiplayer modes (online and offline co-op, split screen, with maximums); player perspective; themes such as open world, sandbox and survival |
| Films | Pace; tension; darkness of tone; humor; romance; violence and gore; complexity of plot; emotional weight; spectacle; dialogue density; how conclusive the ending is; realism to fantasy | TMDB genres, collection, and keywords if TMDB permits |

Each graded attribute gets a one-line definition, a described meaning for 0, 1, 3 and 5, two or three anchor titles at each end, and a "cannot tell" option. The production scorer is Claude Haiku 5.5 through the Batch API, grounded on the catalog's own text with reception fields left out, attribute groups scored in separate calls, the abstention gates above, and every row stamped with rubric version, model id and prompt hash. About 10% of items are scored a second time with a different wording or model. An attribute stays only if the model agrees with a human golden set (an ICC of about 0.7 is a reasonable starting bar) and with itself across wordings (about 0.8); attributes that fail are dropped, much as Pandora's audio models recovered its rhythmic genes and not its perceptual ones.

The genome is a separate term, not a wider vector. Today's layer 1 keeps its cosine on idf-weighted binary features; the genome adds a second content term on standardized 0-to-5 attributes, measured by weighted Euclidean or L1 distance turned into a similarity, and the blend's weights between the two come from the harness. In the engine's vocabulary the combination is a `content-v2` that writes a `genome_v1` component beside `content_v1`. Concatenating the two would put one measure wrong on half the coordinates.

A person is scored against their own loved items, not their average. The default is the maximum similarity to a Loved item, or the mean of the top two or three, weighted by position, which also writes the reason ("closest to Hollow Knight, which you put in Loved"). From a few dozen Loved items, prototypes take over: k-medoids or Ward clusters of the Loved tier (k of 1 to 4 for a 50-item list), can't-compare pairs as cannot-link constraints, the three picks shared across prototypes by cluster size. Disliked and Dropped items subtract a local penalty, γ × the highest similarity to one of them, starting at γ = 0.3 to 0.5. Layer 1's linear profile remains one term.

Weights start shared and become personal only as the list allows. The first build uses population weights, equal after standardization. Personal weights come later, on 5 to 8 grouped dimensions (difficulty, pace, tone, story, social play and the like), fit nightly by pairwise logistic regression on implied pairs weighted so the list counts as about n observations, or by Plackett-Luce, and shrunk toward the population mean, with empirical Bayes once there are enough users. They switch on per user at about three to five ranked items per weight, so 15 to 40 ranked games. "Too close" enters as a Rao-Kupper tie with one threshold per medium; "can't compare" stays out of the likelihood.

"More like this" is Pandora's station, and the genome's natural first screen. On an item page, the item is the seed, the person's weights shape the distance, and a focus chip ("for its atmosphere") raises the weights of the attributes behind it, as Pandora's focus traits did. With several seeds picked by the person, inverse-variance weights with Pandora's floor are the default. It is a screen, so it goes through Figma, and it waits for the harness.

Diversity is a fixed order of rules: drop editions, ports, remakes and remasters of ranked items (this needs IGDB's relation, collection and franchise fields mirrored, a small catalog change worth making whatever the genome's verdict); allow one pick per franchise or collection; run MMR over genome similarity with λ near 0.7 to 0.8; spread picks across prototypes. At most one slot explores, chosen by Thompson sampling from the weight posterior and labeled "a stretch".

Scoring is exact over the filtered pool. `suggestion_candidates` already narrows to 500 items by prior after its filters; the genome lives in a `vector(d)` column with pgvector enabled for its arithmetic and no index, which keeps max-similarity over 20 Loved items to a few milliseconds. An HNSW index on the population-weighted genome is needed only for an unfiltered catalog-wide "more like this", and then only to fetch about 500 for an exact personal rerank, with iterative scans on.

The evaluation plan uses both instruments, paired. In `recommendation_loo`, the genome term is computed with personal weights refit without the held-out item; at every insertion, `finish_insertion` writes `content_v1` and `genome_v1` into `components`, neither read during the placement. The report shows per-user paired differences in absolute error, pairwise accuracy and tier hit, tested at the user level, and the blend weight follows. The can't-compare test runs on the comparison log for free.

The cost is small in money and moderate in work. Model calls: under $10 for the pilot, $8 to $42 to score every game once, $3 to $15 for films if TMDB allows, and pennies a night for new items. Rafa's time: about 90 minutes to score 50 games he knows as the golden set, plus cutting the draft list. Engineering, as the researchers' estimate: a pilot of a day or two offline, and a full build of about four PRs (an attribute table with its scorer on a GitHub Actions schedule like the IGDB mirror; the genome term in `recommendation_scores` and the harness; the shadow score at insertion; personal weights, the relation filter and MMR), each behind the harness.

The cheapest first step that would tell whether the genome is worth it is the offline pilot. Score only the games already on testers' lists, a few hundred, on the cut rubric. Until IGDB answers, the prompt carries title and release year only, so it uses a large model (Sonnet 5.5 or Opus 5.5, a few dollars for a few hundred games) whose knowledge of popular games is far better than a small model's; after IGDB answers, Haiku 5.5 grounded on IGDB text is the production configuration to test. Rafa scores about 50 of the games by hand. The harness then runs three arms paired per user: content-v1, content-v1 plus IGDB's themes, perspectives, modes and keywords as binary features, and content-v1 plus the genome term. If the genome arm beats both for most testers on pairwise accuracy, build it; if the IGDB arm captures the gain, mirror those fields and stop; if neither helps, the genome waits for more users.

Synopsis embeddings are a fourth arm, proposed (Rafa, 2026-10-29, from a lecture the same day on Pandora and on film recommenders built from synopsis embeddings). The engine design already plans one text-embedding model over synopsis plus tags in pgvector at stage 4, for "games like Outer Wilds" and the cross-media slot. The research found no study that pits scored attributes against embeddings of the same text; the literature combines them, the embedding for retrieval and the attributes for reasons, filters and personal weights ([model-built-genome notes](attribute-genome-notes/model-built-genome.md)). An embedding cannot write "slower and cozier than your Loved", and a 30-item list cannot fit personal weights on hundreds of dimensions, so it complements the genome rather than replacing it. The arm is content-v1 plus the highest cosine between a candidate's summary embedding and the person's Loved items, never their mean, which corrects the 2026-09-24 sketch that averaged the top tier ([llm-and-cross-domain notes](recommendation-engine-notes/llm-and-cross-domain.md)). It costs little more than the pilot already does. The same terms gate applies: embedding TMDB overviews is a machine-learning use under clause 1.C, and IGDB summaries fall under the Twitch question, though an open embedding model run on Verso's own machine sends nothing to a third party, which the IGDB email should confirm is enough.

## Inferences to flag

Calls this report makes that Rafa did not, each reversible:

- (a) The genome is treated as a hypothesis to test, not a need: worth a pilot, not yet a build.
- (b) The genome enters as a separate content term beside layer 1's cosine on tags, blended by harness-fitted weights, never as a replacement or a concatenated vector.
- (c) Games first; films wait on a written TMDB answer.
- (d) The draft attribute lists, their size (15 to 25 per medium), and the split between model-scored graded attributes and catalog binary ones.
- (e) Claude Haiku 5.5 as the production scorer, grounded on catalog text; a larger model on title and year for the pilot.
- (f) The validation bars, an ICC of about 0.7 against the golden set and about 0.8 against itself.
- (g) Maximum similarity to Loved items as the default, prototypes from a few dozen Loved items, and a local penalty near Disliked and Dropped items with γ starting at 0.3 to 0.5.
- (h) Population weights first; personal weights on 5 to 8 grouped dimensions, switched on at about three to five ranked items per weight.
- (i) "Too close" as a Rao-Kupper tie, "can't compare" left out of the likelihood and used as a cannot-link constraint and a validity test.
- (j) The diversity order (relation filter, one per franchise, MMR at λ 0.7 to 0.8, prototypes) and one exploration slot by Thompson sampling.
- (k) pgvector for exact arithmetic with no index; HNSW only for an unfiltered catalog-wide search.
- (l) IGDB's themes, perspectives, modes and keywords as the cheaper rival arm in the pilot.

## Addendum, 2026-10-29 night: what the lecture adds

The transcript of the lecture that started this thread, on Pandora's genome and then on a film recommender built from synopsis embeddings, was published the same night. Most of it is already in this report or in [preference-learning.md](preference-learning.md): the multi-dimensional Pythagorean theorem, nearest neighbors over a genome, films found close by the cosine of their synopsis embeddings, and two-tower models at the scale of TikTok, which the preference-learning ranking puts at hundreds of overlapping users. The lecture also preferred implicit signals such as watch time to ratings, because the people who leave ratings skew toward those who disliked the thing; that changes nothing here, since Verso's signal is a deliberate ranking, not engagement. Five points are new or sharper, read below for Verso. The calls on them are this report's and are flagged at the end of each paragraph and in the list of inferences.

### Embeddings find the subject, not the tone

The demo embedded the synopses of 300 well-known films with OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small (1,536 dimensions) and listed each film's nearest neighbors. Mean Girls returned 10 Things I Hate About You, Grease, Lady Bird, Ferris Bueller's Day Off and Dead Poets Society; Finding Nemo returned Up, The Wild Robot and Jaws; Schindler's List returned Inglourious Basterds. The last two are the instructive failures. A synopsis embedding sorts by what a film is about, sharks or the Second World War, more than by its tone or its audience, so a toddler who loved Finding Nemo is offered Jaws, and the night for Schindler's List is not the night for a black comedy about Nazi hunters. Games have the same trap: a cozy farming game and a horror game set on a farm share a subject and little else. This is the plainest case yet for the division of labor this report proposes: the embedding retrieves by subject, and graded attributes such as darkness of tone, humor, violence and emotional weight (the draft film list above) separate the items that share one. The lecture offered two fixes, embedding richer text (the script, reviews, the poster) and learning from past likes which embedding dimensions matter with a logistic regression, which is arm D of the preference-learning pilot. A third needs no model: an age rating as a hard filter settles the toddler case before any similarity is computed. Verso already mirrors film certifications (`media_certifications`); IGDB carries ESRB and PEGI ratings for games, which are not mirrored. For the pilot, this suggests a probe set beside the aggregate metrics: pairs from the testers' lists that share a setting and differ in tone, on which arm E (the genome) should beat arm C (embeddings) if graded attributes earn their cost (inference).

### A linear scorer cannot hold a sweet spot

The lecture set kNN against regression. A regression's coefficient on tempo says faster is better or worse, never "between 40 and 50 beats per minute"; kNN fits any shape, because it only asks how the nearby items were scored. Its price is that there is no coefficient to read. Both halves land on Verso. The personal scorer in the preference-learning report is linear in its attributes and components, s = α·p + w·a + v·z, so it cannot say "some challenge but not brutal" or "long but not endless" on a graded axis. Conjoint analysis solved this decades ago: part-worth coding gives each level of an attribute its own weight (low, middle and high), and the ideal-point model adds a squared term so that a person's utility peaks at their preferred level. For the genome's graded attributes in arms D and E, the default should be part-worth coding on three levels, or an ideal point, rather than one linear weight. That costs two weights per attribute instead of one, which tightens the budget of one weight per three to five ranked items, so it suits the 5 to 8 grouped dimensions, not every attribute (inference). It is also a third reason, beside camps of taste and reach beyond the Loved tier, to keep the max-similarity term beside the scorer, since kNN captures sweet spots for free. The cost the lecture named, that kNN cannot be explained, applies to attribute effects, not to Verso's reasons: an item-to-item neighbor explains itself ("closest to Hollow Knight, in your Loved").

### The lecture's kNN is a regressor, and it fails where the list is thin

The personal recommender built in the lecture predicted a person's rating of an unseen film as the average of their ratings of the three nearest films they had rated. It failed in a telling way. With about twenty ratings, the three nearest rated films can be far away: Alien was predicted at 4.6 out of 5 from Avatar, Gravity and The Martian, which share outer space and little else. Two things follow for arm C. First, it should run in two forms, the max-similarity to Loved items proposed above and this regressor over the whole ranked list with each neighbor weighted by its similarity, because the regressor uses the middle tiers that max-similarity ignores; the harness picks (inference). Second, either form needs a floor. When the nearest ranked item is far, the kNN term should fall back toward the person's mean or the community prior instead of borrowing a confident score from a distant item; counting the prior as one more neighbor at a fixed similarity does this in one line (inference). The number of neighbors is chosen the way the lecture chose it, by sweeping k from 1 to 10 and keeping the best held-out score: k = 1 copies one neighbor's noise, and a large k averages Finding Nemo with The Shawshank Redemption. In Verso the sweep runs in the leave-one-out harness, with k fit across testers, not per person.

### Averaging suits comparing people, and rankings have no scale to misuse

The lecture also averaged. Each student's "movie love essence" was the mean embedding of the films they rated 5 out of 5, and multidimensional scaling placed those means on a two-dimensional map of who shares whose taste; the films rated 1 to 3 gave a second map, of shared dislikes. This does not contradict this report's rule. Averaging is wrong for scoring items, where two camps of taste cancel, and reasonable for comparing people, where a rough location is the point. A taste match between friends, if Verso ever shows one, could start there (inference). The same demo exposed a flaw Verso avoids. The students' mean ratings ran from 2.95 to 4.41, harsh and lenient raters using the same five stars differently, and nothing in an average of ratings corrects for it. A ranked list has no scale to misuse, since each item's place is relative to the person's own other items. That is one more argument for head-to-head ranking, and a caution wherever external star ratings feed the community prior.

### Spreading the first ratings, and two smaller notes

The site that collected the students' ratings chose each next film to be as different as possible from those already rated, so each person's ratings spread across the space. That is the coverage the thin-list failure above calls for, and it has a cost the demo also showed: a film's count of raters then depends on the sampler as much as on its popularity. Verso's bulk-entry deck has a decided order (what the profile expects the person to have played, top sellers pinned). Whether to diversify within that order, so the first ten ranked games cover more of a person's taste, is recorded as an open thought in the [onboarding guidelines](../design/onboarding-guidelines.md), not proposed here.

For unit-length vectors, as OpenAI's embeddings are, cosine similarity and Euclidean distance rank neighbors identically, because the squared distance is 2 − 2 × cosine. After the centering and PCA the preference-learning report recommends, vectors are no longer unit length, so they should be normalized again before either is used, or the two will disagree.

And the genome's first test, as Westergren tells it in a recorded talk shown in the lecture: about 10,000 songs scored over roughly nine months for about $1 million (the lecture's estimate), held in an Excel spreadsheet whose macro took about four minutes to rank the catalog against one song. The first seed was a Beatles song, and its nearest neighbor was an early Bee Gees track, "New York Mining Disaster 1941". The team took that as a failure until they listened and heard the Bee Gees' early Beatles-like sound. A match that looks wrong on paper and is right by ear is the genome's case in one example. The lecture's account that two analysts scored every song conflicts with the published one above, that only a share are scored twice; this report keeps the published account.
