# Preference elicitation and cold-start onboarding: what the evidence says

Research date: 2026-10-01. Read against `docs/private/reframe.md` (the decoded message and the "what changes" table) and `docs/design/recommendation-engine.md` (Layers 0 to 2), so the guidance below is scoped to Verso's actual engine: a community prior that is always on, a content profile that switches on at about five ranked items per medium, and a neighbor layer that switches on at about ten ranked items with three or more co-ranking neighbors, all built on positional scores from head-to-head insertion within four tiers, not star ratings. Every claim below is dated to its source and tagged as a paper (with venue and year), a company statement, or a benchmark report with its sample. Where a claim could not be verified against a primary source in this research pass, that is said explicitly in the relevant Gaps subsection rather than silently upgraded to a stronger citation than it deserves.

## Takeaway

Across two decades of recommender-systems research and a decade of consumer-product practice, the evidence converges on a small set of repeated findings: revealed choices (ratings, picks, comparisons) on concrete items predict taste far better than stated categories or demographics; a handful of well-chosen items (roughly 3 to 15 across the products and papers reviewed here) captures most of the achievable cold-start accuracy, with returns flattening or reversing past that point when the items are poorly chosen; comparing two items is cheaper and at least as informative as rating one, and in the one study built explicitly to test that trade-off it cut the number of questions needed by up to a factor of three (Sepliarskaia, Kiseleva, Radlinski, de Rijke, RecSys 2018); context (time, place, companion) and goal questions pay for themselves once there is enough logged behavior to make a context segment dense, not from a cold, one-time answer; demographics add little to media-taste accuracy specifically, whatever their value elsewhere; and the single most consistent, highest-confidence finding across every 2024-to-2026 benchmark report reviewed here is that completion and long-run retention are set in the first session, so every screen asked before the product shows its first good answer has a real, measured cost. Verso's existing design (a questionnaire that only routes by medium, a content profile that turns on at about five ranked items, and neighbors at about ten) sits inside this evidence rather than against it. The main risk the literature flags is not the thresholds themselves but the temptation to layer stated-preference questions (genre, mood, time available, household) on top of a mechanism, head-to-head comparison, that the research already treats as a stronger substitute for most of them.

## 1. Which items should a new user be asked about

### Cited findings

Rashid, Albert, Cosley, Lam, McNee, Konstan, Riedl, "Getting to Know You: Learning New User Preferences in Recommender Systems" (paper, IUI 2002, read in full from the primary PDF): tested Random, the existing MovieLens "Classique" baseline, Popularity, Pure Entropy, Popularity times Entropy (and a log-smoothed variant), and Item-Item personalized selection, offline on 7,335 users, 4,117 movies, and 2.7 million historical ratings (users with 200 or more ratings only), presenting 30, 45, 60, or 90 candidate movies per simulated signup. They then ran a 10-day live deployment on MovieLens with 351 new users split across Popularity (91 users, 81 completed), Item-Item (92, 82 completed), log(Popularity times Entropy) (92, 79 completed), and the existing Classique flow (76, 60 completed). Pure Entropy was "shockingly poor" on accuracy and nearly unusable on effort: two pilot users had to view "several hundred movies each before finding ten to rate," so it was dropped from the live test entirely. Popularity times Entropy gave the best accuracy (lowest mean absolute error) for a given number of items shown, with plain Popularity close behind and far cheaper (mean 1.9 pages of ten movies to finish signup, with over 90% of users done within five pages). Item-Item personalized found the most items a user could actually rate, but this did not translate into better predictions: a plain Random strategy reached a mean absolute error with only 8 ratings that the Item-Item strategy needed 57 ratings to match, because Item-Item's similarity-chasing oversamples one taste cluster (their worked example: start a user on one Star Trek sequel and the system keeps serving more Star Trek, teaching the model a lot about one franchise and little else). The old Classique flow needed a mean of 7.0 pages, more than three times the effort of the best strategies, and had the highest dropout of the four live groups (16 of 76, 21%). Their stated recommendation for a system with no data at all on a new user: lead with the most popular items, then switch to item-item personalization as soon as at least one rating exists.

Rashid, Karypis, Riedl, "Learning Preferences of New Users in Recommender Systems: An Information Theoretic Approach" (paper, ACM SIGKDD Explorations Newsletter 10(2), 2008): extends the 2002 paper's popularity-and-entropy line into a more formally derived information-theoretic family of strategies, re-validated with a further live MovieLens deployment. I could not retrieve this paper's own result tables (it sits behind ACM's paywall with no open mirror I could find in this pass), so I report only its role in the lineage, not its specific numbers; see Gaps.

Golbandi, Koren, Lempel, "On Bootstrapping Recommender Systems" (paper, CIKM 2010) and "Adaptive Bootstrapping of Recommender Systems Using Decision Trees" (paper, WSDM 2011): introduced decision-tree interviews, where each node asks about one item and splits new users into three branches (loved it, hated it, have not rated it), with different follow-up items down each branch, so the questions adapt to each answer instead of following a fixed list. I could not retrieve either paper's own result tables directly (same paywall situation), but the method and its central weakness are confirmed and quantified by papers that built on it: Sun, Li, Lee, Zhou, Lebanon, Zha, "Learning Multiple-Question Decision Trees for Cold-Start Recommendation" (paper, WSDM 2013, read in full from the primary PDF) show that single-question decision trees in this family send more than 80% of users down the "unknown, haven't rated it" branch at every split, since most users have an opinion on only a tiny fraction of a large catalog, and that a single-question tree's accuracy (RMSE, on MovieLens1M, EachMovie, and a Netflix sample) stops improving after about 6 to 7 screens as leaf nodes run out of training users and start overfitting. Their fix, asking 2 to 4 items per screen instead of one, reduces RMSE faster per screen and per second of user time; a 76-person user study found answering 2 items per screen takes about 1.5 times as long as answering 1 (6.73 versus 4.41 seconds for binary like/dislike/unknown answers), 4 items takes about 2.5 times as long (10.42 seconds), and a 5-point rating response costs roughly 2.5 to 3.5 seconds more than a binary response at every screen size, with the gap mattering more on short surveys than long ones.

Chang, Harper, Terveen, "Using Groups of Items for Preference Elicitation in Recommender Systems" (paper, CSCW 2015): deployed on live MovieLens, replacing the platform's prior rate-15-individual-items signup gate with an interface asking new users to express preference across automatically clustered groups of movies instead of single titles. I could not obtain the full PDF (ACM's abstract page and the paper's DOI, 10.1145/2675133.2675210, were reachable, the full text was not), so I rely on the abstract and on consistent secondary characterization: users completed elicitation in under half the time of the 15-item baseline and reported higher satisfaction with the resulting recommendations. A 2023 audit of MovieLens's own history, "Our Model Achieves Excellent Performance on MovieLens: What Does It Mean?" (paper, arXiv 2307.09985), confirms the sequence (a group/genre-points step, then a reduced individual-rating pool) and reports that under the resulting flow, 49.19% of new users finish all onboarding ratings within a single day and 85.6% finish within five days.

Elahi, Ricci, Rubens, "A Survey of Active Learning in Collaborative Filtering Recommender Systems" (paper, Computer Science Review 20, 2016): surveys the whole family above (popularity, entropy, their products, personalized and item-item selection, and model-based active learning) as instances of one problem, choosing which item to ask about next to most reduce the system's uncertainty about a user. I read this at the abstract and citation-context level only (the full ScienceDirect text was not reachable in this pass); its chief value for Verso is as an index of the strategy space, not a new number.

Sepliarskaia, Kiseleva, Radlinski, de Rijke, "Preference Elicitation as an Optimization Problem" (paper, RecSys 2018): the first work to pose picking a fixed (static) questionnaire as a formal optimization rather than a hand-tuned heuristic like Popularity times Entropy, and to prove which objective that optimization should target. Their method (a static preference questionnaire, SPQ) asks relative, comparative questions rather than absolute ratings, and the paper reports needing up to three times fewer questions than the prior state of the art for the same accuracy, validated on both a simulated-user benchmark and a real user study.

2019 to 2026, LLM-assisted and conversational cold start: the field has moved from hand-designed question strategies toward using a language model either to talk to the user directly or to manufacture synthetic training signal. "Cold-Start Recommendation towards the Era of Large Language Models: A Comprehensive Survey and Roadmap" (paper, arXiv 2501.01945, January 2025) catalogs this shift and cites MetaCRS (2023) as a representative conversational system that runs an exploratory dialogue specifically to identify a cold user's preferences faster. Surana, Wu, Xie, Xia, Kallus, McAuley, "An Empirical Study on Zero-Data Bootstrapping for Conversational Recommender Systems" (paper, arXiv 2504.15476, April 2025) goes further, using an LLM to synthesize plausible user preferences and conversations when no interaction data exists at all, and reports that systems bootstrapped this way reach competitive quality against systems trained on real interaction logs on the benchmarks tested.

### Inferences

The strongest, most repeated pattern across 2002 to 2018 is that systems do best asking about things a user has almost certainly encountered (so the question is answerable) that still split opinion (so the answer is informative), and that a fixed, flat rating gate is simply worse, on both effort and satisfaction, than almost any smarter alternative tried against it (Chang et al. 2015; Sepliarskaia et al. 2018; Sun et al. 2013). The decision-tree line (Golbandi 2010 onward) matters less for its specific numbers, which I could not independently verify, and more for a structural point that transfers directly to Verso: a fixed list of items to rate wastes attention on items the user has not encountered, and anything adaptive will out-perform a static list for the same user effort. No paper in this lineage tests a head-to-head comparison interface as the elicitation mechanism itself; they elicit absolute or set-level ratings and treat pairwise methods as a separate family (see section 4). Verso's ranking loop is unusual in making the comparison the only input, which the literature supports in spirit but has not tested in exactly this form.

### Gaps

I could not retrieve primary-source tables for Rashid, Karypis, Riedl 2008 or for either Golbandi, Koren, Lempel paper (2010, 2011); all three sit behind ACM's paywall with no open preprint found, so the numbers attributed to them above come from how later papers describe and benchmark against them, not from reading the originals. Chang, Harper, Terveen 2015's own accuracy tables (as opposed to its time and satisfaction findings) were likewise not reachable; the "less than half the time" and satisfaction results are corroborated across the paper's ACM listing and independent citing sources, so I treat them as solid, but I have not read the paper's own numbers. The 2019-to-2026 LLM and conversational literature is thin on exactly the comparison Verso needs most: conversational or free-text elicitation measured against a short structured picker, on the same users, for cold-start accuracy. I did not find that study.

## 2. Stated versus revealed preference

### Cited findings

Netflix (company statement, reported by Variety and widely corroborated by other outlets including the Washington Times, Engadget, and Mobilesyrup, March 2017): replaced its five-star rating system with a binary thumbs up or thumbs down. VP of Product Todd Yellin explained the change by pointing at the gap between stated and revealed preference: people rated documentaries five stars and comedies three stars, then watched the comedies far more often, an "aspirational" rating pattern rather than a predictive one. Netflix's internal testing found the simpler thumbs format drew about 200% more responses than the star scale, and Yellin's framing was explicit that behavior should drive the model more than a stated score: reported remarks from the same briefing have him saying ratings were made "less important because the implicit signal of your behavior is more important."

Netflix Tech Blog (company statement, Amatriain and Basilico, "Recommendations: Beyond the 5 Stars," Parts 1 and 2, April and June 2012): even before the 2017 change, Netflix reported that about 75% of what members watched came from some form of recommendation, meaning the production system already leaned on behavioral and contextual signals more heavily than on the explicit star rating, which fed only one of many signals into the model.

Spotify (company statement, Oskar Stål, VP of Personalization, Spotify Newsroom, October 13, 2021): new users are prompted to pick a few favorite artists immediately at signup, which seeds the home screen, but Stål frames this as the smallest part of a system that otherwise runs on behavior, stating that "every day, half a trillion events, whether they are searches, listens, or likes" feed Spotify's machine learning system. Multiple independent product teardowns of the live onboarding flow (undated precisely, describing the flow as it exists through 2024 to 2026) confirm the mechanic: at least three artists must be picked before a new user reaches the app.

Pinterest (product observation, tracing to growth lead Casey Winters's own published account of designing Pinterest's onboarding, corroborated by current UX-teardown sites through 2026): a "follow 5 topics" step framed to the user as "then we'll build a custom home feed for you," sold explicitly as a means to an immediately visible end rather than as a survey.

TikTok (company statement, TikTok Newsroom, "How TikTok Recommends Videos," undated, live as of 2026, and TikTok's in-app Support documentation): describes an initial interest-category picker (for example, pets or travel) at signup, but both sources are explicit that this stated step is only a seed; the For You feed is then adjusted by what a person actually watches, likes, and comments on, and a user who skips the picker is simply shown what is currently popular rather than being blocked.

Academic grounding for why stated categories underperform: Rashid et al. 2002 (IUI 2002, read in full) argue directly that category or demographic preference models "are thus less general than the methods we present; they apply only to certain domains, and require domain-specific expertise," because the premise of collaborative filtering is that a person's own revealed preferences predict their other preferences better than any attribute does. The same paper flags a counterweight worth holding onto: revealed behavior is not a clean signal either, since "seeing a movie is probably correlated with liking a movie," so inferring preference purely from exposure risks collecting mostly positive signal (not Verso's situation, since Verso's signal is a head-to-head placement rather than exposure).

### Inferences

Netflix and Spotify are the cleanest public evidence that a major, data-rich company chose to treat a stated score (five stars, or a one-time artist pick) as a small, temporary scaffold rather than a durable signal; Netflix went further and abandoned its finer-grained stated signal (1 to 5 stars) once it had enough revealed behavior not to need it as much. None of the four products discussed here (Netflix, Spotify, Pinterest, TikTok) asks a new user to self-report taste in the abstract (genres, moods, "how adventurous are you"); all four ask for concrete instances (specific artists, specific topics, specific titles), consistent with the academic finding that attribute or category elicitation is the weaker method.

### Gaps

No source in this pass gives a clean, controlled, recommender-systems-specific "stated genre preference predicted outcome X% worse than rated or picked titles" number; the Netflix case and a long-standing marketing-science intuition (self-reported importance weights predict choice worse than revealed trade-offs, a finding going back to conjoint-analysis work by Green and Srinivasan from the 1970s through the 1990s) support the direction but are not themselves a recommender-systems controlled study. Pinterest's and TikTok's statements are product documentation and growth-industry retelling, not formal research, and are labeled as company statements or product observation for that reason, not as research findings.

## 3. How many ratings or picks are enough

### Cited findings

Criticker (company product mechanic, criticker.com, its own "explain" and "get started" pages, accessed 2026): states that a new user needs about 10 rated titles before the site begins finding taste-compatible users and generating recommendations from them.

MovieLens (product history): in the system Rashid et al. studied directly (IUI 2002), the default signup flow presented pages of ten movies until a user rated ten or more, a 10-rating gate as of 2002. By the time Chang, Harper, Terveen published their CSCW 2015 paper, the platform's baseline had moved to a 15-individual-rating gate (confirmed by the paper's own framing of its baseline and independently by the 2023 MovieLens history audit, arXiv 2307.09985), which the groups-of-items interface then replaced.

Netflix (product mechanic, documented across multiple UX teardowns of the live signup flow, current as of 2026): new accounts are asked to "pick 3 that you like" from a short title grid before the home screen personalizes, with the picker stating a minimum of three.

Spotify (company statement and product mechanic, Oskar Stål, Spotify Newsroom, October 13, 2021, corroborated by product teardowns through 2026): new users must pick at least three favorite artists before reaching the home screen.

Pinterest (product mechanic, via Casey Winters's account of designing it and current teardowns through 2026): "follow 5 topics" at signup.

On the marginal value of each added rating, the clearest number found is a negative result from Rashid et al. 2002 (IUI 2002): in their offline test, a Random item-selection strategy reached a mean absolute error with only 8 ratings per user that the Item-Item personalized strategy needed 57 ratings to match, because the personalized strategy's ratings clustered inside one taste neighborhood rather than sampling the user's taste broadly. Sun et al. 2013 (WSDM 2013, read in full) show a related shape: prediction error keeps falling as more questions are asked, but a single-question tree's improvement flattens after about 6 to 7 screens, once each remaining leaf node has too few training users left to learn from.

### Inferences

Every consumer-product gate found here clusters in a narrow band, 3 to 15, and the one case at the high end (MovieLens's old 15) is the one case a later paper (Chang et al. 2015) directly tested lowering, winning on both time and satisfaction; nobody tested raising a gate and found it helped. Verso's own documented thresholds (about 5 ranked items for the content profile, about 10 for neighbors, per `docs/design/recommendation-engine.md`) sit inside this band, closer to Criticker's 10 and the old MovieLens 10 than to the newer, lower consumer pickers (Netflix's 3, Spotify's 3, Pinterest's 5). That gap is plausible because Verso's content profile estimates catalog-feature weights (genre, developer, decade) per user, which likely needs more than 3 data points to avoid being dominated by one item, whereas Netflix's and Spotify's pickers only need to seed a nearest-neighbor-style shelf, a lower bar. The marginal-value evidence (Rashid 2002; Sun et al. 2013) argues that Verso's gates should be about which items are ranked, not only how many: a 5-item content profile built from 5 well-chosen, varied items should beat a 10-item profile built from 10 similar items, reinforcing the seed-selection guidance in section 1.

### Gaps

None of the four consumer-app gates (Criticker 10, old MovieLens 10 to 15, Netflix 3, Spotify 3, Pinterest 5) comes with a published study explaining why that specific number was chosen; they are product facts, not research findings, and are labeled as such. I found no modern (2020 to 2026), Verso-comparable study isolating "marginal accuracy gain per additional rating" as its own measured curve the way Sun et al. 2013 did for questions per screen; the two data points available here (Rashid 2002, Sun et al. 2013) are suggestive, not a clean dose-response curve for ratings specifically.

## 4. Pairwise comparisons as elicitation

### Cited findings

Loepp, Hussein, Ziegler, "Choice-Based Preference Elicitation for Collaborative Filtering Recommender Systems" (paper, CHI 2014): built a system where users repeatedly choose between two presented sets of items, rather than rating anything on a scale, extracting latent taste factors from the choices. Their user study compared this choice-based approach against three alternative interfaces across 24 parameter comparisons and found the choice-based method significantly ahead in 15 of the 24, specifically on item fit (how well the resulting recommendations matched the user), interaction effort, and the user's sense of control.

Sepliarskaia, Kiseleva, Radlinski, de Rijke, "Preference Elicitation as an Optimization Problem" (paper, RecSys 2018, already cited in section 1): their static preference questionnaire poses relative, comparative questions rather than absolute ratings, and the reported result, up to a threefold reduction in questionnaire length for the same accuracy versus the prior state of the art, is specifically attributed to the comparative question format combined with their method for choosing which comparisons to ask.

Flickchart (product mechanic, flickchart.com and its own FAQ; Wikipedia-corroborated founding detail: launched September 9, 2009, by Jeremy Thompson and Nathan Chase): the entire interface is forced binary choice between two films, repeated, with no absolute rating step at all. The product's own stated rationale is that people find it easier and more natural to say which of two films they prefer than to assign either a number. I was not able to find a published, methodologically traceable accuracy comparison of Flickchart's resulting rankings against star-rating baselines; a specific percentage surfaced during this research pass but could not be traced to a citable primary source, so I am not reporting a number for Flickchart's accuracy specifically.

Bradley-Terry models (a statistical framework from the 1950s for converting win and loss pairwise outcomes into a single strength score per item) are the standard way the ranking and recommender-systems literature formalizes "which do you prefer" data. Verso's own engineering notes (`docs/design/recommendation-engine.md`) already considered and rejected fitting a per-user Bradley-Terry model directly on the comparison log, because binary-search insertion means the current top item "never loses," so the fit has no unique answer. That is an internal finding rather than new literature, but it matters here: Verso cannot treat its own comparison log as a free Bradley-Terry dataset, only use the resulting order.

### Inferences

Across both academic results verified numerically here (Loepp et al. 2014's 15-of-24 win rate; Sepliarskaia et al. 2018's threefold reduction), the advantage of comparison-style elicitation over absolute rating is consistent and lands on effort and fit, which is exactly the trade Verso cares about. Verso's ranking loop is already a pairwise comparison interface, so the literature's general endorsement of "which do you prefer" over "rate this" applies to Verso's core mechanic without needing a separate preference-elicitation screen; the open question is which items to compare first (section 1) and when to treat a user as onboarded (section 3), not whether to compare instead of rate.

### Gaps

A specific, citable accuracy number for Flickchart's method against star ratings could not be verified; rather than risk reporting a fabricated statistic, it is omitted here. None of the pairwise-elicitation papers found test the exact shape of Verso's mechanic (binary-search insertion into a pre-existing ordered list across four fixed tiers, with "too close" and "can't compare" escape hatches); the closest analogues (Loepp et al.'s set-choice, Sepliarskaia et al.'s static comparative questionnaire) are unconstrained pairwise or small-set choice, not insertion into a strict order.

## 5. Time, habit, and context questions

### Cited findings

Adomavicius, Sankaranarayanan, Sen, Tuzhilin, "Incorporating Contextual Information in Recommender Systems Using a Multidimensional Approach" (paper, ACM Transactions on Information Systems 23(1), pages 103 to 145, January 2005, read in full): the foundational empirical paper on context-aware recommendation. They built a dedicated movie-rating website and collected 1,755 ratings from 117 college students over 12 months (May 2001 to April 2002), on a 1-to-13 scale, alongside three contextual answers per rated film: Time (weekday or weekend, and whether it was opening weekend), Place (movie theater or at home), and Companion (alone, with friends, with a boyfriend or girlfriend, or with family or others). After dropping light raters, the working dataset was 1,457 ratings from 62 students across 202 movies. A paired t-test found all three contextual dimensions produced a statistically significant difference in ratings. Splitting the data into context-defined segments and fitting collaborative filtering separately per segment beat a single whole-dataset model on 4 of 9 large segments tested, by F-measure: Theater-Weekend (0.641 versus 0.528 for the whole-dataset model), Theater (0.608 versus 0.479), Theater-Friends (0.607 versus 0.504), and Weekend (0.542 versus 0.484). Pooled across a 10-fold cross-validation, their combined approach (using context where it measurably helped, falling back to the plain model elsewhere) raised overall F-measure from 0.463 to 0.526 (about 14% relative) across all predictable ratings, and from 0.450 to 0.545 (about 21% relative) specifically on ratings that fell inside a context segment worth using.

Duolingo: a widely cited case-study compilation of Duolingo's own reported onboarding experiments (growth.design, secondary source, exact original publication date not confirmed in this pass) states that an "Investment Wager" onboarding step, where a new learner commits to a goal before starting, raised Day-7 retention by 14%, with smaller positive effects on Day-1 and Day-14. Separately, Duolingo's own blog (blog.duolingo.com, company statement, exact post date not confirmed in this pass) describes decoupling "streak" credit from hitting the full daily goal; after the change, the share of learners holding a streak of 7 days or longer rose by more than 40%, because under the old rule almost 40% of users who opened the app on two consecutive days still lost their streak for missing the full goal on one of those days.

Session-length-aware recommendation: Latifi, Mauro, Jannach, "Session-aware Recommendation: A Surprising Quest for the State-of-the-Art" (paper, arXiv 2011.03424, posted November 2020) is the most directly relevant source found on this sub-question, and its finding cuts against a more-is-better intuition: simple nearest-neighbor techniques using only the current session consistently matched or beat more complex neural "session-aware" models that also tried to use a user's longer-term history, and the more elaborate session-aware models were frequently no better than approaches that ignore long-term preference entirely. This does not speak to onboarding directly, but it is evidence that session-level signals carry real, immediately usable information, often as much as a bigger model trying to blend it with history.

### Inferences

The Adomavicius et al. 2005 result is the strongest, most directly relevant evidence found for the claim behind "how much time do you have" or "who are you with" questions: context measurably helps, but only on the subset of segments with enough data behind them (4 of 9 tested, each built on at least 274 to 727 ratings in their dataset), and by a moderate amount (13 to 21% relative F-measure), not a transformative one. This argues for context as a refinement layer on top of an already-working recommender rather than a precondition for any recommendation at all. Duolingo's results are about commitment and streak mechanics (a goal you set and are held to), not about using a stated goal as a content-preference signal; they are better evidence that asking for a commitment changes behavior than that a context question improves recommendation accuracy. They are kept in this section because Verso's analogous idea (asking how much free time someone has) is closer in spirit to Duolingo's goal-setting than to a pure content-preference question.

### Gaps

No study found isolates the accuracy or retention effect of asking a context question (time available, who you are with) at onboarding specifically, before any other data exists, as opposed to inferring the same context later from logged behavior; Adomavicius et al. 2005 collected context per rating from an engaged, ongoing panel of students, not as a one-time upfront question from brand-new users, a meaningfully different setting from Verso's questionnaire. The Duolingo numbers come from a secondary growth-case-study compilation rather than a Duolingo research paper or press release read directly; exact dates and sample sizes behind the 14% and 40%-plus figures could not be confirmed, so treat them as directionally reliable but not citation-grade precise.

## 6. Goal or intent questions and routing

### Cited findings

The general growth and product literature on "welcome surveys" (short first-session questions about role, goal, or use case) is extensive but mostly practitioner-authored rather than peer-reviewed. Claims repeated across this literature (for example, that personalizing onboarding by stated role or goal can lift activation by up to 30%, or that 66% of users expect a product to understand their specific needs) trace to product-growth blogs and vendor content without a traceable underlying study, sample size, or methodology. That this claim exists and is widely repeated in growth practice as of 2026 is reported here; the specific percentages are not treated as reliable research findings.

Goal-question evidence is strongest where "what are you trying to do" routes a user to a different setup flow, typically B2B SaaS. Amplitude's "2025 Product Benchmark Report" (benchmark report, 10,600-plus digital products, a further cut across 2,600-plus companies, 2025) is indirectly consistent with this: products in the top decile for Day-1 activation run at roughly 21% against a median of about 5%, a fourfold gap, the kind of gap role or goal-based routing is generally credited with helping close in SaaS onboarding writing, though Amplitude's own report does not isolate goal questions as the specific cause.

### Inferences

A "why are you here" motivational question is a different thing from a "which medium or mode" routing question, and the evidence base for the two differs in kind: routing questions have a plausible mechanism (less irrelevant UI shown) and show up inside benchmark reports as a general driver of day-1 activation, while motivational goal questions are argued for almost entirely in practitioner writing in the sources reachable here, not controlled studies. Verso's reframe already resolves this for the first screen (`docs/private/reframe.md`): the questionnaire asks "which media" first, a routing question, not "why are you here," a motivational question that is unproven either way for a media recommender.

### Gaps

This is the weakest-evidenced section of the nine. No peer-reviewed study, in recommender systems or adjacent HCI venues, isolating the effect of an onboarding goal or intent question on later recommendation quality or retention for a media or entertainment product specifically, was found. Everything found was either B2B SaaS practitioner writing or general growth-blog content repeating the same few uncitable statistics.

## 7. The cost side: onboarding completion benchmarks, 2024 to 2026

### Cited findings

RevenueCat, "State of Subscription Apps 2026" (benchmark report; a secondary summary describes the underlying base as roughly 115,000 mobile apps and about 16 billion USD in tracked revenue, 2026): hard paywalls (value gated before free use) convert at a median of 10.7%, against 2.1% for freemium (value shown first, payment later); the best 10% of hard-paywall apps hit 38.7% by day 35, and even the worst quartile (4.2%) is double the freemium median. Trial length moves the same direction as "get to value fast": trials of 17 to 32 days convert at a median 42.5%, versus 25.5% for trials under 4 days, and roughly half of all paid conversions, and 80 to 89% of trial starts, happen on day 0.

Amplitude, "2025 Product Benchmark Report" (benchmark report, 10,600-plus digital products, a further cut across 2,600-plus companies, 2025): across the median product, 98% of new users go inactive within two weeks of their first session; the top 10% of products get roughly four times the Day-1 activation of the median (about 21% versus about 5%); reaching at least 7% Day-7 return puts a product in the top quartile for activation; and 69% of products that reach the top quartile on 7-day activation also reach the top quartile on 3-month retention, meaning the earliest days predict the outcome months out.

UXCam, mobile app onboarding and retention benchmarks (benchmark report, described as drawing on 37,000-plus apps, 2026): "healthy" activation rates run 30 to 50% depending on category; unregulated consumer apps complete 70 to 85% of first-session flows, against 40 to 60% for flows in regulated industries; the industry-average drop from the first onboarding step to the second is about 38%; and day-1 completion of one meaningful first action is the single most predictive metric UXCam cites for Day-30 retention, with apps that nail it retaining at 2 to 3 times the rate of apps that do not.

Form length and friction, a narrower but relevant literature: a widely repeated growth-marketing characterization of the Baymard Institute's long-running ecommerce checkout usability research (Baymard studies checkout flows specifically, not in-app onboarding questionnaires, a scope difference worth flagging) states that 26% of users who abandon a checkout cite length or complexity as the reason, and that the number of fields asked for matters more to completion than the number of separate steps those fields are split across. This exact figure could not be independently confirmed on Baymard's own site in this pass, so it is reported here as a secondary characterization of Baymard's work, not as something read on baymard.com directly.

### Inferences

Every benchmark report above agrees on the same shape regardless of category (subscription apps, general digital products, or ecommerce checkout): completion drops with each added step or field, and the earliest session (day 0 to day 1) disproportionately determines the outcome months later. None measures a recommendation questionnaire specifically, so these numbers should be read as the general cost of "screens before value," which Verso's questionnaire-then-deck design is itself an instance of, not as a number that transfers exactly. The "number of fields matters more than number of steps" finding (Baymard, as characterized above) is a direct, practical argument for Verso's questionnaire being a small number of concrete choices (which media, then seed comparisons) rather than a longer form split across several short screens. RevenueCat's finding that trial length and day-0 behavior dominate paid conversion is adjacent to, but not the same question as, cold-start questionnaire design; it is included because `docs/private/reframe.md`'s own lesson 8 (the trial has to land a pick in session one) is already built on this kind of data, so this note confirms rather than re-litigates it.

### Gaps

None of the three benchmark reports (RevenueCat, Amplitude, UXCam) publish a step-by-step drop-off curve specific to a "pick your taste" or recommendation-onboarding questionnaire; their numbers are aggregated across all onboarding types (forms, tutorials, permission prompts, paywalls) within a given app category, so the specific marginal cost of, say, a sixth comparison screen versus a fifth is not something these reports can answer. Pendo's, Appcues's, or Mixpanel's own 2024-to-2026 benchmark reports could not be reached directly in this pass (searches surfaced comparison and marketing pages about the tools rather than the reports' own data pages); where a specific number attributed to one of them surfaced only through an unverifiable search synthesis, it has been excluded rather than repeated here.

## 8. Demographics

### Cited findings

Pazzani, "A Framework for Collaborative, Content-Based and Demographic Filtering" (paper, Artificial Intelligence Review, 1999): established demographic filtering (recommending based on age, gender, occupation, and similar attributes) as one of three classic recommendation families, alongside collaborative and content-based filtering. Per how later papers characterize its comparative result (Pazzani's own tables were not obtained directly in this pass), demographic-only filtering underperformed both plain collaborative filtering and content-based filtering on their own, and became competitive only when blended with one of the other two methods.

A cluster of more recent papers (2014 to 2024, found via ResearchGate and IJECE listings, including "On the Enhancement of Collaborative Filtering by Demographic Data," "Demographic Information Combined with Collaborative Filtering for an Efficient Recommendation System," and "Enhancing Movie Recommendations: A Demographic-Integrated Cosine-KNN Collaborative Filtering Approach") converge on a consistent, modest finding: adding age, gender, or occupation-style demographic data to a collaborative filter can improve accuracy "to a limited degree," mainly as a sparsity patch (helping when a user or item has very few ratings) rather than as a strong signal on its own; none reports demographic data alone beating item-based or user-based collaborative filtering outright. These were read at the abstract level; several sit behind paywalls not reachable in this pass.

### Inferences

The pattern holds across 25 years (Pazzani 1999 to papers from 2024): demographic data is a weak, situational signal for media taste specifically, strongest exactly when there is little else to go on (a brand-new item, a brand-new user) and fading in value as soon as any behavioral or revealed-preference signal exists, the same shape as Verso's own layered design, where demographics never appear as a layer in `docs/design/recommendation-engine.md`. This is consistent with the task's own framing: the evidence genuinely says demographics add little to media recommendation accuracy, and their larger, well-documented value is in advertising targeting, a use case Verso has already ruled out for its free tier (`docs/private/reframe.md`'s "never sold, free or paid" stance and its aggregate-report-only monetization path).

### Gaps

A single, authoritative, modern (post-2015) large-scale study cleanly isolating the marginal accuracy contribution of demographics in a production-scale media recommender (Netflix- or Spotify-scale) could not be obtained; the literature reachable here is mostly smaller academic datasets (MovieLens-scale or similar), so the "little effect" finding, while consistent, is not tested at Verso's eventual target scale.

## 9. Group and household context

### Cited findings

PolyLens (already established in Verso's own research, `docs/design/recommendation-engine.md`, citing a 2001 deployed study on MovieLens): of 338 groups formed, 257 (about 76%) were pairs, and 94% of users wanted to see their own individual predicted score alongside the group's recommendation, not just a single blended number. This is cited here as already-settled context per the project's own convention (research docs are cited, not re-derived) rather than re-researched.

Netflix's household architecture (product design, inferred from Netflix's own profile system and widely discussed in product-analysis writing rather than a single academic paper): Netflix's response to the household problem has been per-person profiles, not a household-level model, treating a parent and a teenager on the same account as two independent taste models rather than inferring or asking about their relationship. Netflix's own public position around its 2023 account-sharing changes (company statement) frames shared profiles (as opposed to shared accounts with separate profiles) as the harder, unsolved case, since a profile used by more than one person mixes signal with no way to separate it after the fact.

Academic work on the harder case, a single login shared by multiple real people with no separate profiles: "Top-N Recommendation for Shared Accounts" (paper, RecSys 2015) and "Guess Who Rated This Movie: Identifying Users Through Subspace Clustering" (paper, Zhang et al., UAI 2012, also posted as arXiv 1208.1544) both start from the premise that the system must first statistically disentangle which family member is likely behind a given action, by clustering behavior or by subspace methods on the rating matrix, before it can personalize; neither approach asks the account holder to self-report who is in the household or who is watching right now, because self-report does not solve the harder problem of attributing a single action to a person in real time.

### Inferences

The consistent pattern across both the product evidence (Netflix) and the academic evidence (shared-account papers) is that household and companion structure is inferred from behavior, never asked for directly as a one-time onboarding fact, because a static self-report ("I share this account with my partner") does not tell the system which of the two people is behind tonight's session; it needs session-level or clustering-level inference either way. This maps cleanly onto Verso's existing design: `docs/design/recommendation-engine.md`'s group engine already builds a session (who is present, right now) rather than a stored household fact, and shrinks a non-member guest's prediction toward the item's prior rather than trying to model them from a profile question; this research supports that choice directly rather than suggesting a change.

### Gaps

No study found measures whether asking a new user, at onboarding, "do you usually watch with a partner or family" and using the answer as a standing preference weight (as opposed to a per-session fact) changes recommendation quality or retention; every source found either infers companionship from behavior (Adomavicius et al. 2005, which asks per rating, not once at signup) or treats it as a per-session state (Verso's own group design; PolyLens).

## Guidance for Verso

1. Make the questionnaire's only taste questions a media picker (routing) plus enough seed comparisons to cross the content-profile gate (about five ranked items), not a genre or mood checklist. Evidence: Rashid et al. (IUI 2002) call category or demographic models "less general" than revealed-preference methods, and every high-scale product that still asks a stated question (Netflix's 3-title pick, Spotify's 3-artist pick, Pinterest's 5-topic follow, all company mechanics current through 2026) asks for concrete instances, never abstract categories.

2. Seed the comparison deck the way Rashid et al.'s best strategy worked: popular enough to have probably been played or watched, varied enough that opinions differ, not pure popularity and not pure novelty. Evidence: pure-entropy selection was "shockingly poor" and nearly unusable in their test (IUI 2002), while Popularity times Entropy gave the best accuracy for a given amount of user effort.

3. Treat five ranked items as a real threshold and expect the first few comparisons to carry the most weight, with seed variety mattering as much as seed count. Evidence: Rashid et al. (IUI 2002, an 8-rating random run matched a 57-rating personalized run) and Sun et al. (WSDM 2013, RMSE gains flatten after 6 to 7 screens) both show per-item accuracy gains front-loaded and prone to flattening or reversing when the seed set is narrow.

4. Do not add a separate "rate this" or slider step anywhere in onboarding. Evidence: Verso's head-to-head comparisons already are the elicitation method the literature increasingly favors over absolute ratings (Loepp, Hussein, Ziegler, CHI 2014, winning on effort and fit in 15 of 24 tested comparisons; Sepliarskaia et al., RecSys 2018, cutting required questions up to threefold by asking relative rather than absolute questions).

5. Present the five-to-ten item range as one continuous deck rather than two visible gates. Evidence: Chang, Harper, Terveen (CSCW 2015) found that restructuring MovieLens's old flat 15-rating requirement, without changing what was ultimately learned, cut completion time by more than half and raised satisfaction; a visible second "unlock" risks reintroducing the same friction in a new place.

6. Hold context questions (time available, who is watching) out of onboarding and derive them from product surfaces instead, as the movies Home finder already plans to. Evidence: Adomavicius, Sankaranarayanan, Sen, Tuzhilin (ACM TOIS, 2005) found context raised accuracy only on dense, logged segments (4 of 9 tested, each built on hundreds of real context-tagged ratings), not from a one-time stated answer collected before any behavior exists.

7. Never ask a household or "who do you watch with" question at signup; infer it per session instead, as the group-session design already does. Evidence: both the shared-account literature (RecSys 2015; Zhang et al., UAI 2012) and Netflix's own per-profile, not per-household, architecture solve this by inference from behavior, because a standing answer cannot say who is actually present tonight.

8. Skip demographic questions (age, gender, location) for the recommendation engine entirely; collect them, if ever, only where a specific non-recommendation need justifies it, such as regional content availability, which is catalog metadata rather than a user attribute. Evidence: 25 years of literature from Pazzani (1999) through 2024-era papers consistently finds demographic signal weak and situational for media taste, and Verso's monetization stance already rules out the one place demographics reliably pay off, advertising (`docs/private/reframe.md`).

9. If a goal or intent question is used at all, limit it to the media router already planned ("which of these do you want to track") and do not add a separate motivational "why are you here" screen. Evidence: goal-question lift is argued for routing contexts in B2B SaaS practitioner writing, but no study of it for an entertainment or media recommender specifically was found; Verso's media picker already is the evidenced version of this question.

10. Budget the whole questionnaire-plus-seed-deck as one timed unit and protect day-0 completion above all else. Evidence: 2025-to-2026 benchmark reports agree that activation measured in the first session predicts outcomes months out regardless of category (Amplitude 2025, a fourfold gap between top-decile and median Day-1 activation; RevenueCat 2026, about half of paid conversions and 80 to 89% of trial starts happen on day 0; UXCam 2026, apps completing one meaningful first action retain at 2 to 3 times the rate of those that do not), the same evidence already behind `docs/private/reframe.md`'s lesson that the first session must produce one convincing, explained pick before anything else is asked of the user.
