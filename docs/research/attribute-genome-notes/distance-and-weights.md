# Distance and weights: the math of "the closest next item" for a ranked-list recommender

Research notes for VER-303 (attribute genome), written 2026-10-29. Read beside [docs/design/recommendation-engine.md](../../design/recommendation-engine.md), which these notes take as given: layer 0 community prior, layer 1 content profile (idf-weighted binary features scored by cosine), neighbors later, `recommendation_loo` and `prediction_calibration` as the two instruments. Every external claim carries a URL and was read on 2026-10-29 unless another date is given. Worked numbers, derivations and simulations are my own and are marked **[own]**; the scripts are pure Python (no numpy on this machine) and every number is reproducible from the description given beside it.

## Distance measures: which one, when, and does 20 to 60 attributes concentrate?

### Takeaway
Pandora's own patent uses a weighted squared Euclidean distance on graded 0-to-5 genes, which is the right family for graded intensity attributes; cosine is right for Verso's current binary, idf-weighted tags, because it cancels how many tags an item happens to carry. Scale (standardize or keep one common 0-to-5 scale), collapse or decorrelate redundant attributes, and keep the attribute set to a few dozen reliable ones: at 20 to 60 attributes concentration is moderate, and per-attribute scoring noise compresses distances more than the dimension count does.

### Cited Findings
- **Pandora's distance, from its patent.** US 7,003,515 B1, "Consumer item matching method and system" (Glaser, Westergren, Stearns, Kraft; filed 2002-05-16, priority 2001-05-16, granted 2006-02-21, now expired) represents a song as an n-dimensional vector of "genes", each "a number between 0 and 5", with fractional values "limited to half integers"; it states about 150 genes per song in one place and 300 to 500 for some genres (rap 350, jazz about 400), and claim 7 requires at least 100. The match is the Euclidean distance with the square root dropped (sum of squared differences), each squared term multiplied by a per-gene weight w_i, with an optional per-gene scaling function f before differencing and an optional general g(|difference|) (x squared, with x cubed suggested). The closest matches are returned as "an adjustable number of closest matches" ranked by distance (claim 6). — [Google Patents, US7003515B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en)
- **Pandora's multi-song form is an inverse-variance (diagonal Mahalanobis) weight.** For a set of songs the patent builds a center vector and a per-gene deviation, and weights each gene by the inverse squared standard deviation, regularized as w_i · 0.25 / (σ_i² + 0.25) so a zero deviation does not give infinite weight; the 0.25 constant "may be modified". — [Google Patents, US7003515B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en)
- **Pandora's weights.** Initial weights come from empirical work by the music team; "focus traits" (the distinguishing aspects of a song, fired by rules such as a gene over a threshold) let a user focus a match, after which "the matching engine modifies its weighting vector"; the patent describes personal weight adjustment from user preference input and user-selected weighting factors (claims 12, 15, 17, 21) but does not describe thumbs up or down. — [Google Patents, US7003515B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en)
- **Pandora's attribute count is reported inconsistently.** Press and analyst pieces give "over 450" or "close to 400" attributes; the patent says about 150 per song and 300 to 500 for some genres. The exact production weights and formula are not public. — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-machine-learning-is-reinventing-the-way-we-discover-music); [Neil Patel on Pandora](https://neilpatel.com/blog/how-pandora-uses-data/); [Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en)
- **Concentration, the original result.** Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan and Shaft (ICDT 1999, LNCS 1540, pp. 217-235) show that under conditions much broader than independent identically distributed dimensions, the nearest point's distance approaches the farthest point's as dimensionality grows, and that the effect "can occur for as few as 10-15 dimensions"; they stress this does not mean high-dimensional NN is never meaningful (the paper has sections on classification and on data of "implicitly low dimensionality"), and that most proposed indexes were never compared with a linear scan, which often wins at 10 to 15 dimensions. — [Beyer et al., PDF copy at LORIA](https://members.loria.fr/MOBerger/Enseignement/AVR/Exposes/beyer.pdf); [UW-Madison TR1377 record](https://minds.wisc.edu/handle/1793/60174)
- **Concentration depends on the norm.** Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim (ICDT 2001, LNCS 1973, pp. 420-434) show the relative contrast of L_k distances depends heavily on k and worsens faster for larger k, so the Manhattan metric "is consistently more preferable" than Euclidean for high-dimensional data, and fractional metrics (k below 1) preserve contrast better still and improve k-means. — [Aggarwal, Hinneburg, Keim, PDF](https://www.haralick.org/ML/surprise_in_high_dimensions.pdf); [KOPS record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/da11ec73-e8e1-43d1-96fe-0d0359996a6c)
- **The textbook statement of the Euclidean-versus-cosine trade-off.** Pazzani and Billsus ("Content-Based Recommendation Systems", The Adaptive Web, LNCS 4321, 2007, pp. 325-341) note that nearest-neighbor content recommenders usually use Euclidean distance for structured data and cosine for the vector-space (tag or text) model; in Euclidean distance a feature small in both items counts the same as one large in both, while cosine is not large when both are small, which is why cosine suits topics ("similar when they are about the same topic"). — [Pazzani and Billsus 2007, PDF](https://cs.fit.edu/~pkc/apweb/related/pazzani07aw.pdf); [DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-72079-9_10](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-72079-9_10)
- **pgvector supports all of the candidate measures natively**: `<->` L2, `<=>` cosine distance, `<#>` negative inner product, `<+>` L1, plus `<~>` Hamming and `<%>` Jaccard for bit vectors; the README adds that for normalized vectors inner product performs best. — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)

### Inferences
- **[own] Worked example 1, graded attributes, where Euclidean and cosine disagree.** Four attributes on Pandora's 0-to-5 scale: difficulty, combat intensity, coziness, story weight. Seed: a brutal soulslike, S = (5, 5, 0, 2). Candidates: X, another demanding action game, (4, 4, 1, 2); Y, the same proportions at half strength, (2.5, 2.5, 0, 1); Z, a cozy farming sim, (0, 0, 5, 2).

  | Candidate | Euclidean | Manhattan | Cosine | Cosine after centering on the catalog mean (2.5 each) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | X demanding action | **1.73** | **3.0** | 0.984 | **0.997** |
  | Y half-strength copy | 3.67 | 6.0 | **1.000** | 0.551 |
  | Z cozy sim | 8.66 | 15.0 | 0.101 | -0.974 |

  Raw cosine calls Y a perfect match because it measures only direction; on an intensity scale, Y is a game half as hard and half as violent, which is exactly what a soulslike fan does not want. Euclidean and Manhattan pick X, and so does cosine once each attribute is centered on the catalog mean (which is the Pearson-style "centered cosine"). Rule for the design: on graded attributes, use (weighted) Euclidean or L1, or cosine only on centered attributes; never raw cosine.
- **[own] Worked example 2, binary tags, where Euclidean is the one that misleads.** Twelve binary tags. Seed with six tags. P is sparsely tagged: two tags, both shared with the seed. R is richly tagged: ten tags, five shared with the seed, five not. Euclidean: P 2.00, R 2.45, so Euclidean prefers P. Cosine: P 0.577, R 0.645; Jaccard: P 0.333, R 0.455; both prefer R. R shares five of the seed's six traits, so R is what the user wants; Euclidean on binary vectors counts every tag the candidate has and the seed lacks as a mismatch, which rewards thinly catalogued items and punishes well-catalogued ones. This is why the existing layer 1's choice of cosine on binary idf features is correct and should stay for the tag part.
- **[own] Scaling.** Euclidean sums squared differences in raw units, so an attribute on a 0-to-100 scale (Metacritic) or in hours (time to beat) swamps a 0-to-5 gene: a 10-point Metacritic gap contributes 100 to the squared distance, a full 0-to-5 swing on coziness contributes 25. Either score every genome attribute on one common anchored scale (Pandora's 0 to 5 in half steps) or z-score each attribute over the catalog before any distance; keep runtime, year and community score out of the distance and use them as filters or separate terms.
- **[own] Worked example 3, correlated attributes double-count.** Three near-redundant attributes (difficulty, punishing death penalty, combat intensity) plus coziness. Seed (5, 5, 5, 0). A is one point less hard on all three, (4, 4, 4, 0): Euclidean 1.73. B is one point cozier, (5, 5, 5, 1): Euclidean 1.00. "Hardness" counts √3 times as much as coziness only because it was written three times; collapsed to one hardness attribute both are 1.00. With two standardized attributes correlated at r = 0.9, Mahalanobis distance d² = Δᵀ Σ⁻¹ Δ gives: a step of (1, 1) along the correlation 1.03 (Euclidean 1.41); a step of (1, −1) against it 4.47 (Euclidean 1.41); a step of (1, 0) 2.29. Mahalanobis treats "both move together" as one unit of change and an unusual combination as a large one. Whitening (multiply by Σ^(−1/2), or PCA then divide each component by its standard deviation) turns plain Euclidean into Mahalanobis, so a whitened vector can sit in pgvector and use `<->` directly.
- **[own] Caution on full whitening.** With 40 to 60 attributes and only the catalog to estimate Σ, full whitening divides by the smallest eigenvalues, which magnifies noisy, rarely varying attributes. A safer default is a shrunk covariance (Σ + δI) or keeping the top k principal components, or simply designing the attribute list without near-duplicates. Pandora's own fix for one user's set of songs was the diagonal case with an additive regularizer (σ² + 0.25), which is the same idea.
- **[own] Graded versus binary, the design consequence.** The current binary features (genre, developer or director, decade, platform) and a graded genome answer different questions: the tags say what kind of thing it is, the genome says how much of each quality it has. They should be two terms with their own measure (cosine on idf tags, weighted Euclidean or L1 on standardized genes), blended by weights the harness fits, rather than concatenated into one vector where one measure is wrong for half the coordinates.
- **[own] Simulation: how much concentration at 20 to 60 attributes.** N = 3,000 items, 15 random queries, attributes iid uniform on [0, 1]. Nearest distance as a share of the median distance (lower is better contrast), L2 and L1:

  | d | 2 | 5 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 60 | 100 | 300 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | L2 nearest / median | 0.02 | 0.17 | 0.36 | 0.53 | 0.63 | 0.67 | 0.73 | 0.80 | 0.88 |
  | L1 nearest / median | 0.02 | 0.16 | 0.33 | 0.50 | 0.60 | 0.64 | 0.70 | 0.77 | 0.86 |
  | L2 relative contrast (max − min) / min | 124 | 12.2 | 3.37 | 1.64 | 1.10 | 0.94 | 0.70 | 0.49 | 0.25 |

  Under the worst case (independent attributes), 60 attributes put the nearest of 3,000 items at 73% of the typical distance: neighbors still exist but the gap is thin, and L1 is slightly better than L2, as Aggarwal and others predict. Real attributes are not independent. With 60 attributes generated from k latent factors plus 30% attribute-specific noise, nearest / median is 0.51 at k = 3, 0.54 at k = 6, 0.59 at k = 12, and 0.69 at k = 60 (L2 relative contrast 3.5, 2.5, 1.7, 1.0). Holding k = 6 and varying the attribute-specific noise share: 0.22 at 0% noise, 0.38 at 10%, 0.53 at 30%, 0.61 at 50%. So the intrinsic dimension and, even more, the per-attribute scoring noise decide contrast; the raw count of 20 to 60 attributes is safe if the attributes are correlated by design (they will be: hardness, pace, tone cluster) and scored reliably. Noisy scores (one LLM pass, one human tagger) add an irreducible floor to every distance; averaging two or three independent scorings per attribute, or projecting onto the top principal components, buys more contrast than dropping attributes.
- **[own] Relevance for Verso's catalog size.** With about 70,000 games the nearest neighbor sits closer than in the 3,000-item simulation, but the recommender never searches the whole catalog for one seed: it scores a filtered pool of hundreds to a few thousand, where the contrast numbers above apply roughly as computed.

### Gaps
- The exact production distance and weights of Pandora's Music Genome Project are not public; everything above is the 2002 patent, which may not match what Pandora ran later. No primary source found on how many genes Pandora uses today.
- I did not find a published study measuring concentration on a real hand-scored attribute genome (Pandora-style) as opposed to image or text features.

## From one seed to one person: centroid, Rocchio, item-kNN, prototypes

### Takeaway
Pandora's station starts from one seed, so "closest to the seed" is well defined; a person's ranked list is many seeds and is often multimodal, and any single summary vector (a centroid, a Rocchio query, or the current layer 1's weighted linear profile) can land between the modes or cancel them out. Score candidates against the user's own top-tier items (max or top-k mean similarity) or against a few prototypes (clusters of loved items, represented by a real item), and keep the single linear profile as one term rather than the whole model.

### Cited Findings
- **Pandora's patent already handles a multi-song taste.** For a set of songs it builds "vector pairs" (a center and a per-gene deviation), and for a taste with several clusters it keeps two or more vector pairs so that a match fits one cluster instead of the average between clusters (paraphrase); when returning results for multi-cluster groups "we can choose to return a few from each cluster according to cluster size" (claims 13 and 17 cover vector pairs built from user-selected or preference-cluster songs). — [Google Patents, US7003515B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7003515B1/en)
- **Rocchio.** The relevance-feedback query is Q' = α·Q + β·(centroid of relevant items) − γ·(centroid of non-relevant items); traditional values are about α = 1, β = 0.8, γ = 0.1, and many modern systems set γ = 0 or use only the "strongest" non-relevant items; the article notes it "often fails to classify multimodal classes". — [Wikipedia, Rocchio algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocchio_algorithm)
- **Rocchio has no theoretical guarantee.** Pazzani and Billsus: the formula moves the query toward relevant clusters and away from irrelevant ones, but "there is no theoretically motivated basis" for it and neither performance nor convergence is guaranteed; the Rocchio-based classifier (one prototype per class) has the same lack of guarantees. Nearest-neighbor content recommenders instead store every rated item and score a new item from its k nearest rated items. — [Pazzani and Billsus 2007, PDF](https://cs.fit.edu/~pkc/apweb/related/pazzani07aw.pdf)
- **PinnerSage (Pinterest, KDD 2020) is the deployed case against one user vector.** Its abstract says a single embedding "falls short" of a user's interests; its Figure 2 shows a user interested in painting, shoes and sci-fi whose merged embedding lands nearest a pin about "energy boosting breakfast". PinnerSage clusters each user's actions with Ward hierarchical clustering, with no fixed cluster count (a light user gets 3 to 5 clusters, a heavy user 75 to 100), represents each cluster by a medoid (a real pin) rather than a centroid because a centroid is sensitive to outliers and costs a stored embedding, and reports offline and online A/B gains over single-embedding methods. — [Pal et al. 2020, arXiv 2007.03634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03634)
- **PinnerSage retrieves from a sample of clusters, not all.** Production samples 3 medoids in proportion to a time-decayed importance score and retrieves nearest neighbors for each, because retrieving from every cluster is too costly and would overwhelm the user. — [Pinterest Engineering blog via search summary](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/pinnersage-multi-modal-user-embedding-framework-for-recommendations-at-pinterest-bfd116b49475); [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.03634) (the blog itself returned 403 to a direct fetch; the 3-medoid detail is from the search summary and the paper)
- **MIND (Alibaba, CIKM 2019)** represents each user with several vectors from a capsule-routing "multi-interest extractor" over the behavior history and was deployed on the Mobile Tmall homepage. — [Li et al. 2019, arXiv 1904.08030](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08030)

### Inferences
- **[own] Worked example 4, a two-cluster user.** Attributes (difficulty, combat intensity, coziness, exploration), 0 to 5. Loved (score 9): three cozy sims (1, 0, 5, 3), (0, 0, 5, 2), (1, 0, 5, 3) and three soulslikes (5, 5, 0, 4), (5, 5, 0, 5), (5, 5, 0, 2). Disliked (score 3): two middling licensed action-adventures (3, 3, 2, 3) and (2, 3, 1, 2). User mean 7.5. The centroid of the loved items is (2.83, 2.5, 2.5, 3.17). Four candidates, scored five ways (distance: lower is better; the others: higher is better):

  | Candidate | Distance to centroid | Linear profile, Verso-style (cosine) | Item-kNN, distance to nearest loved item | Two prototypes, distance to nearest | Rocchio, β = 1, γ = 0.5 (cosine) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | C1 middling action-adventure (3, 3, 2, 3) | **0.75 (best)** | −0.19 | 3.61 | 3.53 | 0.13 |
  | C2 cozy sim (1, 0, 5, 2) | 4.15 | 0.56 | **1.00** | 0.75 | 0.29 |
  | C3 soulslike (5, 5, 0, 3) | 4.15 | **−0.44 (worst)** | **1.00** | **0.67** | **−0.17 (worst)** |
  | C4 "cozy soulslike" hybrid (4, 3, 3, 4) | 1.60 | **0.58 (best)** | 3.74 | 3.76 | **0.78 (best)** |

  The centroid recommends the very kind of game the user disliked. The linear profile (weights = Σ (score − mean) × centered attributes, which is how layer 1 builds its profile from features) comes out as (3.0, −4.5, 9.0, 6.0): the two loved clusters point in opposite directions on difficulty, combat and coziness and largely cancel, so the profile is decided by whatever the two disliked games happen to separate, here "low coziness", and it scores the soulslike below the disliked middling game. Rocchio does the same. Both rank a hybrid that matches neither cluster first. Item-kNN and two prototypes put both true matches on top and the middling game near the bottom. The existing binary-feature profile escapes the worst of this only because binary tags such as `genre:simulator` and `genre:souls-like` do not cancel (they are separate coordinates, not two ends of one axis); a graded genome makes cancellation the default, because a graded axis has two ends.
- **[own] What to use for Verso.** Score each candidate by its similarity to the user's top-tier items: s(c) = max over loved items i of sim(c, i), or the mean of the top 2 or 3 similarities, weighted by how high i sits in the list; this is the content analogue of item-kNN and is also the most legible reason ("closest to Hollow Knight, which you put in Loved"). Prototypes are the cheaper version once a user has dozens of loved items: cluster the loved tier (Ward or k-medoids, with k chosen by a gap in merge distance, typically 1 to 4 for a list of 50), represent each cluster by its medoid, a real game, and allocate the three picks across prototypes in proportion to cluster size, as Pandora's patent and PinnerSage both do.
- **[own] Negatives.** Dropped and bottom-tier items should push away, but only locally: a penalty for being close to a disliked item (s(c) − γ · max over disliked j of sim(c, j)) rather than a subtracted global centroid, which in the example above flips the sign of a whole loved cluster. Start γ near 0.3 to 0.5 and let the harness set it.
- **[own] One seed still has a place.** "More like this one" from an item page is Pandora's original case; there the seed is the query, the user's learned weights change the metric, and the four distance rules from the first section apply unchanged.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures centroid against max-similarity against prototypes for content-based scoring on games or films specifically; the evidence is the patent, PinnerSage (pins, learned embeddings) and MIND (e-commerce behavior), plus the worked example.
- PinnerSage's exact offline and online percentage gains were not extracted (only the direction of the result).

## Learning attribute weights from one ranked list

### Takeaway
The design doc's objection to per-user Bradley-Terry on items does not carry over to a handful of attribute weights: once the list has more items than weights, an order that is consistent by construction is still not reproducible by a linear function of a few attributes, so the fit is finite, and any prior makes it unique. Fit the weights with a pairwise (or Plackett-Luce) logistic model on attribute differences, shrunk toward population weights; in an own simulation, an unshrunk per-user fit needs about 2 to 3 ranked items per weight to match the population prior, while the shrunk fit is never worse than the prior and recovers about half of the available gain by about 3 to 5 items per weight.

### Cited Findings
- **Pairwise learning to rank on feature differences.** Joachims (KDD 2002, pp. 133-142) learned a linear ranking function from clickthrough preference pairs with an SVM (Ranking SVM), reducing ranking to classifying ordered pairs, which minimizes an approximation of discordant pairs (exact minimization is NP-hard). — [Joachims 2002, summary at The Morning Paper](https://blog.acolyer.org/2015/08/27/optimizing-search-engines-using-clickthrough-data/); [Ranking SVM, Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Ranking_SVM); [Cornell test-of-time award note](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/node/1521)
- **RankNet** (Burges and others, ICML 2005, pp. 89-96) uses a logistic map from the score difference to a pairwise probability and a cross-entropy cost summed over pairs, implemented with a neural network; it cites Herbrich and others' large-margin rank boundaries as the ordinal-regression precedent. — [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/learning-to-rank-using-gradient-descent); [PDF mirror](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/IRcourse/6_ML/other_notes/rankNET.pdf)
- **Plackett-Luce with covariates is the rank-ordered (exploded) logit.** Beggs, Cardell and Hausman (1981) and Hausman and Ruud (1987) in economics, and Punj and Staelin (1978) and Chapman and Staelin (1982) in marketing, model a full ranking as a sequence of first choices among the items not yet chosen, with utilities linear in covariates; Stata fits it as `rologit` / `cmrologit`. — [Stata manual, rologit](https://www.stata.com/manuals15/rrologit.pdf); [Allison and Christakis, "Logit Models for Sets of Ranked Items"](https://humannaturelab.net/sites/default/files/pdf/paper/017-Logit-Models-for-Sets-of-Ranked-Items.pdf)
- **Ties.** Rao and Kupper (JASA 1967) add a threshold below which a judge cannot tell two items apart (the model reduces to Bradley-Terry when the threshold is zero); Davidson (JASA 1970) keeps Luce's choice axiom and makes the tie probability proportional to the geometric mean of the two win probabilities, with one extra parameter for how common ties are. — [Encyclopedia of Mathematics, paired comparison model](https://encyclopediaofmath.org/wiki/Paired_comparison_model); [arXiv 2409.17431, which restates both](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.17431)
- **Hierarchical (shrunk) per-person weights are standard in conjoint analysis.** Lenk, DeSarbo, Green and Young (Marketing Science 15(2), 1996) show hierarchical Bayes recovers individual part-worths "even when individual-level least squares estimators do not exist", that is with fewer profiles per person than attributes, and that accuracy can be held while profiles per person fall if more people are added. — [Lenk et al. 1996, INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.15.2.173); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v15y1996i2p173-191.html)
- **Practitioner rule for tasks per person.** Sawtooth Software says that with CBC/HB at least six choice tasks give good simulated shares but about 10 or more are needed for robust individual-level predictions, and 8 to 15 for paper or interviewer studies; a Sawtooth 2016 paper notes priors matter little when tasks are many and parameters few. — [Sawtooth Lighthouse Studio, number of attributes, levels and tasks](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/cbc-number-of-attributes-levels-tasks.html); [Sawtooth CBC/HB](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/advanced-analytical-tools/cbc-hierarchical-bayes)
- **Preference learning across users with features.** Chu and Ghahramani (ICML 2005) give a Gaussian-process likelihood for pairwise preferences; Bonilla, Guo and Sanner (NIPS 2010) put a GP over utilities on the joint space of user and item features and learn its hyperparameters from previous users to cut the elicitation burden for a new user; Houlsby and others (NIPS 2012) extend the same likelihood to multi-user preference learning. — [Chu and Ghahramani 2005, PDF](https://mlg.eng.cam.ac.uk/zoubin/papers/icml05chuwei-pl.pdf); [Bonilla et al. 2010, PDF](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2010/file/46922a0880a8f11f8f69cbb52b1396be-Paper.pdf); [Houlsby et al. 2012, PDF](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/afdec7005cc9f14302cd0474fd0f3c96-Paper.pdf)
- **A RecSys precedent for regression on content features with a pairwise loss.** Park and Chu (RecSys 2009) fit feature-based regression (user demographics times item content) by minimizing a pairwise preference loss, for cold start, on MovieLens and EachMovie. — [Park and Chu 2009, PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/pairwise-preference-regression-for-cold-start-recommendation-2nsdthmf3a.pdf)
- **"Can't compare": the literature treats incomparability as its own outcome.** Klukowski (2014) models each pair as ordered or incomparable when estimating partial orders from noisy comparisons; Destercke and Denoeux (Soft Computing 2014) represent pairwise information about partial orders with belief functions; Gilbert and others (2022) learn multiattribute utilities "cautiously", abstaining when the simplest consistent models disagree. — [Klukowski 2014](https://yadda.icm.edu.pl/baztech/element/bwmeta1.element.baztech-1da4f11b-9fa9-4db0-88e7-c61bd7bdc077/c/klukowski_4_2014.pdf); [Destercke and Denoeux 2014](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00500-014-1553-9.pdf); [Gilbert et al., arXiv 2206.07341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07341)
- **Comparisons are harder and noisier when options trade off.** Tversky and Russo (J. Math. Psych. 1969) reject the independence principle of binary choice in favor of a similarity hypothesis; Shubatt and Yang ("Tradeoffs and Comparison Complexity", arXiv 2401.17578, v6 2026-10-20) model comparisons as hard when they involve "pronounced tradeoffs", and their model explains a large share of choice inconsistency across problems. — [Tversky and Russo 1969, Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/c1ecacdc-75f2-417a-93a0-ac97c1ca1a5d); [Shubatt and Yang, arXiv 2401.17578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17578)
- **Must-link and cannot-link constraints** in clustering: COP-KMeans (Wagstaff, Cardie, Rogers, Schroedl, ICML 2001) clusters under pairwise constraints that two items must or cannot share a cluster. — [Wagstaff et al. 2001, PDF](https://www.wkiri.com/research/papers/wagstaff-kmeans-01.pdf); [ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2001/wagstaff2001icml-constrained/)
- **The design doc's objection, in its own words**: a per-user strength model fit on the comparison log is degenerate because binary insertion never asks a question the current order would contradict, so "the top item never loses and the fit has no unique answer". — [docs/design/recommendation-engine.md](../../design/recommendation-engine.md) (internal, read 2026-10-29)

### Inferences
- **[own] Does the Bradley-Terry objection apply to attribute weights? Mostly no, with one exception.** Per-item Bradley-Terry has one free strength per item, so every order is reproducible exactly and every comparison that agrees with the order is "perfectly separated": the likelihood keeps rising as the strengths spread, and there is no finite maximum. Attribute weights replace n strengths with d weights shared by every item, s_i = w · x_i. For the fit to diverge, some w must reproduce every implied pair, that is the user's whole order must be a linear function of the attributes. With n ≤ d + 1 items in general position any order is reproducible (solve w · x_i = any target), so the exception is the short list: below about d + 1 ranked items the unpenalized fit diverges exactly as the doc says. Above it, real taste is not exactly linear in a few attributes, the order is not reproducible, and the maximum is finite. Any Gaussian prior (ridge or a population prior) makes the objective strictly convex, so the answer is unique at every list length. The same caution applies to sparse one-hot features: a `developer:` tag carried by one ranked item acts like a per-item strength and its weight runs to infinity without a prior, which is why layer 1's "two or more of the user's items" rule is in the right spirit.
- **[own] What the weights add.** Fitting weights is a projection of the order onto the attributes, not new information about the ranked items; its value is generalization to unranked items and a reason ("you weight difficulty high and story low"). It cannot say anything an item's position does not already say about that item.
- **[own] The model, concretely.** Score s_i = w · x_i on standardized attributes. Likelihood options, in order of correctness: (a) Plackett-Luce over the whole list, P(order) = Π over positions i of exp(s_(i)) / Σ over j at or below i of exp(s_(j)), which is the proper likelihood of one full ranking and needs no independence assumption; (b) logistic on all implied pairs, P(a above b) = σ(w · (x_a − x_b)), which is RankNet with a linear scorer and is easy in SQL or a small script but treats the n(n − 1)/2 pairs as independent; (c) the comparison log only (about 200 logged comparisons for a 50-item list against 1,225 implied pairs), which throws away information the order already holds. Use (a) or (b), with the pairs re-weighted so the whole list counts as about n observations, not n(n − 1)/2: each item appears in n − 1 pairs, so unweighted pairs overstate the evidence about (n − 1)/2-fold, give overconfident weights and swamp the prior. Prior: w ~ Normal(m, τ² I), where m is the population's weights (equal weights after standardization, or expert weights, until enough users exist; later the mean of fitted users, which is empirical Bayes) and τ the spread between people.
- **[own] Simulation: how many ranked items to fit d weights.** Setup: d standardized attributes; a person's weights w = m + Normal(0, 0.5²) per attribute with population mean m ~ Normal(0, 0.5²); utility u = w · x + η, with η an item-specific taste the attributes do not capture, sized so the attributes explain 40% of the variance of u; the person ranks n items by u; fit the pairwise logistic model on implied pairs (capped at 1,500 random pairs), weighted to total n, by Newton's method; score on 200 new items by held-out pairwise accuracy against u (0.5 is chance; the "oracle" uses the true w and is the ceiling the attributes allow). 60 simulated people for d = 8, 40 for d = 24, 25 for d = 48. Hyperparameters set on the logit scale (pair logit ≈ 1.7 / (√2 · sd(η)) × w · Δx).

  Reading it: an unshrunk per-user fit is worse than simply using the population's weights until about n ≈ 2 to 3 × d (about 10 to 20 items for 8 weights, about 40 to 60 for 24, more than 80 for 48). The hierarchical fit never falls below the prior and recovers about half of the gap between the prior and the oracle at n = 40 for d = 8 (56%), n = 80 for d = 24 (52%) and n = 160 for d = 48 (48%), roughly 3 to 5 ranked items per weight. A typical Verso list (20 to 50 games) supports about 8 to 15 personal weights shrunk toward a population prior, not 40 to 60 independent ones; fit the personal weights on a few grouped dimensions (say difficulty, pace, tone, story weight, social) and let the full genome enter through the population weights and the similarity terms. A first run with the prior mean on the wrong (utility, not logit) scale made the hierarchical fit worse than the prior as n grew, which is the practical warning: the prior must be estimated on the same scale the model fits, which empirical Bayes over real users does automatically.
- **[own] What breaks, and the fix for each.** (1) Pairs are not independent: weight the list to n observations or use Plackett-Luce, and get uncertainty from the user-level leave-one-out rather than from pair counts. (2) The order is partly noise (a re-asked comparison may flip): the logistic link absorbs it, the prior keeps it from becoming a weight, and the tier structure can be used as a coarser, more reliable target (fit tier membership with an ordinal model, positions within tier with a smaller weight). (3) Taste outside the attributes (the η above): the ceiling in the simulation is 0.68 to 0.71 because attributes explained 40%; the harness measures the real share. (4) Correlated attributes: weights on near-duplicate attributes are poorly identified and trade off against each other; the prior stabilizes them, and grouping the genome into a few composite dimensions before personal fitting avoids it.
- **[own] "Too close" as a near tie, in the weight model.** With Rao-Kupper on the logistic scale, Δ = w · (x_a − x_b) and threshold θ ≥ 0: P(a above b) = σ(Δ − θ), P(b above a) = σ(−Δ − θ), P(tie) = the rest. At θ = 1, P(tie) is 0.462 when Δ = 0, 0.381 at Δ = 1, 0.222 at Δ = 2 and 0.101 at Δ = 3. A "too close" answer therefore pulls w · (x_a − x_b) toward zero: it says the attributes on which a and b differ carry little weight for this person, in the direction of that difference. That is real information that the current equal spacing of positions discards, and it costs one extra parameter per user (or per medium) for θ.
- **[own] "Can't compare" as distance, with a caution.** The choice literature supports that hard comparisons are those with pronounced trade-offs, which in attribute space means items far apart and in different directions (neither dominates), not merely far apart. Three uses follow, each an inference: (a) leave can't-compare pairs out of the weight likelihood rather than coding them as a win (the person declined to say); (b) read them as cannot-link constraints when clustering the loved tier into prototypes, so two loved items the person refused to compare land in different prototypes; (c) check, in the log, whether can't-compare pairs are farther apart in genome distance than ordinary pairs, which is a free validity test of the genome itself. I found no study using a "can't compare" answer this way in a recommender.

### Gaps
- I found no published number for how often a person reverses their own head-to-head answer on a re-ask (test-retest of pairwise preferences for games or films), which sets how much of the order is noise; the comparison log could measure it by occasionally re-asking a settled pair.

## Diversity and exploration

### Takeaway
Pick three that are each good and not near-copies of each other or of something already ranked: drop editions and remasters of ranked games outright using IGDB's relation fields, cap one pick per franchise, then re-rank the remainder with maximal marginal relevance; reserve at most one of three slots for exploration, chosen by uncertainty rather than at random.

### Cited Findings
- **MMR.** Carbonell and Goldstein (SIGIR 1998, pp. 335-336) select the next item maximizing λ · relevance − (1 − λ) · max similarity to items already selected; λ = 1 is the plain relevance ranking and lower λ gives more diversity; they report benefits for document reranking and especially multi-document summaries. — [SIGIR'98 abstract](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/ammoffat/sigir98/abstracts/carbonell.html); [Webis anthology](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/1998.sigirconf_conference-98.43/)
- **Intra-list similarity and topic diversification.** Ziegler, McNee, Konstan and Lausen (WWW 2005) define intra-list similarity and a diversification method; on book data (about 361,349 ratings and a study of more than 2,100 people) diversification lowered average accuracy but raised user satisfaction with the lists. — [Ziegler et al. 2005, PDF](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p22.pdf)
- **Beyond-accuracy measures.** Kaminskas and Bridge (ACM TiiS 7(1), 2016/2017) survey definitions and metrics for diversity, serendipity, novelty and coverage and find diversity tracks novelty, which raises coverage; Ge, Delgado-Battenfeld and Jannach (RecSys 2010) evaluate coverage and serendipity, with unexpectedness defined against a "primitive" baseline recommender. — [Kaminskas and Bridge, UCC record](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/diversity-serendipity-novelty-and-coverage-a-survey-and-empirical/); [Ge et al. 2010, dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/recsys/GeDJ10)
- **How Pandora answered "it all sounds the same".** Pandora Modes (launched 2019-03-19) let a listener switch a station between "My Station" (the default algorithm), Deep Cuts (lesser-known tracks), Discovery (artists that do not normally play on the station), Crowd Faves, Artist Only and Newly Released. — [Engadget, 2019-03-19](https://www.engadget.com/2019-03-19-pandora-modes-stations-feature.html); [9to5Mac, 2019-03-19](https://9to5mac.com/2019/03/19/pandora-modes-custom-streaming-radio/)
- **Pandora's repetition limits are partly law.** US webcasters under the statutory license follow the "sound recording performance complement": in any three hours, at most three tracks from one album (no more than two in a row) and at most four by one featured artist (no more than three in a row). — [17 U.S.C. 114 text, stoneslaw.net](https://www.stoneslaw.net/practice-areas/usc-title-17/17-usc-114-scope-of-exclusive-rights-in-sound-recordings/4); [CBI explainer](https://askcbi.org/webcasting-information/sound-recording-performance-complement/)
- **A thumbs-down removes similar items.** IEEE Spectrum (2017-08-01) reports that after a thumbs-down Pandora likely will not play that song or similar ones again, and that the service weighs stations saved and played. — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-machine-learning-is-reinventing-the-way-we-discover-music)
- **IGDB carries the relations needed to catch near-duplicates.** The `games` fields include `collection`, `collections`, `franchise`, `franchises`, `parent_game`, `version_parent`, `remakes` and `remasters`; `parent_game` covers DLC, editions and ports and `version_parent` the same game's editions. — [docs/research/game-data-sources.md](../game-data-sources.md) (internal); [igdb-api-types schema](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DmitryScaletta/igdb-api-types/master/api.json)
- **Exploration by posterior sampling.** Chapelle and Li (NIPS 2011) found Thompson sampling highly competitive and at the state of the art on display ads and news recommendation. — [Chapelle and Li 2011](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2011/hash/e53a0a2978c28872a4505bdb51db06dc-Abstract.html)

### Inferences
- **[own] Worked example 5, MMR on five candidates for a soulslike fan.** Relevance: Dark Souls Remastered 0.95, Dark Souls II 0.90, Lies of P 0.85, Hollow Knight 0.80, Hades 0.75. Genome similarities: the three souls games 0.8 to 0.9 with each other, 0.3 to 0.45 with the other two, Hollow Knight and Hades 0.5. Top three by relevance alone (λ = 1): Dark Souls Remastered, Dark Souls II, Lies of P, three versions of one experience. λ = 0.85: Remastered, Dark Souls II, Hollow Knight. λ = 0.7: Remastered, Hollow Knight, Hades. MMR fixes the set but not the first pick: the remaster of a game already ranked stays on top, which is why the relation filter must run before MMR, not instead of it.
- **[own] Order of operations.** (1) Hard rule: exclude any candidate whose `version_parent` or `parent_game` is a ranked item, or that is a remake or remaster of one (IGDB relation fields), unless the person asks for it. (2) Soft rule: at most one pick per franchise or collection among the three, the Pandora-statute idea applied to games. (3) MMR over the genome similarity with λ around 0.7 to 0.8 for three picks. (4) Spread picks across the user's prototypes in proportion to cluster size (the patent's own rule). Relevance and similarity must be on comparable 0-to-1 scales for λ to mean anything.
- **[own] Exploration slot.** With a hierarchical weight posterior, Thompson sampling is natural: draw w from the person's posterior, score, take the top candidate not already picked; a person with a short list gets more varied third picks automatically, a person with a long list gets near-greedy ones. Label it ("a stretch", as the design doc already does for low confidence) and log it, since its realized tier is the best evidence about the weights' uncertainty.
- **[own] Serendipity, measurable in Verso terms.** Ge and others' definition (unexpected relative to a primitive recommender, and liked) maps directly: a pick that the community prior would not have surfaced (outside the prior's top 100 for the filters) and that the person later ranks Liked or Loved.

### Gaps
- No primary source found for how Pandora's station algorithm itself avoids "sounds the same" beyond Modes and the statutory limits; Pandora's later blend of genome and collaborative signals is described only in press.
- No study found on near-duplicate handling (sequels, remasters) in game recommenders specifically.

## Retrieval in Postgres with pgvector on Supabase

### Takeaway
At Verso's scale (about 70,000 games, a few hundred to a few thousand after filters, 30 to 60 attributes) exact scoring is the right default: measured on the local Supabase image, an exact top-30 over all 70,000 48-dimension vectors takes about 26 to 40 ms and over a 2,100-row filtered set about 5 ms, with perfect recall. Per-user weights cannot use a shared HNSW index for weighted Euclidean distance: the algebraic workaround is exact in a scan but measured recall through HNSW fell to 8%.

### Cited Findings
- **Operators and defaults.** `<->` L2, `<#>` negative inner product (negative because Postgres index scans only run ascending), `<=>` cosine distance, `<+>` L1, `<~>` Hamming and `<%>` Jaccard on bit vectors. Without an index pgvector does exact search with "perfect recall"; approximate indexes "trade some recall for speed"; for a small table "a table scan may be faster"; `max_parallel_workers_per_gather` speeds exact scans. — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- **HNSW versus IVFFlat.** HNSW has the better speed-recall trade-off but slower builds and more memory, needs no training step (can be built on an empty table), defaults m = 16, ef_construction = 64, hnsw.ef_search = 40; IVFFlat builds faster and uses less memory, should be created after data exists, lists = rows / 1000 up to a million rows, ivfflat.probes = 1 by default. Both index `vector` up to 2,000 dimensions and `halfvec` up to 4,000. — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- **Filtering and iterative scans.** With an approximate index the filter is applied after the index scan, so a filtered query can return fewer rows than asked (the README's example: a 10% match rate at ef_search 40 returns about 4 rows on average). Iterative index scans (`hnsw.iterative_scan` = `strict_order` or `relaxed_order`; `ivfflat.iterative_scan` = `relaxed_order`) keep scanning until enough rows pass, bounded by `hnsw.max_scan_tuples` (default 20,000) or `ivfflat.max_probes`. — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- **Versions.** 0.5.0 (2023-08-28) added HNSW, `l1_distance`, element-wise multiplication of vectors and `sum`; 0.7.0 (2024-04-29) added `halfvec`, `sparsevec`, HNSW L1 indexing, `binary_quantize`, `l2_normalize`, `subvector`; 0.8.0 (2024-10-30) added iterative index scans and better cost estimation when filtering; the latest release is 0.8.7 (2026-10-01), with 0.9.0 unreleased (asynchronous I/O for HNSW). — [pgvector CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pgvector/pgvector/master/CHANGELOG.md); [PostgreSQL news, 0.8.0](https://www.postgresql.org/about/news/pgvector-0.8.0-released-2952/)
- **0.8.0's planner change** means that for small or selective filters Postgres may skip the vector index entirely, and the same latency without an ANN index is preferable because it gives 100% recall. — [Nile, pgvector 0.8.0](https://www.thenile.dev/blog/pgvector-080)
- **Published exact-scan latencies are scarce and inconsistent.** EDB's pgvector docs say starting with no index under a million rows is reasonable because an exact sequential scan is fast enough for most workloads (no number given); blog benchmarks put brute force over 100,000 × 768 dimensions at single-digit to tens of milliseconds, and I could not verify their pgvector figures. — [EDB pgvector docs](https://www.enterprisedb.com/docs/pg_extensions/pgvector/using/); [Mastra benchmark](https://mastra.ai/blog/pgvector-perf)
- **What Supabase ships locally.** The local Supabase image in this repo (`public.ecr.aws/supabase/postgres:17.6.1.167`, PostgreSQL 17.6) offers pgvector 0.8.2 as an available extension; it is not installed in Verso's local database today (observed with `pg_available_extensions` and `pg_extension`, 2026-10-29). Supabase's pgvector guide enables it with `create extension vector` (in the `extensions` schema in its example), warns that naive filtering on an IVFFlat or HNSW index can return fewer rows than requested, and says to use iterative search for filtered queries; it states no version number. — own observation for the local version; [Supabase pgvector guide](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/extensions/pgvector)
- **Verso's current exact pass.** The existing content scorer with idf counted live ran the full candidate pass over 70,000 games in about 0.4 s. — [docs/design/recommendation-engine.md](../../design/recommendation-engine.md) (internal)

### Inferences

  Takeaways: pgvector's C arithmetic is about 20 to 80 times faster than the same arithmetic over Postgres arrays; an exact score over everything is tens of milliseconds and over a filtered pool a few milliseconds, well inside a suggest request.
- **[own] Why per-user weights defeat a shared index, and the workaround that fails.** A weighted Euclidean distance Σ w_k (x_k − q_k)² equals the plain distance between √w ⊙ x and √w ⊙ q, so it is a different space for each person and an index built on x does not apply. Algebra rescues it on paper: Σ w_k (x_k − q_k)² = Σ w_k x_k² − 2 Σ w_k q_k x_k + constant, which is an inner product between a stored augmented vector z = (x, x²) and a per-user query r = (−2 w ⊙ q, w); so `ORDER BY z <#> (−r)` returns exactly the weighted nearest neighbors (verified: 30 of 30 identical to the weighted exact top 30). Through an HNSW `vector_ip_ops` index on z, though, recall@30 at ef_search 100 was 0.08 (1 to 5 of 30 over 10 queries) on structured data (48 attributes from 6 latent factors plus 30% noise), while plain unweighted L2 through HNSW on the same data had recall 0.93 (26 to 30 of 30); on independent uniform data L2 recall was 20 of 30 for one query. Inner-product search over vectors with very different norms is a poor fit for the graph. Conclusion: do not index per-user weighted distance; score it exactly over a filtered pool.
- **[own] The linear-model case is different in principle.** If the personal score is linear, s = w · x, then the person's weights are the query and one shared inner-product index applies (`ORDER BY x <#> w`). I did not measure its recall; given the augmented result, verify before relying on it.
- **[own] Recommended shape.** Stage 1: hard filters in SQL (medium, platform, services, runtime, not ranked) leave hundreds to a few thousand rows. Stage 2: exact scores over that pool: idf-tag cosine (today's layer 1), weighted genome distance or max similarity to the user's loved items (here pgvector's `<->`, `<=>` and `*` help even without an index), prior. Stage 3: MMR and the relation filter on the top 50 to 100. If an index is ever needed (an unfiltered "more like this" across 70,000 items), use HNSW on the unweighted or population-weighted genome to fetch about 500 and rerank exactly with personal weights; with a selective filter set `hnsw.iterative_scan`, because in the measured 3% filter case a plain HNSW scan returned 0 rows and the iterative scan returned 30.
- **[own] Max-similarity to loved items is cheap.** For a user with 20 loved items and a pool of 2,000 candidates it is 40,000 distance evaluations, a fraction of the 70,000-row scans timed above.

### Gaps
- No published, verifiable pgvector exact-scan latency at about 70,000 rows and 30 to 60 dimensions was found; the numbers above are my own single-machine measurements on synthetic data inside Docker on an M1 Pro, and hosted Supabase compute will differ.
- The hosted Supabase pgvector version was not confirmed from Supabase's own documentation.
- HNSW recall for a linear per-user query (`x <#> w`) on standardized attributes was not measured.

## Evaluation: genome score against the current content score

### Takeaway
Compare the two scorers paired, on the same held-out items and the same insertions, never as two separate averages; make the user the unit of inference, since items within one list are correlated. With a dozen testers only a large, consistent improvement is detectable; a pooled test over a few hundred held-out items or insertions can see a mean-absolute-error gain of about 0.2 points, and pairwise accuracy needs about 1,000 independent pairs to see a 4-point gain.

### Cited Findings
- **Which significance tests.** Smucker, Allan and Carterette (CIKM 2007, pp. 623-632), using five TREC collections, found the paired t-test, the bootstrap and the randomization (permutation) test agree closely, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank and sign tests both miss real differences and can flag false ones, and recommended discontinuing them for differences in means; their 2009 follow-up found agreement falls as the number of topics shrinks and the bootstrap may overstate significance on small sets. — [Smucker et al. 2007, PDF](https://ciir-publications.cs.umass.edu/getpdf.php?id=744); [Webis anthology](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2007.cikm_conference-2007.67)
- **The instruments already exist.** `recommendation_loo` and its summary report per user the mean absolute errors of the content score, the user's mean and the prior, tier accuracy and the Spearman correlation; `prediction_calibration` reports prospective insertion rows by user, medium, `model_version`, list-size band, confidence and source; the design doc says the split by `model_version` is "the before-and-after for any change to the scorer", and that interleaving, not an A/B split, tests picks. — [docs/design/recommendation-engine.md](../../design/recommendation-engine.md) (internal)
- **Accuracy and satisfaction can diverge**, so diversity changes need a user-facing measure as well as error: Ziegler and others' diversified lists were less accurate and better liked. — [Ziegler et al. 2005, PDF](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2005/proceedings/docs/p22.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[own] Metrics, defined on Verso's data.** Per held-out item i of a user's list: absolute error |predicted − true score|; tier hit (predicted tier = true tier); pairwise accuracy, the share of the user's other ranked items j for which sign(pred_i − pred_j) equals sign(true_i − true_j) (0.5 is chance; this is a per-item concordance and averages to the list's AUC-like agreement); per list, Spearman between predicted and true scores. Pairwise accuracy is the metric closest to what Verso asks people (which of two), the least sensitive to how scores are mapped onto 0 to 10, and the one the weight model optimizes.
- **[own] Leave-one-out without leakage.** For the genome scorer with learned weights, refit the weights with the held-out item excluded (n fits per user; at d around 10 and n around 50 each fit is milliseconds), and fit the population prior on other users only. Otherwise the held-out item's own position has already shaped the weights and the harness flatters the genome.
- **[own] Prospective, shadow-scored.** Write both scores into every insertion row (the existing `components` jsonb can carry `content_v1` and `genome_v1` side by side), computed from the list as it stood, neither read during the insertion, as the design doc's rule requires. Every insertion is then a paired observation of the two scorers on the same item at the same moment, which removes between-model differences in who used which version and when.
- **[own] How many items or users.** Paired absolute error: n ≈ (2.8 × sd / δ)², where sd is the standard deviation of the per-item difference in absolute errors and δ the true gain (two-sided α = 0.05, power 0.8). With sd = 1.0 point: δ = 0.3 needs about 87 items, δ = 0.2 about 196, δ = 0.1 about 784 (sd 0.8: 56, 125, 502; sd 1.2: 125, 282, 1,129). Pairwise accuracy: only pairs where the two scorers disagree carry information (McNemar). If they disagree on 20% of pairs and one is 4 points more accurate, it wins 60% of the disagreements, which needs about 194 discordant pairs, about 970 pairs in total; a 3-point gap with 30% disagreement needs about 2,600. Spearman, two correlated predictors on the same list (Meng, Rosenthal and Rubin's test for dependent correlations): 0.55 against 0.45 with the two predictors correlated 0.85 needs about 175 items, at 0.70 about 340; 0.50 against 0.45 needs about 950. These counts assume independent units, which items within one list are not; with few users, test at the user level: a paired t-test on per-user mean differences detects a standardized per-user effect d_z of about 1.6 with 5 users, 1.1 with 8, 0.9 with 12, 0.66 with 20 and 0.4 with 50. With a dozen testers, the genome must win for nearly every one of them to show up.
- **[own] A decision rule that fits the data Verso will have.** Report per-user paired differences (MAE, pairwise accuracy, tier hit) with a user-level randomization test or cluster bootstrap over users, and ship the genome term as one weighted component of the blend whose weight the harness fits, rather than as a replacement; a component that does not help gets a weight near zero without a separate decision.

### Gaps
- No source found for the standard deviation of paired error differences on ranked-list data like Verso's; the sample sizes above are sensitivity tables, to be recomputed once the harness gives a first sd.
- No published comparison of a graded attribute genome against binary tag features inside the same content-based recommender was found.
