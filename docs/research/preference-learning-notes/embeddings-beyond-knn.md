# Item embeddings for personal recommendation, beyond plain kNN (2023 to 2026), for a small pairwise-preference app

Notes compiled 2026-10-29 for VER-310, as input to [preference-learning.md](../preference-learning.md). They extend the companion report [attribute-genome.md](../attribute-genome.md), which already proposes item kNN over model-scored attributes or synopsis embeddings, scored by the maximum similarity to a person's Loved items, with per-person weights learned from the ranked list and shrunk toward everyone's (its simulation: an unshrunk per-user fit needs about two to three ranked items per weight to beat population weights; a shrunk fit recovers about half the gap to the oracle at three to five items per weight). Numbers and arguments marked "(researcher's own)" are this researcher's arithmetic or reasoning, not published results. Verso's setting throughout: 69,183 games and about 25,000 films; each person's signal is a full order plus the stored head-to-head answers, typically 20 to 50 items early; about a dozen testers; Postgres on Supabase with pgvector, small Edge Functions, a nightly GitHub Actions job, and Claude Haiku 5.5 picking and explaining three candidates.

One framing point before the questions (researcher's own): a pretrained text embedding does not itself "improve with more data"; it is frozen. What improves with data is whatever is learned on top of it: a per-person head, a projection shared across people, or a collaborative model aligned to it. Every option below is a choice of what to learn on top of fixed vectors, and how much data that learning needs.

## 1. Per-person models on frozen embeddings: Bradley-Terry heads, shrinkage, low rank, and how many pairs

### Takeaway
The 2024 to 2026 personalized-reward-model literature converges on one form that fits Verso: a Bradley-Terry (logistic on score differences) head on frozen embeddings, where a basis is shared across users and each person fits only a few coefficients; this beats one shared head for unseen users given 4 to 50 preference pairs each. A full per-person head at 384 to 1,536 dimensions is out of reach for 20 to 50 ranked items; the personal part must live in roughly 4 to 16 dimensions, reached by PCA first and by a basis learned across users once there are enough of them.

### Cited Findings
- LoRe (Bose et al., Meta FAIR, University of Washington, CMU; arXiv 2504.14439, April 2025): each user's reward is a weighted combination of B shared basis rewards; in the experiments the basis is a linear map of frozen embeddings, R = A·e with A of size B × D and D = 4,096 from a frozen Skywork reward model, user weights on the simplex; for an unseen user only the weight vector is fit while the basis stays frozen; B is picked from {2, 5, ..., 50} by cross-validation — [arXiv 2504.14439](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14439v1)
- LoRe accuracy on unseen users: PRISM (643 seen users; about 3.87 examples per unseen user) 71.0% against 61.0% for one shared Bradley-Terry model, 58.2% for VPL and 59.0% for PAL; Reddit TLDR (only 20 seen and 20 unseen workers, 50 examples per unseen user, 100 to 150 per seen user) 66.2% to 66.7% against 60.0% to 64.3% for the shared model; PersonalLLM synthetic (1,000 seen users, 9 prompts per unseen user) 93.3% to 96.1% against 86.4% to 93.1%. The paper reports no per-user-only baseline — [arXiv 2504.14439](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14439v1)
- PReF, "Language Model Personalization via Reward Factorization" (Shenfeld, Faltings, Agrawal, Pacchiano; ICML 2025, arXiv 2503.06358): each user's reward is a weighted mix of shared base reward functions, so personalization reduces to estimating a few user-specific coefficients; with synthetic users, five samples from a new user were enough to beat a standard (shared) reward model; with about 10 responses per user it reached a 67% win rate over default GPT-4o responses in a human evaluation. That the base functions are initialized by SVD of a preference matrix and that questions are chosen by uncertainty-based active learning come from secondary summaries and were not verified in the paper — [arXiv 2503.06358](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06358v1); [ICML 2025 listing](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/50236)
- PAL (Chen, Chen, Rege, Vinayak; arXiv 2406.08469, June 2024; ICLR 2025): an ideal-point model with a mixture of prototype preference points in a shared latent space, built on a foundation model's penultimate-layer representation plus small MLPs, few-shot generalizes to unseen users and matches large reward models at far fewer parameters — [arXiv 2406.08469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08469)
- "Reusing Embeddings" (Sun, Shen, Ton, van der Schaar; arXiv 2502.04357, 2025-02-04): reward models trained on precomputed embeddings run on one CPU; a three-layer MLP on embeddings trained in about 28 s on CPU against about 4,755 s for a LoRA-tuned Gemma 2B on a V100; embedding-based reward models can beat the Gemma 2B one when annotations are few or noisy — [arXiv 2502.04357](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04357v1)
- PCA before learning helps on recommendation data: across Amazon Beauty, Delivery Hero and Steam, recommending by LLM-embedding similarity (LLMSeqSim) was always best with dimensionality reduction, PCA "always" gave the best configuration, and higher target dimensions (512) beat lower (128); the starting embeddings had 1,536 (OpenAI) and 768 (Google) dimensions — [Boz, Zorgdrager, Kotti, Harte, Louridas, Karakoidas, Jannach, Fragkoulis, arXiv 2402.01339 v2, 2025-01-11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01339)
- Pretrained text embeddings of items are anisotropic (a secondary summary reports an average cosine above 0.8 between items), and whitening them before use significantly improves sequential recommendation; full whitening can erase similarity between items with similar text, so the authors ensemble full and relaxed whitening (WhitenRec+) — [Zhang, Zhou, Zeng, Shen, ICDE 2024, arXiv 2402.10602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10602)
- Conjoint analysis is the marketing name for this exact model (per-person part-worths from choices between profiles, hierarchical Bayes shrinkage); the companion report already cites Lenk et al. (Marketing Science 1996) and Sawtooth's guidance of about ten or more choice tasks per person — [attribute-genome.md](../attribute-genome.md)

### Inferences
- How many pairs (researcher's own): a person's 20 to 50 ranked items imply 190 to 1,225 pairs but carry roughly n independent observations, as the companion argues. By the companion's simulation, an unshrunk per-person head needs about two to three items per weight to beat population weights, which at 384 to 1,536 dimensions means about 770 to 4,600 ranked items: out of reach for anyone. A shrunk head recovers about half the gap at three to five items per weight, so 20 to 50 items support about 4 to 16 personal dimensions. The personal head therefore has to sit on a projection, not on the raw embedding.
- Which projection (researcher's own): with a dozen users, PCA (or whitening) of the catalog's embeddings is the only basis available, and Boz et al. show PCA itself helps similarity search. PCA keeps the directions along which items vary most, which need not be the directions along which taste varies. Once enough people have ranked enough items, a LoRe-style basis learned from everyone's pairs (A of size k × d, k chosen by cross-validation over users) replaces PCA with taste-relevant directions; each person then fits only k weights, shrunk toward the population mean. LoRe's Reddit TLDR result with only 20 seen workers suggests the shared basis can start to pay at a few dozen people with 100 or more comparisons each, but that is text summaries, not games or films.
- The model form is the same as the companion's attribute weights: P(a above b) = σ(w · P(e_a − e_b)), with P the projection and w ~ Normal(m, τ²I). It inherits the companion's fixes: pairs reweighted so the list counts as about n observations (or Plackett-Luce over the whole order), "too close" answers as Rao-Kupper ties, "can't compare" pairs left out.
- A linear head and max-similarity to Loved items answer different questions (researcher's own): max-similarity finds items near any one taste cluster and cannot cancel clusters; a linear head finds a direction of taste ("darker", "slower") and can rank items far from every loved item. They belong as two terms in the harness blend, not as rivals.
- Centering or whitening embeddings before cosine (subtract the catalog mean, then PCA) is the embedding version of the companion's "centered cosine" point and should apply to max-similarity scoring too (researcher's own).

### Gaps
- No study found that fits per-person Bradley-Terry heads on frozen text embeddings from ranked lists of films or games and reports accuracy against item kNN by list length; the closest evidence is LLM reward personalization (text responses, not catalog items).
- No published count of pairs per person needed at a given embedding dimension with shrinkage; the counts above rest on the companion's simulation, which used synthetic attributes.
- Whether taste-relevant variation in synopsis embeddings lies in the top principal components is unknown; only the harness can say.

## 2. Gaussian-process and Bayesian preference learning

### Takeaway
A preference GP (Chu and Ghahramani 2005) puts a GP prior on a person's utility over item features and learns it from pairwise answers; with a linear kernel it reduces to the Bayesian Bradley-Terry head above, and with a nonlinear kernel on reduced embeddings it can capture several taste clusters. At 20 to 50 items per person it is computationally trivial. Its distinct value for Verso is calibrated uncertainty, which feeds the "stretch" slot by Thompson sampling, not active choice of the next comparison, since binary-search insertion already decides which pairs are asked.

### Cited Findings
- BoTorch's PairwiseGP implements the Chu and Ghahramani (2005) preference GP: a probit (or logit) likelihood over pairwise comparisons, a Laplace approximation of the posterior over latent utilities, and a scaled RBF kernel by default — [BoTorch source, pairwise_gp](https://botorch.readthedocs.io/en/stable/_modules/botorch/models/pairwise_gp.html); [BoTorch pairwise likelihoods](https://botorch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/botorch/models/likelihoods/pairwise.html)
- Collaborative GPs for preference learning (Houlsby, Hernández-Lobato, Huszár, Ghahramani; NIPS 2012, older): recast pairwise preference learning as GP binary classification through a preference kernel, share information across users through latent functions, and choose queries by BALD (Bayesian active learning by disagreement) to learn from as few answers as possible — [NIPS 2012](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2012/hash/afdec7005cc9f14302cd0474fd0f3c96-Abstract.html)
- Active preference learning for ordering items in and out of sample (Bergström, Carlsson, Dubhashi, Johansson; NeurIPS 2024): a logistic model on item attributes, with comparisons sampled to minimize a bound on ordering error that accounts for both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, so new items can be ordered out of sample — [NeurIPS 2024 poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/95312)
- PEBOL (Austin, Korikov, Toroghi, Sanner; RecSys 2024, arXiv 2405.00981): Bayesian beliefs over items are updated from natural-language-inference scores between a user's utterances and item descriptions, Thompson sampling or UCB picks the next item to ask about, and the LLM only phrases the question; after 10 turns MRR@10 reached up to 0.27 against 0.17 for the best monolithic LLM, on simulated users — [arXiv 2405.00981 v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00981v2)
- Earlier embedding-based elicitation (2019, older): a user's point in a shared embedding space can be estimated from noisy "which do you prefer" pairs, choosing the most informative pair given earlier answers — [arXiv 1905.04363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.04363)
- Thompson sampling with a posterior over a person's weights was already proposed for the stretch slot in the companion, citing Chapelle and Li (NIPS 2011) — [attribute-genome.md](../attribute-genome.md)

### Inferences
- Scale (researcher's own): the Laplace step factors an n × n matrix over the items a person has compared, which is about 50³ ≈ 125,000 operations at n = 50; scoring 500 filtered candidates is a kernel product plus one triangular solve each. This runs in milliseconds in a nightly Python job or even in an Edge Function; a dozen users is far below any limit. A collaborative GP over all users costs more, but still runs on a laptop at hundreds of users.
- Kernel choice (researcher's own): an RBF kernel on raw 1,024-dimensional embeddings suffers the distance concentration the companion documents; the GP should sit on the same PCA-reduced, centered features as the linear head. With a linear kernel the GP's posterior mean equals the Bayesian Bradley-Terry head, so the GP adds value only through the nonlinear kernel and the uncertainty.
- Active selection (researcher's own): in Verso the next comparison during insertion is set by the decided binary-search ranking (decision Option A); choosing comparisons by information gain would reopen that decision. Two places where active selection fits without reopening it: which item fills the stretch slot (Thompson sampling from the posterior), and the order of the onboarding bulk-entry deck (decision 25), where items could be shown by expected information about the person's weights. Both are proposals for Rafa, not decided.

### Gaps
- No 2023 to 2026 study found that runs a preference GP over text embeddings of films or games and compares it against item kNN.
- The qEUBO acquisition (preferential Bayesian optimization) and its cost were not checked; it targets continuous design spaces more than catalogs.

## 3. Collaborative methods that use embeddings: two-tower, BPR, content-initialized MF, cold-start models

### Takeaway
Every collaborative method that uses embeddings (BPR or two-tower models with content features, DropoutNet, CLCRec, ColdLLM, AlphaRec, MARec) learns its mapping from a warm interaction matrix with many users and items co-rated. They beat content-only methods on public datasets with thousands of users, but no study gives a crossover user count, and at a dozen testers there is almost no co-occurrence to learn from. They become worth trying when Verso has hundreds of users whose lists overlap; one exception worth an offline test is transferring a mapping learned on public interaction data.

### Cited Findings
- BPR's loss is a logistic on score differences between a preferred and a non-preferred item, the same form as Bradley-Terry; a RecSys 2024 replicability study found open-source BPR implementations that diverge from the paper and lose up to 50% of performance, and that a tuned BPR comes near state-of-the-art top-n results, beating Mult-VAE by 10% NDCG@100 on the Million Song Dataset and competing with EASE — [Milogradskii et al., RecSys 2024, arXiv 2409.14217](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14217v2)
- Tuned simple baselines are hard to beat (2019, older): six of seven neural recommenders examined were often outperformed by comparably simple nearest-neighbor or graph methods once those were tuned — [Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi, Jannach, RecSys 2019](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/4581acd8-b5d1-4b3d-8bc8-5982f25b751b/are-we-really-making-much-progress-a-worrying-analysis-of-recent-neural-recommendation-approaches.pdf); a 2026 audit of 64 LLM-based recommender papers raises the same reproducibility worries for the LLM era (its baseline findings were not read in full) — [Shehzad and Jannach, UMAP 2026](https://faisalse.github.io/LLM-reproducibility-audit_)
- Pilászy and Tikk (RecSys 2009, older) titled their Netflix Prize study "even a few ratings are more valuable than metadata" for new movies; the exact rating counts were not verified — [RecSys 2009 record](https://bibtex.github.io/RecSys-2009-PilaszyT.html)
- AlphaRec (Sheng et al., ICLR 2025, arXiv 2407.05441): item representations linearly mapped from advanced language-model embeddings of item text, plus an MLP, graph convolution and a contrastive loss, beat leading ID-based collaborative filtering on several datasets (a reviewer summary cites 6.79% to 9.75% Recall@20 over the best baseline), converge fast, and transfer zero-shot to entirely new datasets; newer embedding models gave consistently better results than BERT-style ones — [arXiv 2407.05441](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.05441v4)
- ID-based vs modality-based (Yuan et al., SIGIR 2023): with modern text encoders trained end to end, modality-based recommenders matched or beat ID-based ones even outside cold start, but ID-based models kept their edge on items with many interactions; three medium-sized datasets only — [arXiv 2303.13835](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.13835)
- MARec (Monteil, Vaskovych, Lu, Majumder, van den Hengel; arXiv 2404.13298, v3 2024-12-10): aligns item metadata to a collaborative backbone with a closed-form solution (EASE); beats the best cold-start baseline by 8.4% to 53.8%, including Amazon Video Games (title, description, features, brand, categories) hr@10 0.1568 against 0.1231 and MovieLens 10M 0.1094 against 0.0961; EASE trained in 34 s on MovieLens 10M and 92 s on Amazon Video Games; CLCRec was among the baselines — [arXiv 2404.13298 v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13298v3)
- ColdLLM (arXiv 2402.09176; WSDM 2025): an LLM simulates interactions for cold items; reports average NDCG gains of 10.79% overall and 37.10% on cold items over LightGCN, and finds generative embedding simulators beat dropout-based methods (DropoutNet-style) on warm and overall recommendation — [arXiv 2402.09176](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09176)
- FilterLLM (arXiv 2502.16924, 2025) benchmarks against DropoutNet and CLCRec on CiteULike and MovieLens 10M and argues that dropout-based models ignore the gap between warm behavior embeddings and cold content embeddings — [arXiv 2502.16924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.16924)
- CLCRec (Wei et al., 2021, older) learns content representations by contrastive learning against collaborative embeddings and was benchmarked against DropoutNet, CB2CF and Heater — [arXiv 2107.05315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.05315)
- LightFM (Kula, 2015, older) represents users and items as sums of their metadata features' embeddings inside a matrix factorization, aimed at user and item cold start — [arXiv 1507.08439](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.08439)

### Inferences
- Data condition (researcher's own): these methods learn from co-occurrence, meaning items that appear together in many people's lists. With 12 testers, each ranking 20 to 50 of about 94,000 items, almost every item pair is co-ranked by zero, one or two people, so a learned item-item or content-to-collaborative mapping would fit noise. They become plausible at hundreds of users with overlapping lists, and they ship only when the harness shows them beating the content scorer, which is decision 24's existing gate for neighbors.
- A two-tower model with a frozen item tower (the embeddings) and a user tower that pools the person's ranked items, trained on everyone's pairs with the BPR or Bradley-Terry loss, is the same object as the LoRe-style shared projection in question 1, seen from the recommender side (researcher's own). At Verso's scale the shared projection is the realistic version.
- Borrowing a crowd (researcher's own, untested): AlphaRec's zero-shot transfer suggests a mapping from text embeddings to a collaborative space could be learned on public interaction data (MovieLens for films; a public Steam dataset for games) and applied to Verso's catalog, giving a content kNN whose similarity reflects co-consumption rather than wording. This is the only collaborative idea that needs no Verso users; it is an offline experiment for the harness, subject to the datasets' licenses.
- MARec's closed form and its 34 s training time make EASE plus metadata the cheapest collaborative step when the data arrives; a nightly job could run it (researcher's own).

### Gaps
- No study found that measures how many users or interactions a collaborative or hybrid model needs before beating content-only kNN; the 2009 Netflix result is the closest and is old.
- The licenses of MovieLens and the public Steam datasets for a commercial app were not checked.
- Two-tower models at small scale: no 2023 to 2026 evidence found on their data needs below thousands of users.

## 4. Newer families: sequential recommenders, generative retrieval, HSTU, LLM embeddings as features, LLM-as-ranker

### Takeaway
Sequential recommenders, TIGER-style generative retrieval and HSTU all model what a person consumes next and need tens of thousands to billions of interaction sequences; Verso's signal is an order of preference, not a timeline, and its user count is four to eight orders of magnitude too small. LLM embeddings as item features help most when item text is discriminative, and they helped least on Steam games. As rankers, LLMs lose to plain text-embedding similarity in the most direct 2025 comparison, which supports keeping Haiku as the picker and explainer of three, not the ranker of hundreds.

### Cited Findings
- SASRec vs BERT4Rec: BERT4Rec's published results replicated only with up to 30 times longer training (Petrov and Macdonald, RecSys 2022) — [arXiv 2207.07483](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.07483); with the same cross-entropy loss, SASRec significantly beats BERT4Rec in quality and training speed (Klenitskiy and Vasilev, RecSys 2023) — [arXiv 2309.07602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07602); gSASRec's generalized BCE loss beats BERT4Rec, for example +9.47% NDCG on MovieLens 1M — [IJCAI 2024 extended abstract](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/939)
- Boz et al. (arXiv 2402.01339 v2, 2025-01-11; extends Harte et al., RecSys 2023): initializing SASRec and BERT4Rec with LLM embeddings raised NDCG@20 about 45% on Amazon Beauty and 9% on Delivery Hero, but on Steam (279,290 sessions, item text = game title plus tags) the boost was "steady but marginal", which the authors attribute to titles that are semantically non-discriminative and tags shared across many games; pure embedding similarity (LLMSeqSim) did well only on Beauty and trailed on Steam, and its popularity hybrid trailed even the popularity baseline on Steam; an embedding-based session kNN only slightly improved on plain session kNN outside Beauty; LLMSeqSim gave the best catalog coverage and novelty; an LLM re-ranker (LLMSeqPromptRank) did worse than the LLMSeqSim candidates it re-ranked — [arXiv 2402.01339](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01339)
- TIGER (Rajput et al., Google; NeurIPS 2023): each item gets a Semantic ID, a tuple of codewords, and a sequence-to-sequence Transformer predicts the next item's Semantic ID from a session; reports significantly beating state-of-the-art models and better retrieval for items with no interaction history — [arXiv 2305.05065](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05065)
- A semantic-ID reproducibility study (arXiv 2609.24430, 2026-09-21): holding TIGER's backbone fixed, no single semantic-ID design is best across Video Games, Microlens and Yelp; on Video Games the best generative method reached NDCG@10 0.0301 against SASRec's 0.0152, but SASRec beat several generative methods on Microlens and Yelp (read from search snippets, not the full text) — [arXiv 2609.24430](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.24430)
- HSTU (Zhai et al., Meta; ICML 2024): generative recommenders with 1.5 trillion parameters, trained on a platform with tens of billions of user actions a day; up to 65.8% NDCG over baselines offline and 12.4% in online A/B tests — [arXiv 2402.17152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17152)
- Text embeddings vs LLM rerankers for training-free cold start (Kusano, Abe, Takeoka; arXiv 2512.13001, 2025-12-15; MovieLens 1M, a jobs dataset, Amazon Music, Movie and Book): text embedding models beat LLM rerankers in cold and warm settings; narrow cold start Recall@10 / nDCG@10: Qwen3-Embedding-8B 0.392 / 0.430, gte-Qwen2-7B 0.372 / 0.412, gpt-4.1 0.311 / 0.283, gpt-4.1-mini 0.282 / 0.260, BM25 0.315 / 0.362; broad cold start with one history item: gte-Qwen2-7B 0.536 / 0.581, Qwen3-Embedding-8B 0.514 / 0.569, gpt-4.1 0.433 / 0.377. Users with history were scored by the average of cosine similarities to each history item; ColBERT-style max-sum and multi-query variants generally did not help at one or three history items. No trained collaborative baseline was included — [arXiv 2512.13001](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13001v1)
- LLMs as zero-shot rankers (Hou et al., ECIR 2024, arXiv 2305.08845): competitive with conventional recommenders on candidates from several retrievers, but with position and popularity bias, reduced by bootstrapping (ranking shuffled candidates repeatedly) — [arXiv 2305.08845](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.08845v2)
- LLMs are competitive near cold-start recommenders (Sanner, Balog, Radlinski, Wedin, Dixon; RecSys 2023): for language-based preferences with no item ratings, zero- or few-shot LLM prompting was competitive with item-based CF; language preferences are more scrutable than vectors — [arXiv 2307.14225](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14225v1)

### Inferences
- Signal mismatch (researcher's own): Verso's insertion order is the order people happened to add items, not the order they played or watched them, so next-item models (SASRec, BERT4Rec, TIGER, HSTU) would learn onboarding habits. Even with timestamps, they need sequence counts Verso will not have for years. Not worth it at any scale on the roadmap.
- LLM-as-ranker (researcher's own): the 2025 head-to-head and Boz et al.'s re-ranking result both say the LLM should not replace embedding retrieval or scoring. Verso's current design, where SQL picks candidates and the model writes the reason (decision 24), matches the evidence; Haiku choosing among a short scored list, with candidates shuffled to reduce position bias, is the defensible use.
- Kusano et al.'s finding that averaging similarities to history items worked as well as max-sum at one to three history items does not contradict the companion's max-similarity proposal, which matters when a list has several taste clusters; with 20 to 50 items, clusters are likely (researcher's own).

### Gaps
- No public result found for LLM-embedding recommenders trained on full ranked lists rather than consumption sequences.
- Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and EmbeddingGemma were not in Kusano et al.'s headline numbers available here; whether small local models keep most of the 8B model's advantage on recommendation is unverified.

## 5. Synopsis embeddings for films and games: measured results

### Takeaway
For films, sentence-transformer embeddings of item text beat bag-of-words features by about 27% hr@10 in a cold-start model on MovieLens 10M, and adding crowd tags on top added far more (about 58% hr@10); LLM-written synopses add little where titles and genres are already present. For games, the one controlled result (Steam, titles plus tags) found LLM embeddings gave only marginal gains; no study with full Steam or IGDB descriptions was found. Tags or attributes beside the embedding matter more than the choice of embedding model.

### Cited Findings
- MARec on MovieLens 10M (item text: title, description, genres, actors, directors): in a metadata-only setting, sentence-transformer embeddings reached hr@10 0.0378 against 0.0329 for TF-IDF and 0.0071 for an item-kNN baseline; with the EASE backbone, bag-of-words 0.1094, sentence-transformer 0.1385, plus poster images 0.1401, plus MovieLens user tags 0.2194 (hr@100 0.4692, 0.4721, 0.6168); the paper reports semantic features add 46.7% to 105.5% over bag-of-words and tags 30.7% at hr@100 — [arXiv 2404.13298 v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13298v3). By the researcher's arithmetic, embeddings over bag-of-words are +27% hr@10, tags over embeddings +58% hr@10, images +1%.
- MARec on Amazon Video Games (title, description, feature description, brand, categories): hr@10 0.1568 against 0.1231 for the best baseline (+27.4%) — [arXiv 2404.13298 v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13298v3)
- ViLLA-MMBench (Nazary, Tourani, Deldjoo, Di Noia; arXiv 2508.04206, 2025-08-06; MovieLens 1M subset of 992 films, 6,040 users): LLM-written 100-to-150-word synopses changed Recall and NDCG by about −1.5% to +2.6% for most models (one model gained about 11%); fusing text with a collaborative VAE raised NDCG@10 from 0.3584 to 0.3666 (OpenAI text) or 0.3698 (Sentence-T5); text helped cold-start coverage most — [arXiv 2508.04206](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04206)
- EmerFlow (arXiv 2512.10370, December 2025): on MovieLens, a variant using only LLM-generated features performed about as well as the full model, because the LLM's descriptions mostly restate title and genre (from a search summary, not the full text) — [arXiv 2512.10370](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.10370)
- Steam (Boz et al., 2025): titles concatenated with tags; LLM-embedding gains marginal; tags like "action" and "multi-player" are shared by many games and add little — [arXiv 2402.01339](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01339)
- Model-scored game attributes added to learned recommenders raised MRR by 14% to 20% (Jia et al., 2025), already cited by the companion — [arXiv 2504.11658](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11658v1)

### Inferences
- What to embed (researcher's own): the Steam result says titles and generic tags carry little; IGDB summaries and storylines and TMDB overviews carry more, but the film results suggest a synopsis mostly restates genre unless it is rich. The richest cheap text is probably synopsis plus IGDB themes and keywords (or TMDB keywords) in one string, scored by the harness against content-v1.
- Embeddings complement rather than replace tags (researcher's own): MARec's tag jump and the companion's two-term design (cosine on idf tags, a separate similarity on graded or embedded content) point the same way. The embedding term should be a third term in the blend, with its weight fit by the harness.
- MovieLens user tags are crowd-sourced, closer to a tag genome than to IGDB keywords; their large gain does not transfer automatically to curated keywords (researcher's own).

### Gaps
- No measured cold-start or content-recommendation result found from embeddings of full game descriptions (Steam store text or IGDB summaries); the 2026 CPGRec+ Steam work surfaced only through an aggregator and was not read.
- No study found that isolates plot-only against genres-only embeddings on MovieLens.

## 6. Practical: embedding model, cost, local models, pgvector storage and search, refresh

### Takeaway
Embedding the whole catalog costs under a dollar by API or an overnight run locally; an open Apache-2.0 model such as Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B keeps catalog text on Verso's machines. At about 94,000 items, 1,024-dimension float vectors take about 390 MB before overhead, close to Supabase's 500 MB free database, so store reduced (PCA or Matryoshka-truncated) or half-precision vectors and search exactly without an index.

### Cited Findings
- OpenAI text-embedding-3-small: $0.02 per million input tokens, 1,536 dimensions by default, shortenable with a dimensions parameter; a $0.01 batch rate is widely reported but was not confirmed on OpenAI's page — [OpenAI model page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/text-embedding-3-small)
- Voyage (pricing page updated 2026-09-29): voyage-4-lite $0.02, voyage-4 $0.06, voyage-4-large $0.12 per million tokens, each with 200 million free tokens; batch 33% cheaper — [Voyage pricing](https://docs.voyageai.com/docs/pricing)
- Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B: Apache-2.0, 0.6B parameters, up to 1,024 dimensions (any output size from 32), 32K context, query instructions add about 1% to 5%; MTEB English v2 mean: 0.6B 70.70, 4B 74.60, 8B 75.22, against gemini-embedding-exp-03-07 73.3 and gte-Qwen2-7B 70.72; paper dated 2025-06-05 — [Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B)
- EmbeddingGemma (Google, arXiv 2509.20354, September 2025): 308M parameters, 768 dimensions truncatable to 128 (Matryoshka), best under 500M parameters on MTEB multilingual, English and code v2, built for on-device use — [arXiv 2509.20354](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20354v3); Qwen3-Embedding-8B was first on MTEB multilingual on 2025-06-05 at 70.58 — [Modal, MTEB leaderboard](https://modal.com/blog/mteb-leaderboard-article)
- On recommendation specifically, the larger LLM-based embedders (Qwen3-Embedding-8B, gte-Qwen2-7B) led multilingual-e5-large, bge-m3 and gte-modernbert in Kusano et al.'s cold-start comparison — [arXiv 2512.13001](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13001v1)
- pgvector: a vector takes 4 × dimensions + 8 bytes and a halfvec 2 × dimensions + 8; both store up to 16,000 dimensions; HNSW and IVFFlat index up to 2,000 dimensions for vector and 4,000 for halfvec; without an index the search is exact with perfect recall; latest release 0.8.7 — [pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)
- Supabase: 500 MB database on the Free plan; 8 GB disk on Pro, then $0.125 per GB; Pro's default Micro compute has 1 GB RAM — [Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing)
- The companion measured exact cosine over 70,000 48-dimension vectors at 26 to 27 ms and exact per-user weighted Euclidean at 33 to 40 ms on the local Supabase image, and recommends exact scoring over a filtered pool with no index — [attribute-genome.md](../attribute-genome.md)

### Inferences
- Storage (researcher's own arithmetic, about 94,200 items, before row and index overhead): vector at 384 dims about 145 MB; 768 dims about 290 MB; 1,024 dims about 387 MB; 1,536 dims about 579 MB; halfvec at 1,024 dims about 194 MB. Full-size float vectors for both catalogs at 1,024 dimensions or more do not fit beside the rest of the data on the Free plan; on Pro they fit easily. Storing a PCA- or Matryoshka-reduced 256-dimension copy for scoring (about 97 MB as vector) and keeping full vectors off the database or as halfvec is the economical layout.
- Embedding cost (researcher's own arithmetic): at 150 to 300 tokens per item (synopsis, title, genres, keywords), the catalog is 14 to 28 million tokens: $0.28 to $0.57 with text-embedding-3-small or voyage-4-lite, or free within Voyage's 200 million token allowance. Monthly refresh of a few thousand new or changed items is cents.
- Local cost (researcher's own estimate, not measured): at about 2 × parameters × tokens operations, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B over 28 million tokens is about 3.4 × 10¹⁶ operations, roughly 4 to 9 hours on an M1 Pro at an assumed 1 to 2 TFLOPS effective, and EmbeddingGemma about half that; a one-off overnight run. Nightly increments of about 1,000 items are about 3 × 10⁵ tokens, under an hour on a CPU-only Actions runner for the 0.6B model and less for EmbeddingGemma. Worth timing before relying on it.
- Search (researcher's own extrapolation from the companion's 48-dimension timings): exact cosine over the full 70,000 games at 1,024 dimensions should take a few hundred milliseconds, and over a 500-item filtered pool a few milliseconds, so the companion's no-index design holds for embeddings too. Max-similarity of 20 to 50 loved items against 500 candidates is 10,000 to 25,000 dot products, trivial in pgvector.
- Model choice (researcher's own): Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Apache-2.0, local, Matryoshka down to 32) is the default for a no-third-party constraint; EmbeddingGemma is the lighter alternative (its license terms were not checked). An API model only matters if the harness shows a gain worth sending catalog text out, and the TMDB and IGDB terms gate any embedding of their synopses either way, as the companion notes.

### Gaps
- Actual local throughput of Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B or EmbeddingGemma on the M1 Pro and on a GitHub Actions runner was not measured.
- EmbeddingGemma's license terms and the current MTEB standings (the leaderboard is a live app) were not checked.
- Whether synopsis embeddings of TMDB and IGDB text are permitted under their terms is the companion's open question and was not re-examined here.

## 7. Ranked shortlist for Verso: what to try first, second and later, and when each becomes worth it

### Takeaway
First, at today's dozen testers: synopsis-plus-keyword embeddings from a local model, centered and PCA-reduced, scored by maximum similarity to Loved items (the companion's fourth arm), plus a per-person Bayesian Bradley-Terry head on about 8 to 16 reduced dimensions, shrunk toward the population mean; both as blend terms tested in the harness. Second, at a few dozen active users: a projection learned across users (LoRe-style) in place of PCA, with a GP or Laplace posterior driving the stretch slot. Later, at hundreds of users with overlapping lists: a collaborative hybrid (EASE plus metadata, or BPR with content-initialized items). Not on the roadmap: sequential and generative recommenders, HSTU, and an LLM as the main ranker.

### Cited Findings
- Few-shot personal coefficients on a shared basis over frozen embeddings beat one shared head for new users with 4 to 50 pairs each — [LoRe, arXiv 2504.14439](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14439v1); [PReF, arXiv 2503.06358](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06358v1)
- PCA of LLM embeddings always gave the best embedding-similarity configuration — [arXiv 2402.01339](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01339)
- Text-embedding similarity beat LLM rerankers for training-free cold start — [arXiv 2512.13001](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13001v1)
- Collaborative cold-start hybrids beat content baselines when a warm matrix exists; EASE with metadata trains in seconds — [MARec, arXiv 2404.13298](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13298v3)
- Sequential, generative and HSTU-class models are trained on hundreds of thousands to billions of sequences — [arXiv 2402.01339](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01339); [HSTU, arXiv 2402.17152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17152)

### Inferences
The ranking below is the researcher's own synthesis; none of it is decided, and each step ships only if it beats the previous one in `recommendation_loo` and the paired insertion log, per the repo's rule.

1. **Try first: embedding max-similarity plus a shrunk personal head on reduced embeddings.** Data: what exists today (each tester's order and comparison log; the catalog's text). Compute: one embedding run (overnight locally, or under $1 by API), PCA in the nightly job, a Newton or Laplace fit per person in milliseconds. Infrastructure: a reduced vector column in Postgres, exact pgvector scoring, a nightly Python step. Evidence over kNN: indirect (LoRe, PReF and conjoint show shrunk few-shot personal weights work; Boz et al. show PCA helps similarity), none on game or film lists. The head adds over max-similarity only if taste has directions the embedding captures; the harness answers that. Worth it now, as one pilot arm beside the companion's genome arm, because it costs no new data.
2. **Second: a learned shared projection (LoRe-style) and posterior-driven exploration.** Worth it when about 20 or more people have 50 or more ranked items each (LoRe's smallest setting had 20 seen users with 100 to 150 examples each), and only if the per-person head from step 1 earns weight in the blend. Adds a cross-validated rank k and a nightly fit over all users' pairs on CPU. The GP or Laplace posterior is cheap from step 1 and can feed the stretch slot by Thompson sampling whenever that slot is built.
3. **Offline experiment, any time: a mapping transferred from public interaction data.** Learn a text-to-collaborative projection on MovieLens or a public Steam dataset (AlphaRec-style zero-shot transfer) and test it as a similarity in the harness. Worth it only if the dataset licenses allow commercial use; unverified.
4. **Later: collaborative hybrids.** EASE plus metadata (MARec), BPR or LightFM-style factorization with content-initialized items, or a two-tower model with a frozen item tower. Worth it at hundreds of users whose lists overlap, under decision 24's existing gate that neighbors must beat the baseline in the harness.
5. **Not on the roadmap: SASRec, BERT4Rec, TIGER, HSTU, and an LLM as main ranker.** The signal is a preference order, not a consumption sequence, and the data needs are orders of magnitude beyond Verso. Haiku keeps its current job: choosing among a short, already-scored list (shuffled to blunt position bias) and writing the reason.

### Gaps
- Every step above lacks direct evidence on ranked lists of games or films; the harness with a dozen testers can see only large, consistent gains (the companion's power arithmetic: a per-user standardized effect of about 0.9 with 12 users).
- The data thresholds for steps 2 and 4 are judgment calls anchored on LoRe's smallest setting and on co-occurrence reasoning, not on a published crossover study.
