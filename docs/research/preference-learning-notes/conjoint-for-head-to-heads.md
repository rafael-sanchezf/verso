# Conjoint analysis for head-to-head comparisons: what it adds to Verso's attribute weights

Notes compiled 2026-10-29 for VER-310. Numbers marked "(researcher's own)" are this researcher's arithmetic or derivations, not published results. Companion report read: `docs/research/attribute-genome.md` (Summary, "A ranked list is many seeds", "Each person's weights can be learned from the list", "What this means for Verso", Addendum). Verso's pivot and escape-hatch rules were read from `docs/design/ranking-algorithm.md` (Option A) and the engine's "one rule" from `docs/design/recommendation-engine.md` (line 89). Sources marked "via search snippet" were seen only as search-result summaries, not fetched in full; treat them as leads to confirm.

Bottom line for the report writer (researcher's synthesis of the sections below): conjoint does not give Verso a new model. Choice-based conjoint with two options per task is the same pairwise logistic (Bradley-Terry with attribute-linear strengths) the companion proposes, and its hierarchical Bayes estimation is a fuller version of the companion's "shrunk toward a population prior". What conjoint adds is practice around that model, in five places. (1) A full hierarchical prior: a covariance of tastes, not one spread τ; a mixture of normals when the population has taste clusters; and sign or order constraints. (2) Nonlinear part-worths per attribute level, or ideal-point terms, for attributes like difficulty where "medium" can be the favorite. (3) Validation conventions: holdout hit rates against chance, and against an aggregate-only baseline. (4) Adaptive question design, which helps Verso less than it first appears, because a full order implies the same pairs whichever pivots were asked; it helps fully in optional questions that never touch ranking. (5) The misfits to correct for. Real, self-selected, correlated items break the orthogonal-design assumptions behind conjoint. Unobserved item quality that correlates with attributes will load onto the weights unless the community prior enters the model as an offset.

## The method: choice-based conjoint, its logit, part-worths, and Bradley-Terry; ratings-based, adaptive and MaxDiff variants

### Takeaway
Choice-based conjoint (CBC) is a multinomial logit (MNL) on designed product profiles, with each profile's utility a sum of part-worths, one per attribute level. With two options per task, the logit is exactly a Bradley-Terry model whose log-strengths are linear in attributes. That matches the companion report's pairwise logistic model term for term, and a full ranking is the exploded logit the companion already cites. The variants differ in the question asked: ratings (traditional and ACA), choices (CBC), choice after screening (ACBC), and best and worst (MaxDiff). The part-worth function model allows non-monotone utility per attribute, which a single linear weight does not.

### Cited Findings
- A typical CBC study shows about ten choice tasks with about three to six product concepts each — [Sawtooth, What is CBC analysis](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/hid_web_whatcbc.html) (help page, undated, accessed 2026-10-29).
- In CBC the item utility is linear in attribute part-worths, U(x) = w·x — [Zhang et al., ConjointNet, arXiv 2503.11710, 2025-03-12](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11710).
- Chapelle and Harchaoui (NIPS 2004) cast CBC as machine learning. Each consumer has a linear utility u(x) = w·x. Each choice yields constraints w·(x_chosen − x_other) ≥ 0, so estimation becomes classification on profile differences. They fit it with a soft-margin SVM with no bias term — [Chapelle and Harchaoui, NIPS 2004](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2004/hash/4bbdcc0e821637155ac4217bdab70d2e-Abstract.html).
- Ranked data fit a standard logit or mixed logit by treating the ranking as a sequence of choices: the first-ranked item is chosen from all, the second from the rest, and so on. This is the exploded logit — [Train, Discrete Choice Methods with Simulation, 2nd ed. (2009), ch. 7, "Ranked Data"](https://eml.berkeley.edu/books/choice2nd/Ch07_p151-182.pdf).
- Green and Srinivasan's (1978) three preference models: the vector model (linear, fewest parameters); the ideal-point model (quadratic, w·(x − x₀)², peak at an ideal level); and the part-worth function model (a separate value per level, the most flexible shape). Flexibility and parameter count rise in that order, and a mixed model lets each attribute take its own form — [Springer chapter citing Green and Srinivasan, via search snippet](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/978-0-387-78213-3_2); [Green and Srinivasan 1978 PDF, via search snippet](https://typeset.io/pdf/conjoint-analysis-in-consumer-research-issues-and-outlook-3q5v6wkzks.pdf).
- Relative attribute importance in conjoint is conventionally the range of an attribute's part-worths divided by the sum of ranges across attributes — [MetricGate docs, 2025-01-16](https://metricgate.com/docs/conjoint-analysis/) (secondary tool documentation).
- Ratings-based adaptive conjoint (Sawtooth's ACA) has two sections. Self-explicated priors ask people to rate attribute importances and level preferences directly; then come metric (graded) paired comparisons adapted to each person's earlier answers. Hierarchical Bayes later replaced ad hoc ways of combining the two — [Sawtooth, ACA technical papers](https://events.sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/technical-papers/adaptive-conjoint-analysis-aca); [Sawtooth, Details of ACA utility estimation](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/hid_web_estaca.html) (via search snippet). Sawtooth's own material reports that the self-explicated importances can flatten the final importances. Comparative work (Agarwal and Green) found self-explicated data alone predicted better than ACA in some tests — [Huber et al., ACA vs full profile, Duke](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/ACAvs%20full%20profile.pdf) (via search snippet).
- Adaptive CBC (ACBC, Sawtooth, technical paper February 2014) runs a build-your-own configurator, then a screening section, then a choice tournament among surviving concepts. Most of its evidence sits in Sawtooth conference proceedings, not peer-reviewed journals. It takes 50% to 200% longer than standard CBC — [Sawtooth, ACBC technical papers](https://events.sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/technical-papers/adaptive-choice-based-conjoint-acbc); [Greenbook](https://www.greenbook.org/marketing-research/choice-based-conjoint-technique) (via search snippet). One academic description calls the tournament a single-elimination bracket of pairs — [arXiv 2212.00885](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.00885) (via search snippet).
- Sawtooth's motivation for ACBC: in one study with 9 attributes, 85% of respondents' choices could be explained by attention to at most four attribute levels, which points to non-compensatory screening — [Sawtooth, Motivation for Adaptive CBC](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/motivationforadaptivecbc.html) (via search snippet).
- Best-worst scaling (BWS) asks for the best and the worst item in a set; MaxDiff is the BWS model that treats the pair as the one with the maximum utility difference. Louviere, Flynn and Marley's book (Cambridge University Press, 2015) set out the theory and separated the names. Response-time studies found the maxdiff model fits the choices well, but no individual actually uses a maxdiff process — [Louviere, Flynn and Marley, Best-Worst Scaling, CUP 2015, record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:cbooks:9781107043152); [Wikipedia, Best-worst scaling](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Best%E2%80%93worst_scaling); [Cambridge chapter "Looking forward"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/bestworst-scaling/looking-forward/40291D2F03C32DC28204980349E2668B) (via search snippet).
- Polyhedral methods also exist for metric paired-comparison data (Toubia et al. 2003). The choice-based version (2004) differs in important ways — [Toubia, Hauser and Simester, JMR 2004, PDF](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.1.116.25082).

### Inferences
- The core formula, written out by the researcher. Person h, choice task t, alternatives j in set C_t, each with a coded attribute vector x_j: dummy or effects codes per level for categorical attributes, numbers for graded ones. Utility is U_hj = β_h · x_j + ε_hj, with ε i.i.d. Gumbel (type I extreme value). Then P_h(j | C_t) = exp(β_h · x_j) / Σ_{k in C_t} exp(β_h · x_k), where β_h is person h's vector of part-worths. With C_t = {a, b}, P_h(a over b) = exp(β_h·x_a) / (exp(β_h·x_a) + exp(β_h·x_b)) = σ(β_h · (x_a − x_b)), with σ(z) = 1/(1+e^(−z)). That is Bradley-Terry with log-strength β_h·x_i, and it is the companion's P(a above b) = σ(w · (x_a − x_b)) with w = β_h. A whole Verso list is a ranking-based conjoint task: one exploded logit over the n items, which is Plackett-Luce with covariates.
- Worked numbers (researcher's own). Take three standardized game attributes (difficulty, story weight, coziness) and part-worths β = (0.8, −0.3, 0.5). Item A = (1.5, −0.5, −1.0) and item B = (−0.5, 1.0, 1.0). Then U_A = 1.2 + 0.15 − 0.5 = 0.85 and U_B = −0.4 − 0.3 + 0.5 = −0.2, so Δ = 1.05 and P(A over B) = σ(1.05) = 0.741. That equals the two-option MNL share exp(0.85)/(exp(0.85)+exp(−0.2)) = 2.340/3.159 = 0.741. Decomposed by attribute: difficulty contributes +1.6 to the gap, story weight +0.45, coziness −1.0. That decomposition is a "reason" in Verso's sense: "you put A above B mainly for its difficulty, despite it being less cozy".
- What the model menu means for a graded genome (researcher's inference). The companion's s_i = w·x_i is Green and Srinivasan's vector model on every attribute: "more difficulty is always better (or worse) for this person". For difficulty and pace, the ideal-point model (a favorite level) is more plausible, at one extra parameter per attribute. Alternatively, the part-worth function model codes each graded attribute as a few levels (0-1, 2-3, 4-5), at two parameters per attribute instead of one. Either can represent "likes medium difficulty", and neither can represent the companion's cozy-and-soulslike person, whose taste is in two corners of several attributes at once. That needs interactions, a mixture, or the companion's max-similarity-to-Loved scorer. So conjoint's part-worths fit the "personal weights on 5 to 8 grouped dimensions" layer, not the multi-cluster similarity layer.
- Where MaxDiff fits Verso (researcher's own arithmetic and inference). Choosing the best and the worst of four items {A, B, C, D}, say A and D, establishes A>B, A>C, A>D, B>D and C>D: five of the six pairs, missing only B against C. Two taps for five ordered pairs is efficient for the batch onboarding deck, where people place many items at once. It does not fit single-item insertion, which already asks only about log2(n) questions. It would be a change to the ranking flow, which decision 1 (ranking is Option A) does not allow without Rafa reopening it.
- ACA's graded pairs are the precedent for a graded head-to-head ("much better / a bit better / too close"). Verso's binary answer plus "too close" is already a three-point graded scale.
- ACBC's screening stage and the 85% finding (non-compensatory rules) are the conjoint-literature version of "I don't play horror": a filter, not a weight. A linear weight model will try to explain such a rule with a large negative weight, and only for people whose lists contain a horror item. That points to explicit exclusions from onboarding or a "not for me" signal rather than a learned weight.

### Gaps
- The full text of Green and Srinivasan (1978, 1990) was not fetched; the three models' descriptions come from secondary sources that cite them.
- No measured comparison was found between CBC with two options per task and with three to five options for the same number of seconds of respondent time. That comparison would say whether Verso's paired format loses efficiency against multi-option tasks.

## Individual-level estimation: hierarchical Bayes, tasks, attributes, hit rates, and the companion's shrunk logistic fit

### Takeaway
Hierarchical Bayes (HB) estimates each person's part-worths as draws from a population distribution whose mean and covariance are learned from everyone. Each person's estimate is pulled toward the population, most strongly when that person's data are thin or unusual. The companion's "pairwise logistic regression shrunk toward a population prior, empirical Bayes" is the posterior-mode version of the same model, and the conjoint literature shows that mode-finding versions match full HB accuracy at a tiny fraction of the compute. Practice: about 10 or more choice tasks per person for individual-level prediction (8 to 15 typical, under 30 always); about 6 attributes for full profiles and about 10 as the practical top; holdout hit rates of 55% to 75% on three- or four-alternative tasks. What HB adds to the companion's proposal is a full taste covariance, a mixture prior for clustered populations, ordinal constraints, and posterior draws.

### Cited Findings
- Rossi and Allenby (Marketing Science 22(3), 2003) write the hierarchical model in three layers. The first is the unit-level likelihood p(y_i | β_i). The second is a first-stage prior p(β_i | τ), the population distribution of tastes. The third is a second-stage prior p(τ | h) on its hyperparameters. A normal prior makes the posterior means cluster closer to the prior mean than the unit-level maximum-likelihood estimates, with outliers "shrunk" dramatically. Estimating the prior's tightness from the data is "adaptive shrinkage", whose original motivation was Efron and Morris's empirical Bayes — [Rossi and Allenby 2003, PDF](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf).
- The same review warns that a normal first-stage prior may shrink unit-level estimates so much that it masks a bimodal population, and offers a mixture of normals as the fix — [Rossi and Allenby 2003](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf).
- The review also reports that Sawtooth implemented the hierarchical Bayes logit, and that Sawtooth's founder Rich Johnson concluded Bayesian methods beat the others in the conjoint literature (Sawtooth Software 2001) — [Rossi and Allenby 2003](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf).
- The book (Rossi, Allenby and McCulloch, Bayesian Statistics and Marketing) comes with the R package bayesm. Its `rhierMnlRwMixture` fits a hierarchical MNL with a mixture-of-normals distribution of heterogeneity. Each unit's coefficients are a term explained by unit characteristics plus an individual error. A Gibbs sampler with random-walk Metropolis steps fits it, newer versions allow sign constraints per coefficient, and chapter 5 of the book covers it — [bayesm reference, rhierMnlRwMixture](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/bayesm/html/rhierMnlRwMixture.html) (via search snippet).
- Allenby, Arora and Ginter, "Incorporating Prior Knowledge into the Analysis of Conjoint Studies" (JMR 32, May 1995, 152-162, DOI 10.2307/3152044), brings prior ordinal information about part-worths into the estimate, for example that a lower price is never worse. It is cited as one of the earliest Bayesian conjoint applications — [DeepDyve record](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/doc/u406BA8DL7); [Rossi and Allenby 2003, annotated references](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf).
- Lenk, DeSarbo, Green and Young (Marketing Science 15, 1996) used fractionated designs to show the population distribution can "bridge" across respondents. It imputes part-worths for attributes a respondent never saw — [Rossi and Allenby 2003, annotated references](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf); [INFORMS record](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.15.2.173).
- Evgeniou, Pontil and Toubia (Marketing Science 26(6), 2007, 805-818) shrink individual part-worths toward a population mean by minimizing a convex loss, instead of sampling an HB posterior. Across simulations and field data they generally beat standard HB with diffuse second-stage priors, for both metric and choice data, because the amount of shrinkage differs. A later SVM paper notes the fit-shrinkage trade-off is set by cross-validation — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:26:y:2007:i:6:p:805-818); [Montoya et al., SVM for CBC, JORS](https://www.dii.uchile.cl/~rmontoya/papers/SVM_JORS.pdf) (via search snippet).
- Chapelle and Harchaoui (NIPS 2004) fit individual weights jointly, penalizing deviation from a common mean, with the ratio of two regularization constants setting the individual-versus-aggregate trade-off. Their HB-MAP, the posterior mode found by convex optimization with identity covariance, matched sampled HB's accuracy and was sometimes slightly better. It averaged about 1.8 seconds against about 19 minutes for Gibbs sampling — [NIPS 2004](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2004/hash/4bbdcc0e821637155ac4217bdab70d2e-Abstract.html).
- Sawtooth's task guidance: 8 to 15 choice tasks for CAPI or paper CBC. For individual utilities by CBC/HB it says "at least six" tasks for simulated shares and "about 10 choice tasks or more" for robust individual predictions — [Sawtooth, CBC Questionnaires and Design Strategy](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/hid_web_cbc_designs_1.html) (accessed 2026-10-29). More than 30 tasks is hard for respondents, and 15 or fewer is typical in practice — [Sawtooth, Number of attributes, levels and tasks in CBC](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/cbc-number-of-attributes-levels-tasks.html) (via search snippet).
- Sawtooth's attribute guidance: CBC exercises rarely exceed about 10 attributes, for respondent experience and data quality. Older material cites Green and Srinivasan (1990) for about six attributes as the full-profile maximum. Base CBC software allows 10 attributes with 15 levels each, and an add-on allows 100 attributes, used with partial profiles. Showing 5 of 10 attributes at a time collects about half the information of a full profile but cuts fatigue — [Sawtooth, Number of attributes, levels and tasks](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/hid_web_cbc_designs_4.html); [Sawtooth CBC core settings](https://info.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/discover/survey-elements/cbc/core-settings) (via search snippet).
- Huang and Luo (Marketing Science 35(3), 2016) report that 70 to 100 attribute levels is "typically considered prohibitive" for decompositional methods. Their adaptive method handles it — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:35:y:2016:i:3:p:445-464); [USC PDF](https://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/lan-Luo/ActiveLearningFinal.pdf) (via search snippet).
- Sawtooth's glossary: holdout hit rates on tasks with three or four alternatives usually range from 55% to 75%, falling as alternatives per task rise — [Sawtooth glossary](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/knowledge-base/general-issues/glossary-of-terms) (via search snippet). Sawtooth papers used, for example, 18 calibration tasks of 5 alternatives with 9 holdouts, with an unconstrained hit rate of about 0.655 (Johnson 2000, on monotonicity constraints). Another ("Fine-Tuning CBC and Adaptive CBC Questionnaires", 2009) used 24 random tasks of 12 alternatives with 4 holdouts — [Sawtooth technical papers index](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/technical-papers/categories/choice-based-conjoint) (figures via search snippet from Sawtooth content assets; the exact asset was not identified).
- Toubia, Hauser and Simester (JMR 2004), Monte Carlo: estimates after 16 questions were about 25% more accurate than after 8, and after 24 about 12% more accurate than after 16. In their field study (354 web respondents, four profiles per task, executive-education programs), HB estimates reached an internal hit rate of 94%; analytic-center estimates hit 100% by construction. From the third question on, the two converged about equally — [Toubia, Hauser and Simester 2004, PDF](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.1.116.25082).
- Moore (IJRM 2004) compared OLS, HB and latent-segment models for ratings- and choice-based conjoint. HB matched or beat latent-segment and aggregate models on hit rates and share validation, and choice-based models did not dominate ratings-based ones — [Bohrium record of Moore 2004](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/a-cross-validity-comparison-of-rating-based-and-choice-based-conjoint-analysis-models/812339339060051970-7548) (via search snippet). Contrary evidence: in one Sawtooth analysis, aggregate logit predicted individuals' holdout choices better than HB in four of nine partial-profile datasets, until the priors were adjusted — [Sawtooth asset, via search snippet](https://content.sawtoothsoftware.com/assets/d320d51d-ad42-476b-b781-726df2e2828e) (not confirmed which asset).

### Inferences
- The mapping to the companion (researcher's analysis). The companion's model is w_h ~ Normal(m, τ²I), with an implied-pair logistic likelihood weighted to n and m estimated over users. It is HB's first-stage prior with two simplifications, isotropic covariance and a single normal, fit at the mode, which is Chapelle and Harchaoui's HB-MAP or Evgeniou et al.'s convex shrinkage. Conjoint practice suggests four upgrades, in order of value for Verso:
  - A full covariance Σ across people instead of τ²I. If people who weight difficulty also weight combat intensity, one person's answers on difficulty inform their combat weight. It also handles the companion's warning that "correlated attributes trade weight between themselves".
  - A mixture of normals, or latent classes, for the population. Rossi and Allenby's warning applies directly when Verso's population holds clusters (cozy players and soulslike players): a single normal prior pulls everyone toward a middle nobody holds.
  - Sign or order constraints where Rafa is sure, Allenby, Arora and Ginter style. For example, "reception" never lowers a pick, and if story weight matters at all its sign follows the person's answers.
  - Posterior draws, not only the mode. The companion's Thompson-sampling exploration slot needs them, and a Laplace approximation at the mode is the cheap way to get them.
- Data per person, conjoint against Verso (researcher's arithmetic). A Sawtooth respondent answering 12 tasks of 4 concepts gives 12 first choices, each a choice among 4. As an exploded logit, a Verso list of n items is n − 1 successive choices from sets of size n, n − 1, …, 2: 49 choices for a 50-item list, most from large sets. On raw count Verso has more evidence per person than a typical conjoint respondent. It is worth less per item, because the items are not designed: they are self-selected, correlated, and unbalanced on attributes (next sections). The companion's simulated rule of about three to five ranked items per personal weight sits plausibly beside conjoint practice. A study at Sawtooth's suggested size, say 6 attributes at 3 to 4 levels, has about 12 to 18 part-worth parameters (levels minus one per attribute), estimated from 10 to 15 designed tasks per person, which is less than one task per parameter. HB's borrowing across respondents makes that possible, and it presumes designed variation.
- Shrinkage in one line (researcher's own derivation and numbers, Gaussian approximation). Posterior precision on a weight is the prior precision plus the Fisher information from the answers. A logistic comparison with predicted probability p and attribute gap Δx_k carries information p(1−p)·Δx_k² about weight k. The share of the estimate driven by the person's data is I / (I + 1/τ²). With τ = 0.5 (prior precision 4) and balanced comparisons (p ≈ 0.5), random pairs of standardized items (E[Δx²] = 2) carry 0.5 per comparison: the data share is 0.56 after 10 comparisons, 0.83 after 40 and 0.94 after 120. Pairs of similar items (E[Δx²] = 0.5) carry 0.125: 0.24, 0.56 and 0.79. The figures treat one weight with the others known and comparisons as independent, so they overstate the real data share. They show why which pairs are compared matters as much as how many.
- The hit rate is Verso's pairwise accuracy (researcher's inference). Conjoint's holdout hit rate on two-alternative tasks is exactly the companion's "pairwise accuracy", with chance at 0.5. Sawtooth's 55% to 75% band is for three or four alternatives (chance 25% to 33%), so it is not comparable to the companion's simulated 0.59 to 0.67. Conjoint holdouts are also designed, same-session, fully described profiles, an easier target than a real held-out game whose appeal lies partly outside the attributes. The practice worth copying is the double benchmark: report each person's hit rate against chance and against the population-weights-only model, as Moore and Sawtooth do with aggregate logit. The companion's harness already has the second column ("Population weights only").
- Use the posterior-mode fit with nightly empirical Bayes, as the companion proposes. Full MCMC HB is not needed at Verso's scale, given HB-MAP's parity in Chapelle and Harchaoui. The mixture prior and the full covariance are the parts worth taking from HB, and they cost one more estimation step over users, not MCMC per user.

## Choosing the next comparison: adaptive and polyhedral designs, and Verso's pivot rule

### Takeaway
Adaptive conjoint chooses each next question to shrink what is still unknown about a person's part-worths: polyhedral methods (2003, 2004), probabilistic and Bayesian versions (2007, 2019), and machine-learning versions (2010, 2016). The common rules are choice balance (options nearly equally attractive under the current estimate) and contrast along the directions still uncertain. In Verso the payoff is smaller than it looks. A full order implies the same pairs whichever pivots were asked, as long as answers are consistent, so the pivot changes which pairs are attested directly, not what the list implies. Binary insertion is already choice-balanced on the person's own order. An information-seeking pivot, restricted to the middle 40% to 60% of the eligible range, would cost about 3% more questions and break no rule, provided it reads the uncertainty about the person's weights but not the new item's predicted score. The full machinery of adaptive conjoint fits optional questions that never touch ranking.

### Cited Findings
- Toubia, Simester, Hauser and Dahan, "Fast Polyhedral Adaptive Conjoint Estimation", Marketing Science 22(3), 2003, 273-303, DOI 10.1287/mksc.22.3.273.17743. Each question is chosen to shrink the polyhedron of part-worths consistent with past answers fastest, and the estimate is its analytic center. In Monte Carlo, polyhedral design was best or tied with few questions and fixed efficient designs were better with many. Analytic-center estimation did well under high heterogeneity and low response error, HB under low heterogeneity and high response error. A web field test with 330 respondents, who chose a laptop bag afterward, supported the polyhedral method — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:22:y:2003:i:3:p:273-303); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/1811).
- Toubia, Hauser and Simester, "Polyhedral Methods for Adaptive Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis", JMR 41, February 2004, 116-131. Choice questions are designed on four criteria: nondominance, feasibility, choice balance and symmetry, which the authors call an individual-level analogy to D-efficiency. Choice balance means the options in the next question "should be approximately equally likely". In their simulations polyhedral design did well where heterogeneity and part-worth magnitudes were large. They note that orthogonal designs are optimal only when the part-worths are zero, citing Arora and Huber 2001 and Huber and Zwerina 1996 — [Toubia, Hauser and Simester 2004, PDF](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.1.116.25082).
- Huber and Zwerina, "The Importance of Utility Balance in Efficient Choice Designs", JMR 33(3), 1996, 307-317. Efficient choice designs rest jointly on orthogonality, level balance, minimal overlap and utility balance, judged under nonzero prior part-worths rather than the traditional all-zero assumption. Swapping and relabeling levels to raise utility balance cut the respondents needed for a given error by 10% to 50% across five designs — [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1485813); [Teye-Ali et al., summarizing the four principles](https://transp-or.epfl.ch/heart/2013/ABS56_Teye-Ali_Davidson.pdf) (via search snippets).
- Toubia, Hauser and Garcia, "Probabilistic Polyhedral Methods for Adaptive Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis", Marketing Science 26(5), 2007, 596-610. The deterministic polyhedral methods were "susceptible to the propagation of response errors" and implicitly assumed a uniform prior. The 2007 method adds response error and informative priors. They describe the earlier choice-based extension as "mixed success": better when response errors were low, not when high. A field experiment with over 2,200 wine consumers in three countries supported the new question selection. For logit questions, the information matrix is maximized by questions "close to, but not perfectly, choice balanced", citing Hauser and Toubia 2005 — [Toubia, Hauser and Garcia 2007, PDF](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1060.0257).
- Sauré and Vielma, "Ellipsoidal Methods for Adaptive Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis", Operations Research 67(2), 2019, 315-338, DOI 10.1287/opre.2018.1790. They replace the polyhedron with a normal approximation of the posterior, an ellipsoid, so response error is modeled while the geometric question selection is kept. They report it beats earlier methods in extensive numerical experiments — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:oropre:v:67:y:2019:i:2:p:315-338); [U. de Chile repository](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/handle/2250/171271) (via search snippet).
- Huang and Luo (Marketing Science 35(3), 2016) start each person from collaboratively filtered initial part-worths, then pick questions by fuzzy-SVM active learning and estimate response error during the survey. It handles 70 to 100 attribute levels and outperformed self-explicated, adaptive CBC and traditional CBC methods in their study — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:35:y:2016:i:3:p:445-464); [USC PDF](https://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/lan-Luo/ActiveLearningFinal.pdf) (via search snippet).
- Guo and Sanner (AISTATS 2010) do real-time Bayesian elicitation of multi-attribute utility using pairwise comparison queries, chosen for their low cognitive load. Closed-form updates make expected value of information (EVOI) query selection cheap under a diagonal Gaussian belief, though scoring all candidate pairs becomes costly — [PMLR v9](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/guo10b.html). Vendrov, Lu, Huang and Boutilier (2020) make EVOI for pairwise and slate queries tractable by gradient optimization — [arXiv 1911.09153](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.09153) (via search snippet).
- Verso's Option A already departs from the midpoint. It prefers an eligible pivot that shares a genre with the new item, "as long as it is not too far from the midpoint", costing at most one extra question, and genre "only influences which question gets asked", never where the item may land. Batch sessions also prefer provisional items as pivots within reach of the midpoint — `docs/design/ranking-algorithm.md`, Option A and batch session (repo, 2026).
- "Too close to call" ends the search and places the new item directly below the pivot. "Can't compare" swaps in another eligible pivot, and after two in a row the item is placed at the middle of the current range — `docs/design/ranking-algorithm.md`, Option A.
- The engine's rule: the prediction "is written at insertion and never read during it", because a guess that chose the pivot or starting tier would make "the placement … lean toward the guess" — `docs/design/recommendation-engine.md`, line 89 (rule dated 2026-10-15).

### Inferences
- The central point (researcher's analysis): in Verso, pivots change what is asked, not what is learned from the order. When answers are transitive, binary insertion reaches the same slot whichever eligible pivot is asked, so the finished list, and therefore the n(n−1)/2 implied pairs the companion fits, does not depend on the pivots. For n = 50 that is 1,225 implied pairs against about 282 logged comparisons (n·log2 n). Adaptive design, the core of polyhedral conjoint, therefore buys little for the companion's order-based fit. It matters in four narrower places:
  - (a) If the weight model ever uses the comparison log rather than the order.
  - (b) With noisy answers, where the asked pairs are the attested ones and the rest are inferred through transitivity.
  - (c) "Too close", which ties the new item to whichever pivot was on screen. The pivot decides which tie is learned (Rao-Kupper evidence that w·(x_new − x_pivot) ≈ 0) and where the item lands.
  - (d) "Can't compare", whose second occurrence places the item mid-range.
- Binary insertion is choice-balanced already (researcher's inference). The pivot sits at the median of the person's remaining order, so each answer is close to a coin flip and carries about one bit about position, whatever the model says. Toubia et al. and Huber and Zwerina get the same balance from part-worth estimates. Binary insertion gets it from the person's own order, without reading any prediction. What binary insertion does not control is attribute contrast: two neighbors in a list may differ on many attributes, which teaches trade-offs, or on none.
- Worked information numbers (researcher's own). Under a Gaussian posterior with covariance Σ = 0.25·I on three standardized weights, one logistic answer multiplies the posterior precision's determinant by 1 + p(1−p)·Δxᵀ Σ Δx (matrix determinant lemma), the D-optimal criterion. Expected information gained is half the log of that factor.
  - A same-genre pivot with Δx = (0.3, 0.2, −0.1) gives Δxᵀ Σ Δx = 0.035 and gains 0.0044 nats at p = 0.5.
  - A contrasting pivot with Δx = (1.5, −1.0, 0.8) gives 0.97 and gains 0.109 nats at p = 0.5, or 0.088 at p = 0.73.
  - The contrasting pivot teaches about 25 times more about the weights. It pays for that in apples-to-oranges answers, which the companion's sources associate with more "can't compare" (Tversky and Russo; Shubatt and Yang).
- Cost of an off-midpoint pivot (researcher's own). If the new item's final position is uniform over the eligible range, a pivot splitting the range at fraction q yields H(q) bits per answer (binary entropy), so the expected number of questions scales by 1/H(q): q = 0.4 costs 3% more, 0.35 costs 7%, 0.3 costs 13.5%, 0.25 costs 23% and 0.2 costs 39%. Choosing the most informative pivot within the middle 40% to 60% of the range costs about one extra question per 30.
- Does an informative pivot break the engine's rule? It depends on what it reads (researcher's analysis).
  - Classic utility balance reads the new item's predicted utility, choosing the pivot the model thinks is closest to the new item, and so does EVOI computed from predicted win probabilities. That reads the guess, which the rule forbids, and it would also steer the "too close" landing toward the guess.
  - A rule that maximizes Δxᵀ Σ_post Δx over the middle band reads only the attributes and the current uncertainty about the person's weights. It does not read the new item's predicted score, and binary insertion supplies the balance. It sits in the same class as the genre-preferring pivot already allowed: it influences which question is asked, not where the item may land. The exception is the "too close" and double "can't compare" branches, where any pivot rule (genre included) already influences the slot.
  - The informative pivot pulls against the genre-preferring one. The genre pivot minimizes contrast and the information pivot maximizes it, so Verso would have to choose, or alternate between them by step (early steps for information, the last step or two for apples-to-apples). That choice is Rafa's to make.
- Where adaptive conjoint fits whole (researcher's inference). Adaptive conjoint applies directly to optional questions that place nothing: "which of these two would you rather play next?" between two backlog items, or a short designed deck during onboarding. Its answers go to the weight model only, which respects "the backlog never touches ranking". Polyhedral, ellipsoidal or EVOI selection can pick those pairs freely. Huang and Luo's design is the closest precedent for Verso's case: start from a population or collaborative estimate, then adapt per person. Such answers are stated, pre-experience preferences, while the list holds post-experience evaluations. Train's stated-versus-revealed literature (next section) says to give them their own scale factor rather than pool them as equal evidence.

### Gaps
- Toubia, Hauser and Simester 2004's simulation hit-rate tables did not survive PDF extraction legibly; only the accuracy ratios across 8, 16 and 24 questions and the field internal hit rates are reported.
- No study was found of adaptive question design inside a binary-insertion or sorting flow, where the full order is the output and pivots are constrained to a range.
- Kanninen (2002) and Hauser and Toubia (2005), on why the information-optimal question sits near but not at choice balance, were not fetched.

## The fit and the misfit: real, experienced, self-selected items with correlated attributes; "too close" and "can't compare"

### Takeaway
Conjoint's guarantees come from design: orthogonal or efficient attribute variation, every level shown to every respondent, hypothetical profiles with nothing outside the listed attributes. Verso's pairs are the opposite. They are real items the person chose to play or watch, with correlated attributes, unobserved qualities, and attributes the person avoids never appearing at all. Discrete-choice work on revealed preferences names these problems and their remedies. Separate scales for stated and real choices; population priors to bridge attributes a person never faced; groupings, or a full prior covariance, against collinearity. In this researcher's inference, the most important one for Verso is an offset for item quality, so that personal weights explain departures from consensus rather than consensus itself. "Too close" maps to indifference (ties), not to conjoint's "none". "Can't compare" has no clean conjoint analog. The dual-response literature suggests that a forced follow-up choice would not bias estimates.

### Cited Findings
- Train on revealed-preference data. It is limited to alternatives that exist or existed, and may have "insufficient variation" in the factors of interest. The paradox: the factors most important to consumers "will often exhibit the least variation", because markets equalize them, so their importance is hard to detect. Stated-preference data complement it, but people may not do what they say — [Train 2009, ch. 7](https://eml.berkeley.edu/books/choice2nd/Ch07_p151-182.pdf).
- Combining stated and revealed data requires separate alternative-specific constants and a relative scale parameter for each source (Ben-Akiva and Morikawa 1990; Swait and Louviere 1993). Adamowicz, Louviere and Williams (1994) found separately estimated RP and SP models seemed to reflect different preferences, but joint estimation with a relative scale suggested the preferences were similar — [Train 2009, ch. 7](https://eml.berkeley.edu/books/choice2nd/Ch07_p151-182.pdf); [Morikawa, Ben-Akiva and Yamada, TRB paper](https://barrel.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/4186/files/TRB%20paper%20number_910877.pdf); [Adamowicz et al. summary via U. Alberta staff paper](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/232531/files/ualberta-staffpapers-92-04.pdf) (via search snippets).
- Naturally correlated attributes (horsepower and fuel economy) make orthogonal designs produce unrealistic profiles; non-orthogonal designs can still be efficient. In a non-orthogonal set, the regression attributes part of an effect to the wrong attribute — [Marketing Engineering technical note on conjoint](https://www.decisionpro.biz/business-users/training/technical-notes/send/5-technical-notes/13-tn09-conjoint-analysis-technical-note); [CASRAI guide](https://www.casrai.org/guides/conjoint-analysis-profiles-utilities-importance) (via search snippets).
- Orthogonal designs are optimal only when the part-worths are zero — [Toubia, Hauser and Simester 2004](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.1.116.25082); [Huber and Zwerina 1996](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1485813).
- The population distribution in HB can impute part-worths for attributes a respondent was never shown (Lenk et al. 1996) — [Rossi and Allenby 2003](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf).
- Compiani, Morozov and Seiler, "Demand Estimation with Text and Image Data" (arXiv 2503.20711, March 2025, revised), took principal components of pretrained text and image embeddings and gave them random coefficients in a logit, like observed attributes. On choice-experiment data it beat attribute-based models at predicting second choices. Across 40 Amazon categories, text did better than images at finding close substitutes. The code is released as DeepLogit — [arXiv 2503.20711](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20711v3); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:arx:papers:2503.20711) (via search snippet).
- Gui and Toubia (arXiv 2312.15524, December 2023, revised through November 2025): when a language model is the simulated subject, changing one stated attribute (price) also shifts its beliefs about unstated ones (competitor prices and the like). That breaks unconfoundedness and yields implausibly flat demand curves; revealing the design to the model helps — [arXiv 2312.15524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524) (via search snippet).
- The no-choice option: Brazell, Diener, Karniouchina, Moore, Séverin and Uldry, "The No-Choice Option and Dual Response Choice Designs", Marketing Letters 17(4), 2006, 255-268. A "none" option mimics markets, but choosing it says nothing about how the shown alternatives compare. Dual response asks a forced choice first, then whether the person would really take it. Their studies found little systematic violation of IIA. Choosing "none" was tied more to the attractiveness of the set than to decision difficulty, and dual response adds power without biasing estimates — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:mktlet:v:17:y:2006:i:4:p:255-268). Sawtooth reports that in their split samples the non-None parameters did not differ significantly after scale adjustment, at the cost of extra time per task — [Sawtooth, CBC None option](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/cbc-none-option.html) (via search snippet).
- Hung, Liu, Brazell and Allenby, "Dual Response in Conjoint Analysis" (Marketing Letters, 2025), report about 30% better parameter-estimation efficiency than a single-response model — [Utah Eccles School](https://eccles.utah.edu/news/document/dual-response-in-conjoint-analysis) (via search snippet).
- The companion report already models "too close" with Rao-Kupper's tie threshold and leaves "can't compare" out of the likelihood — `docs/research/attribute-genome.md`, citing [Encyclopedia of Mathematics, paired comparison model](https://encyclopediaofmath.org/wiki/Paired_comparison_model).
- In Hauser's Apple v. Samsung studies, operating system could not be an attribute because combinations like an Android iPhone are not meaningful (the "Apple problem"); Samsung's expert attacked the omission of brand, OS and battery life — [Quirk's, 2014-05-01](https://www.quirks.com/articles/when-apple-samsung-and-conjoint-came-together).

### Inferences
Five misfits and what each implies for Verso (researcher's analysis):
- Self-selection (Train's paradox at the level of one person). A person's list holds what they chose to play or watch. An attribute they strongly avoid, such as horror or extreme difficulty, may barely vary on the list, so the list cannot reveal the weight that matters most to them. A large negative weight on horror may be unidentified for exactly the people who hate horror. Remedies:
  - Let the population prior carry the weight for unseen ranges (Lenk's bridging).
  - Collect stated evidence outside ranking: onboarding exclusions, or "not for me" on a recommendation, read as ACBC-style screening rules rather than weights.
  - Treat Dropped and Disliked items as the real low end they are.
- Correlated attributes. Real items move together (difficulty with combat intensity; darkness of tone with horror), the horsepower-and-mileage case. With two standardized attributes correlated at r, each weight's variance inflates by 1/(1 − r²) (researcher's arithmetic): r = 0.5 gives 1.33 (standard errors ×1.15), 0.7 gives 1.96 (×1.40), 0.8 gives 2.78 (×1.67) and 0.9 gives 5.26 (×2.29). This supports the companion's grouping of attributes before the personal fit. A full prior covariance learned over users (previous section) is the HB alternative; it lets the population say how weights on correlated attributes usually co-move.
- Unobserved quality that correlates with attributes, the demand-estimation endogeneity problem in recommender form (researcher's inference). Conjoint profiles have nothing outside their listed attributes; real games do. If critically acclaimed games also tend to be hard and story-heavy, a person who simply likes good games will show positive weights on difficulty and story that are really weights on quality. Compiani et al. handle unobserved product traits with embeddings; the standard demand-estimation answer is an item-level term. For Verso the cheap fix is to put the community prior (`item_prior`, layer 0) into the person's utility as a fixed offset or a covariate with its own weight: s_hi = α_h·prior_i + w_h·x_i. The personal weights then explain where the person departs from consensus, which is also what a reason should say ("you rank harder games higher than most people do"). The harness can test this as one more arm at no data cost.
- Experienced against hypothetical, which favors Verso (researcher's inference). Conjoint's chief validity attack, used against Hauser in Apple v. Samsung, is that stated choices among hypothetical profiles carry no consequences. Verso's comparisons are post-experience judgments of real items, the same quantity Verso predicts (where an item will land once played). That is closer to a revealed preference than conjoint ever gets. The gap runs the other way: recommendations ask people to choose before experiencing. Stated "which next?" answers would measure anticipated liking, needing their own scale (Ben-Akiva and Morikawa; Train).
- No designed balance. Conjoint's efficiency rules (level balance, minimal overlap, orthogonality) cannot be imposed on a person's list. The only lever is which pairs get compared (previous section) and, for optional questions, which items get shown.
- How the escape hatches map:
  - "Too close" is indifference, a tie in a paired comparison (Rao-Kupper or Davidson), or the midpoint of ACA's graded pair. It is not conjoint's "none". "None" means every shown option falls below an outside option, which in Verso is closer to "neither is worth my time", a tier statement.
  - "Can't compare" resembles "none" in what it records: the person declined to rank the shown options. Brazell et al. found "none" tied to the set's attractiveness rather than difficulty, while the companion's sources tie incomparability to trade-off difficulty, so the two are not the same act.
  - The dual-response evidence suggests that a soft follow-up after "can't compare" ("if you had to pick?") would give a usable, unbiased-on-average choice, with a separate scale factor. Whether to add that tap is a product question for Rafa; it would change the ranking flow's escape hatch.
  - The cheaper, consistent option is the companion's: leave "can't compare" out of the likelihood, use it as a cannot-link constraint, and test whether such pairs sit far apart in attribute space.

### Gaps
- No study was found that estimates conjoint-style part-worths from a person's own ranked list of real, experienced products (as opposed to designed profiles or market shares); the closest are ranking-based conjoint with designed profiles and demand estimation from market data.
- No study was found that measures how much part-worth estimates degrade when choice sets are formed by a sorting algorithm (binary insertion) rather than a design.
- Berry, Levinsohn and Pakes (1995), the standard treatment of unobserved product characteristics in demand estimation, was not fetched; the item-quality offset above is the researcher's inference, not a cited result.

## Conjoint meets machine learning (2023 to 2026), and conjoint-style part-worths inside recommenders

### Takeaway
Three threads matter for Verso.
- Embeddings as conjoint attributes. Compiani, Morozov and Seiler (2025) put principal components of text and image embeddings into a random-coefficients logit, and they beat hand-coded attributes on substitution. That bridges the companion's fourth arm (synopsis embeddings) and personal weights.
- Neural choice models (TasteNet-MNL 2020/2022, ConjointNet 2025). They keep a logit core while learning taste functions, and report gains of a few percent over linear part-worths.
- Language models as synthetic respondents. This is the busiest thread, and its own authors' revisions and a NeurIPS 2026 workshop paper find that it often gets attribute importances wrong, even reversing their rank.
Inside recommenders, the marketing precedents are older and direct. Ansari, Essegaier and Kohli (JMR 2000) built a Bayesian preference model for recommendations, and Chung, Rust and Wedel (Marketing Science 2009) built an adaptive music personalizer tested in the field. Huang and Luo (2016) seed conjoint with collaborative filtering.

### Cited Findings
- ConjointNet (Zhang et al., arXiv 2503.11710, 2025-03-12) combines two neural architectures with conjoint and reports beating traditional conjoint estimation "by over 5%" on two preference datasets, with insight into non-linear interactions; no peer-reviewed venue listed — [arXiv 2503.11710](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11710).
- TasteNet-MNL (Han, Zegras, Pereira and Ben-Akiva; arXiv 2002.00922, February 2020, revised July 2022). A feed-forward network maps individual characteristics to the taste parameters of a hand-specified MNL, trained jointly, and constrained to keep behavior indicators realistic. On Swissmetro data it predicted better than the MNL benchmarks and a mixed logit — [arXiv 2002.00922](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.00922) (via search snippet).
- Compiani, Morozov and Seiler (arXiv 2503.20711, 2025): embedding principal components as random-coefficient attributes; better second-choice predictions than attribute-based models; released as DeepLogit — [arXiv 2503.20711](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20711v3) (via search snippet).
- Brand, Israeli and Ngwe, "Using LLMs for Market Research". GPT-3.5 Turbo, answering conjoint-style questions, reproduced known willingness-to-pay patterns for household products and computers. The MSI working paper (October 2025) calls the estimates "realistic and comparable" to human studies — [MSI working paper](https://www.msi.org/working-paper/using-llms-for-market-research-2/). The revised SSRN abstract (2026, via search snippet; the page returned 403) says they are sometimes comparable but "often inaccurate and in some cases wrong-signed" — [SSRN 4395751](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4395751). Fine-tuning improved alignment for new features but not for new categories or segments — [MSI Report 25-136](https://thearf-org-unified-admin.s3.amazonaws.com/MSI/2025/MSI_Report_25-136.pdf) (via search snippet).
- Hu and Gao, "Can LLMs Serve as Synthetic Respondents for Consumer Demand Estimation?" (NeurIPS 2026, EconML workshop; 18,144 choice observations from a national discrete choice experiment). Demographics-only simulation reversed the signs of all product-origin coefficients. Few-shot prompting with full choice histories cut errors substantially, and dropping rejected alternatives roughly doubled them. Even the best strategy reversed the human ranking of attribute importance — [NeurIPS 2026 virtual page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/169654).
- Wang, Zhang and Zhang (arXiv 2412.19363, revised January 2025) combine LLM-generated conjoint choices with real data through a statistical augmentation rather than using LLM output alone. "Generative Augmented Inference" (arXiv 2604.14575, 2026) reports GPT-4o barely better than chance on a vaccine conjoint, with embedding-based labels comparable to label-based ones — [arXiv 2412.19363](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19363v2); [arXiv 2604.14575](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.14575) (via search snippets).
- Xuan, Hwang and Lee, "Your Reviews Replicate You" (arXiv 2604.22756, submitted 2026-03-06). They built per-user "customer digital twins" from Reddit review histories with retrieval-augmented generation, had them answer paired comparisons of fractional-factorial monitor profiles, and fit part-worths by logistic regression. The twins predicted actual users' preferences with 87.73% accuracy — [arXiv 2604.22756](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22756).
- Gui and Toubia (arXiv 2312.15524, 2023, revised 2025): LLM-simulated conjoint confounds stated attributes with unstated ones — [arXiv 2312.15524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524) (via search snippet).
- Ansari, Essegaier and Kohli, "Internet Recommendation Systems", JMR 37(3), August 2000, 363-375, DOI 10.1509/jmkr.37.3.363.18779. A Bayesian preference model that statistically integrates several types of information useful for recommendations — [Scilit record](https://www.scilit.com/publications/17f99657c8af85b42466cbea93cc94dd); a scanned copy exists but its text was not extractable.
- Chung, Rust and Wedel, "My Mobile Music: An Adaptive Personalization System for Digital Audio Players", Marketing Science 28(1), 2009, 52-68. It loads personalized playlists with little user effort and no explicit ratings. In simulation and a field test on PDAs it beat benchmarks on songs listened to and listening time — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:28:y:2009:i:1:p:52-68); [EUR repository](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/my-mobile-music-an-adaptive-personalization-system-for-digital-au/) (via search snippet).
- Scholz (BLED 2008) proposed conjoint analysis to measure preferences in product recommender systems, with adaptations — [core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/301347348.pdf) (returned 403; title and gist from search snippet only).
- Huang and Luo (2016) start adaptive conjoint from collaboratively filtered part-worths — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:35:y:2016:i:3:p:445-464).

### Inferences
- Compiani et al. connect the companion's two content arms (researcher's inference). Reduce the synopsis embedding to its first 5 to 10 principal components and give each person shrunk weights on them, exactly as on grouped genome attributes. That puts the embedding arm and the genome arm in one choice model and one harness. The cost is legibility, since a principal component has no name for a reason string. The genome stays the source of reasons, and the embedding components become extra attributes that soak up taste the genome misses, which also reduces the omitted-quality bias described in the previous section.
- TasteNet's structure is the hierarchical model with covariates. A person's prior mean is a function of what they told Verso, such as the onboarding questionnaire (media tracked, maybe a stated taste or two), rather than one population mean. bayesm's unit-level characteristic term is the linear version. It is cheap, and it matters most at cold start, when the prior is nearly the whole estimate.
- Language models as respondents should not stand in for a person's weights (researcher's inference). They reverse attribute rankings and confound attributes, and Verso has each person's real comparisons anyway. A defensible narrow use is a weak prior at cold start, scored in the harness like any other arm. Gui and Toubia's confounding result is also a warning for the genome itself: if a model scores "difficulty" while reading a title it knows to be acclaimed, its scores may carry quality. That is the same omitted-quality problem, entering at the measurement step.
- The recommender precedents (Ansari et al. 2000; Chung et al. 2009) show marketing science built Bayesian, attribute-based personal recommenders two decades ago, which supports the companion's design. No source found compares them with collaborative filtering at modern scale.

### Gaps
- The full text of Ansari, Essegaier and Kohli (2000) is a scanned image, so its data (whether movies or another category), model details and comparison results could not be confirmed. Only its abstract-level description is cited.
- No 2023 to 2026 paper was found in which a language model generates the attribute vocabulary itself for a conjoint study. The closest are embedding-based attributes (Compiani et al.) and LLM respondents.
- No RecSys paper was found that runs hierarchical-Bayes conjoint part-worths as a production recommender scorer; the evidence is marketing-journal field tests.
- ConjointNet's datasets and baselines were not visible on the abstract page.

## Apple v. Samsung: what conjoint was used for

### Takeaway
In the Apple v. Samsung patent litigation, Apple's expert John Hauser (MIT) ran choice-based conjoint surveys of smartphone and tablet buyers. They estimated how much more consumers would pay for features covered by Apple's patents, and Apple used those willingness-to-pay figures to support its damages claims. Samsung's experts attacked the surveys as hypothetical, as leaving out brand and operating system, and as measuring stated rather than market prices.

### Cited Findings
- November 2013 partial retrial on damages (case 11-01846, N.D. Cal.), after Judge Koh struck $450 million of the 2012 jury's $1.05 billion. Hauser testified that consumers would pay about $100 more on a $199 smartphone and about $90 more on a $499 tablet for features of three Apple patents: the rubber-band bounce at the end of scrolling, single- versus multi-touch gesture handling, and tap to re-center after zoom. Apple sought $380 million and Samsung argued for about $52 million — [Macworld, 2013-11-15](https://www.macworld.com/article/222382/apple-experts-seek-to-justify-380-million-damages-claim-against-samsung.html).
- A 2014 account (Quirk's, 2014-05-01, by Rajan Sambandam) of the second case describes two online studies, 507 phone and 459 tablet respondents, run in Sawtooth Software with hierarchical Bayes, covering features including slide to unlock, universal search and automatic word correction. They reported an added willingness to pay of $32 to $102. Under cross-examination Hauser agreed these were stated preferences, not necessarily what Apple could charge in a competitive market. Apple's economist combined them with other data into a damages figure of slightly over $2 billion. Wharton's David Reibstein, for Samsung, faulted the omission of brand, operating system and battery life — [Quirk's](https://www.quirks.com/articles/when-apple-samsung-and-conjoint-came-together).

### Inferences
- The connection to the class (researcher's inference and arithmetic). Willingness to pay in conjoint is a part-worth difference divided by the price coefficient: WTP = Δβ_feature / |β_price|. For example, a feature part-worth of 0.6 with a price coefficient of −0.012 per dollar gives $50; this is an illustration, not Hauser's numbers. Verso has no price attribute, so the analogous "exchange rate" would be in list positions or tier probability, as in "for this person, high difficulty is worth about as much as moving up one tier".
- Samsung's main critique, omitted attributes such as brand, is the conjoint version of the omitted-quality bias Verso faces with real items. The "Apple problem", that an attribute cannot be varied independently of brand, is the same correlated-attributes issue in a designed study.

### Gaps
- The exact jury outcomes of the 2013 retrial and the 2014 trial, and the courts' later treatment of Hauser's surveys on appeal, were not verified in this pass. The Quirk's article does not say whether its 507 and 459 respondent counts belong to the 2012 or the 2014 study; the two cases' figures should be kept apart.
- Which course module uses the case, and what it asks students to compute, comes from the companion report's addendum (Rafa's account), not from a fetched syllabus.
