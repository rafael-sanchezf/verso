# Choosing what to watch tonight: what people use, and what products show

Research notes for the movies Home redesign. Gathered 2026-10-06 by web search, about 20 queries and fetches. Every number carries its source and that source's date. Anything not checked against a primary or near-primary page is marked **unverified**.

## In one paragraph

Choosing takes a long time and often fails. Nielsen measured 10.5 minutes per session in 2023, and one in five viewers who could not find something gave up on watching. Netflix's own data says a member loses interest after 60 to 90 seconds and 10 to 20 titles, of which perhaps 3 get a close look. When people describe how they decide, genre comes first (94% in a Comcast Advertising survey), then mood (63%), then time available (53%), then who they are watching with (42%). Friends' word of mouth is the strongest outside signal for finding a title, but asking a friend at the moment of choice is rare (6%). In the browse view the artwork does most of the work (Netflix: over 82% of browsing focus, about 1.8 seconds per title). Products that offered one random pick dropped it (Netflix's Play Something, low use) or keep it as a side feature (Letterboxd shuffle). The products still in use ask for mood or genre, service, and sometimes length, and show a poster, title, short tags, where it streams, and more and more often a reason. On explanations, MovieLens's 2000 study found that "people similar to you liked this" and "it resembles films you rated highly" raised willingness to watch. Critics' ratings did not, and cluttered explanations did worse than giving no explanation at all.

## 2. Decision-making evidence

1. **Mood management theory** (Zillmann, 1988 onward). People pick media to regulate their mood: stressed or overstimulated people lean to soothing content, bored people to exciting content ([summary, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mood_management_theory)). A frequently repeated finding is that angry or bored viewers chose fewer dramas, calm viewers more comedies and energetic viewers more action. **Unverified**: I could not trace it to the original study. The 63% "depends on my mood" survey figure above is consistent with the theory. Implication: mood is a real input, but it should be asked as a target ("something light", "something gripping"). Asking for a diagnosis ("how do you feel?") is a different question.
2. **Choice overload is conditional.** The famous jam study (Iyengar and Lepper, 2000) did not replicate cleanly. A meta-analysis of 50 experiments found a mean effect near zero (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd, *Journal of Consumer Research*, 2010). A later meta-analysis found overload appears when options are hard to compare, when the task is hard, when preferences are unclear, or when the goal is to pick one quickly (Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 2015). Picking one film on a tired evening matches all four conditions. Citations are from memory and were not re-fetched this session: **unverified links**. Supporting the same point in this domain: Netflix's 60 to 90 seconds and "perhaps 3 in detail", and 25% of undecided Czech viewers "play something random".
3. **Time available.** 53% say the choice depends on their schedule (Comcast, 2024). Netflix's 2025 AI search shows people naturally ask for length in plain words ("a dark mystery that's not too long"; [Tom's Guide](https://tomsguide.com/entertainment/netflix/netflix-is-making-a-massive-change-to-its-homepage-for-the-first-time-in-years), May 2025). I found no academic study that isolates runtime fit in film choice. **Gap.**

Patterns across products:

1. Free-text mood input (Netflix AI search, Tubi) is the newest decision-time input. Genre plus service filters (JustWatch, Plex, Letterboxd Pro) are the oldest.
2. Where to watch appears on every aggregator card (Likewise, Plex, JustWatch). Netflix and Tubi omit it only because the answer is always their own service.
3. A pick with no inputs and no reason failed at Netflix and is a side feature at Letterboxd. Netflix's stated reason was that people arrive with a genre or a title in mind.
4. Netflix uses short labels ("Emmy winner", "Recently added") in place of scores at the moment of decision.

## 4. Explanations

- **Herlocker, Konstan and Riedl, CSCW 2000** (MovieLens; summarised in [Jannach et al., *Recommender Systems: An Introduction*, ch. 6 slides](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/vip/teach/DMcourse/6_graph_analysis/notes_slides/collab_filter/Aggrawal_recomm_systems/Recommender_Systems_An_Introduction_Chapter06_Explanations_in_recommender_systems.pdf)). Participants rated, on a 1 to 7 scale, how likely they were to see a recommended film under 21 explanation styles plus a no-explanation base case. Findings:
  1. Best: neighbour ratings shown simply, as a histogram of how similar people rated it.
  2. Next best: a plain statement of the system's track record.
  3. Among the best: content reasons that tie the film to films the user rated highly, or to a favourite actor.
  4. Poorly designed explanations lowered willingness **below the base case**, and adding more information hurt.
  5. Ratings from critics did **not** increase acceptance.
  The share of users who wanted explanations (a figure of 86% is often quoted) is **unverified**.
- **Tintarev and Masthoff, 2007**: explanations serve seven aims (transparency, scrutability, trust, effectiveness, persuasiveness, efficiency, satisfaction). They warn that persuasive explanations can raise acceptance while lowering trust when they oversell, and that effective explanations, the ones that help people decide well, build trust (same source).
- **Netflix "Because you watched X" rows** are the industry form of a content-similarity reason. Netflix has published no acceptance-lift figure for them that I found. **Unverified.**

## What this implies for a pick card and a finder

### (a) Inputs a finder should ask for, most decisive first

1. **Mood as a target, in one tap** ("light", "gripping", "make me think"...). 63% say the choice depends on mood (Comcast, 2024). Mood management theory predicts that people choose to shift their mood. Netflix and Tubi's newest inputs are free-text mood.
2. **Time available**, which sets a runtime ceiling. 53% say it depends on their schedule (Comcast, 2024). People write "not too long" in plain words into Netflix's AI search (2025).
3. **Who is watching** (alone, partner, friends, family). 42% factor in co-viewers (Comcast, 2024). Co-viewing share for films is likely high, but the 81% figure is unverified.
4. **Services I have**, set once and kept, not asked each night. Every aggregator filters by it (JustWatch, Plex, Letterboxd Pro). 37% cite convenience (Comcast, 2024).
5. **Genre, optional, as a refinement.** 94% call genre key (Comcast, 2024), and Netflix found people arrive with a genre in mind. It overlaps with mood, so offer it as a chip after mood, not as a first step.

Do not ask for: a live poll of friends at choice time (6% ask friends when stuck; atmedia, 2025), or a no-input surprise button as the main path (Netflix removed Play Something for low use).

### (b) Fields a pick card should carry, most decisive first

1. **Poster or key art.** Over 82% of browsing focus and the biggest single influence (Netflix, 2014 research, published 2016).
2. **Title and year.**
3. **The reason, tied to the user's own list** ("because you ranked X in your top tier"). Content-similarity reasons were among the best in Herlocker 2000. Netflix uses "Because you watched" rows.
4. **Runtime.** It answers the second-most-decisive input, time available (53%).
5. **Where it streams**, with a mark for the services the user has. Every aggregator card shows it.
6. **Genre or a short tag** (one or two words, Netflix 2025 style).
7. **Friend signal, only when true** ("Maya ranked it top tier"). Word of mouth leads discovery (YouGov 2023; Conviva, unverified), and Likewise shows friends who liked it.

Leave off:

- **Critic or aggregate scores.** Critics' ratings did not raise acceptance in Herlocker 2000. Netflix shows tags, not scores.
- **Long synopses or cast lists on the card.** Netflix found people review about 3 titles in detail and spend about 1.8 seconds on each. Synopsis and cast belong behind a tap.
- **Dense or numeric explanations** (neighbour counts, similarity scores, match percentages). In Herlocker 2000, more explanation data performed worse than none.
- **More than about three picks at once.** Netflix found members consider about 3 in detail, and choice overload is likely in a tired, single-pick decision (Chernev 2015, link unverified).
