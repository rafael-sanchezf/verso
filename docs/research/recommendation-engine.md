# Recommendation engine: how Beli scores for you, what comparable products do, and the math

Research compiled 2026-09-24 for the recommendation layer of Verso: personal suggestions ("what people who share my taste actually like") and group suggestions ("a film neither of us has seen that we would both like," with genre, runtime, and streaming filters). It extends [beli-ranking-algorithm.md](beli-ranking-algorithm.md), which covers Beli's ranking mechanic and touches recommendations only in its sections 4 and 5, and it corrects two of that file's claims (noted in the Beli section below and applied to that file the same day). The design that follows from it is [../design/recommendation-engine.md](../design/recommendation-engine.md); nothing here is decided.

Five research threads fed this synthesis: Beli's personalization layer (its app copy, press from 2023 to 2026, job postings, App Store reviews), the math of prediction from ranked lists, group recommendation, the engines of comparable products (Criticker, Flickchart, Steam, Netflix, Letterboxd, Goodreads, MovieLens, the game trackers, Spotify Blend), and the LLM-in-the-loop and cross-media literature. Each claim about Beli is labeled Confirmed (Beli's own copy, a founder or employee, or two or more independent sources), Single-source, or Inferred. Formulas are in plain text with every symbol defined; the notation is introduced in the taste-similarity section and holds after it. Model prices were checked on the providers' pages on 2026-09-24. Sources are linked inline.

## Summary

Verso should build personal recommendations as a neighborhood collaborative filter over each user's rank-derived percentiles. Criticker has published this method and run it with a two-person team since 2005. Verso's version should add the three things Criticker lacks: shrinkage for small overlaps, a confidence label, and a content-based fallback. Group recommendations should aggregate each member's personal prediction with one tunable rule, "mean minus a penalty on disagreement," applied after hard filters, and the result should show every member's own number. Beli, the model product, has never published a formula. That it shows a personal Rec score beside an unweighted Friend score is **Confirmed**. Its 10-item and 15-item gates are **Single-source** and date from 2023 and 2024. Everything about its math is **Inferred**: a batch user-user filter, and a Match Score that behaves like a mean-centered correlation, which is how two friends who both loved the same ten places can read -11%. Verso's positional 0 to 10 scores are already normalized per user, so the ranking loop has done the part that star-rating products struggle with. What remains is a baseline, a similarity, a prediction mapped back through the user's own list, and an honest confidence. All of V1 and V2 fits in Postgres with pg_cron, small Edge Functions, and one LLM call that picks three items from about 30 SQL candidates and explains them, at about $0.0035 a request on Claude Haiku 4.5. Factor models wait until items have dozens of rankers. Cross-media transfer helps a user who is new to a medium and should fade as they rank in it, and the one study that tests games is asymmetric: game history improved film picks by 44% and film history hurt game picks by 8%. None of the tuning constants can be settled before there is data, and neither can the question of whether collaborative filtering beats a good baseline at Verso's size. The first build item is therefore a leave-one-out harness and a log of every prediction shown.

## Beli shows two numbers and a percentage, and explains none of them

On any restaurant a user has not ranked, Beli shows two numbers. The **Rec score** is "Beli's prediction of your liking" ([Washington Square News, 2023-10-16](https://nyunews.com/culture/dining/2023/10/16/beli-app/)). The **Friend score** is the plain average of the user's friends' scores, which "averages all of a user's friends' scores for a particular spot" ([Today, 2025-07-17](https://www.today.com/food/trends/what-is-beli-app-rcna217748)). Both are **Confirmed** by several independent sources ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beli_(app)); [Spoon Brown](https://spoonuniversity.com/school/brown/beli-the-new-app-personalizing-restaurant-reviews-and-recommendations/)). The prediction is built from the user's own ratings (**Confirmed**, [Spoon Emory](https://spoonuniversity.com/school/emory/rate-save-and-recommend-restaurants-on-app-beli/); [Long Beach Post, 2023-06-06](https://lbpost.com/esd/hi-lo/get-restaurant-recs-from-the-people-you-trust-most-with-beli/)). It is the default number in search, with no toggle to an average (**Single-source**, a 2026-04-04 review on v8.4.5, [MWM mirror](https://mwm.ai/apps/beli/1478375386)). A third number, the community average, unlocks only after the user invites friends (**Confirmed**, [Valley Magazine, 2025-04-17](https://www.valleymagazinepsu.com/foodies-unite-meet-beli/) and two 2026 App Store reviews). Beli's founders describe an order of trust: "yours, your friends' and then ultimately personalized recommendations" ([InsideHook, 2025-12-16](https://www.insidehook.com/food/does-dining-out-count-beli-app)). Two things a user would want to know are documented nowhere. The Rec score's scale is almost certainly the same 0 to 10 as the user's own list, because every source compares it directly with the Friend score (**Inferred**). And no source, review, or critique quotes any explanation such as "because you liked X" (**Inferred**).

The Match Score is the part most worth studying, because Verso's roadmap already schedules a "match percentage between two users" for V2 (`ROADMAP.md`). Beli's copy invites users to "check out your 'Match Score' with your friends to see who really shares your tastes" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/beli/id1478375386)). Match percentages appear for every user on the leaderboard, and the green ones "encouraged me to follow users with similar tastes" (**Single-source**, [Pratt 2024](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2024/09/design-critique-beli-app/)). A reviewer on v8.4.5 wrote on 2026-03-30 that "my friend has 10 restaurants ranked similarly to me but we have a -11% match" (**Confirmed as a displayed value, single reviewer**, [MWM mirror](https://mwm.ai/apps/beli/1478375386)). Beli has not published the formula. A mean-centered correlation over co-ranked places explains the complaint without assuming a bug (**Inferred**). Suppose A scores five shared places 9.5, 9.0, 8.5, 8.0, 7.5 and B scores them 8.0, 9.5, 7.5, 9.0, 8.5. Both loved all five, yet Pearson r = -0.10, which displays as "-10%". The metric discards the level at which they agree and scores only the order, and the user reads the result as a mistake. At 10 shared items the sampling noise of r is about 0.33, so -11% cannot be told apart from 0 or from +30%, and Beli appears to show the raw value without damping it toward zero (**Inferred**). A hobbyist tool, "Beli Blend," computes 0.7 × restaurant cosine + 0.3 × cuisine cosine, but that is its author's design, not Beli's ([GitHub](https://github.com/trishatjokro/beli-blend)).

The engine behind the Rec score is most likely a batch-computed neighborhood collaborative filter (**Inferred**). Several descriptions use user-user language, such as "Beli looks for users from all over with similar taste for those recommendations—not just people nearby" (**Single-source**, [Long Beach Post](https://lbpost.com/esd/hi-lo/get-restaurant-recs-from-the-people-you-trust-most-with-beli/)). Spoon Emory's "from the ratings you have given similar restaurants" is item-item language. The per-city suppression gate fits too: a neighborhood method switches off when a city has too few co-rankers, while an embedding model would degrade gradually. The company is small. It had "a team of five" in May 2025 ([WSJ via To Vima](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/the-app-that-makes-rating-restaurants-fun-again-and-gets-better-the-more-you-use-it/)) and eight employees plus the two founders by August 2026 ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/04/lifestyle/beli-restaurant-rating-app/)). Its stack is Python and Django, Postgres, and GCP; a senior backend posting mentions "recommendation systems" and names no ML library or vector store ([Beli on Breezy](https://beli.breezy.hr/p/893b5379a9f6-senior-backend-infrastructure-engineer)). A Summer 2026 AI/ML intern posting targets "natural language understanding, content summarization, personalization, or ranking" ([Built In](https://builtin.com/job/ai-ml-engineer-intern-summer-2026/7634925)), so learned models are at least being prototyped. Searches found no patents and no engineering talk.

On inputs, the evidence says cuisine is a soft lean ("based on what types of cuisine you tend to like," **Single-source**, [Zillennial Zine](https://thezillennialzine.com/2024/01/23/beli-app-review/)), while price and location are filters the user applies (**Confirmed**, [Today](https://www.today.com/food/trends/what-is-beli-app-rcna217748)). Dietary settings are not hard filters. One user wrote: "Despite hundreds of entries and dietary restrictions set to vegetarian, my top recommendations are still steakhouses, BBQ places, and chains I've already been to" (**Single-source**, 2026-03-30, [MWM mirror](https://mwm.ai/apps/beli/1478375386)). Beli has not shipped a group recommendation (**Inferred** from absence). Its multi-person features record who you eat with rather than recommending for a group: "Places you both want to try," companion tags, and the "Food Squad" in its 2025 year-end recap ([Spoon University](https://spoonuniversity.com/news/what-your-2025-beli-plated-says-about-you/)). The founders list making it "easier for users to plan their dining experiences" as future work ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/04/lifestyle/beli-restaurant-rating-app/)). The "AI group dining with swipe matching" that search engines attribute to Beli comes from a job-seeker's portfolio clone ([GitHub](https://github.com/tor-iv/beli-app)). Beli's scale shows why the density gate exists: **120 million ratings across 2 million establishments in more than 50,000 cities** by August 2026 ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/04/lifestyle/beli-restaurant-rating-app/)). That is about 60 ratings per place, concentrated in a few big cities. Game, film, TV, and book catalogs are global rather than city-scoped, so Verso reaches useful overlap with far fewer users. For Verso, the long tail of obscure titles plays the role of Beli's small cities.

Two claims in `docs/research/beli-ranking-algorithm.md` need correcting. First, its section 1 (item 8) and section 9 attribute the 15-ranked-restaurant gate to NYU Washington Square News through a search snippet. Read directly, that article states no threshold. The verbatim source is the Long Beach Post of 2023-06-06, "with enough data (at least 15 ranked restaurants), the app can then begin to suggest personalized recommendations," so the claim is **Single-source, 2023** ([Long Beach Post](https://lbpost.com/esd/hi-lo/get-restaurant-recs-from-the-people-you-trust-most-with-beli/)). The same article is where the new notes found the "users from all over" quote, which the prior file had also taken from an NYU WSN snippet. Second, its section 5 marks the "-11% match" figure as unverified, and its complaints table lists "match percentages seem inaccurate" as a search summary. Both are now verified verbatim from courtneyjchen's App Store review of 2026-03-30 (v8.4.5), and the figure should be labeled Confirmed as a displayed value from a single reviewer.

## Criticker published the whole algorithm, and the rest of the field converges on it

Criticker is the closest public analog to a Beli-style Rec score plus Match Score, and it publishes every step. Each rating becomes a percentile of the user's own list, because "a rating of 75 can mean something different for each user." Two users' **Taste Compatibility Index (TCI)** is the mean absolute percentile gap over the titles both have rated. In Criticker's example, "If my percentile for Miller's Crossing is 68, and yours is 85, that's a difference of 17," and lower is closer: "a TCI of 10.67 is really good" ([Criticker, How Criticker Works, Wayback 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://www.criticker.com/explain/)). Matching starts at **10 ratings**. A TCI needs **at least 3 titles in common**, and "this three-title minimum raises, the more ratings you have." By default only users sharing **at least 10% of your titles** are considered, and the user can adjust that from 10% to 50%. Criticker stores each user's top 1,000 TCIs.

The **Probable Score Index (PSI)** averages the percentiles of your 10 closest users who rated the title. It exists only if **at least 3 of your top 1,000** rated it, and the average is then mapped back onto your own scale: an average percentile of 56.33 becomes a PSI of 77 for one user ([Criticker's Algorithm Explained, Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://www.criticker.com/critickers-algorithm-explained/)). Criticker says "recommendations really start improving after 100" ratings ([Criticker FAQ, Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://www.criticker.com/help/)). It shows all the math behind every number, and it is "developed and maintained by a team of just two people."

Criticker's published percentile formula has a misplaced parenthesis. Its own example, a new 85 with 195 lower and 10 equal out of 251 landing at the 79th percentile, only works as floor(((num_lower + num_matching/2) / total) × 100), the standard mid-rank form. Its second example is slightly inconsistent too: 46% of 251 is 115.5, not the 117 printed.

Criticker also shows what to improve. It does not shrink small overlaps, so a 3-title TCI can outrank a 60-title one. It takes an unweighted mean of the top 10 neighbors. It shows no confidence, and it has no content fallback.

Across products, the minimum-data gate for personal predictions clusters at **10 to 20 rated items**. Criticker adds a second gate per item: at least three supporting neighbors. The comparables teach three further lessons.

Popularity has to be blended in, in small doses. Netflix warns that "using predicted ratings on their own as a ranking function can lead to items that are too niche or unfamiliar being recommended," and ranks by w1·popularity + w2·predicted rating + b ([Netflix TechBlog, Part 2](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://netflixtechblog.com/netflix-recommendations-beyond-the-5-stars-part-2-d9b96aa399f5)). MovieLens v4 blends in popularity as well. When GroupLens gave users the controls, **85% moved away from the default**, the median chosen popularity weight was 0.021 (IQR 0.000 to 0.193), and "there is no globally optimal setting that works for all users" ([Harper et al., RecSys 2015](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/harper-recsys2015.pdf)). Steam exposes a "mainstream-ness" slider for the same reason ([Steam, 2019](https://store.steampowered.com/news/posts/?feed=steam_blog&enddate=1563000000)).

A number without a reason does not age well. Netflix's personal % match launched in 2017, alongside thumbs that replaced stars after 2016 tests showed they drew "200% more ratings" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/03/16/netflix-is-replacing-five-star-ratings-with-thumbs-up-or-down/)). In January 2024 Netflix said it would "likely" drop the percentage in favor of descriptive tags ([MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2024/01/17/netflix-content-match-percentages-tags-report/)).

Two models blended beat a big ensemble. Netflix deployed only SVD (RMSE 0.8914) and an RBM (0.8990), blended linearly to 0.88. It skipped the Grand Prize ensemble because "the additional accuracy gains... did not seem to justify the engineering effort" ([Netflix TechBlog, Part 1](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://netflixtechblog.com/netflix-recommendations-beyond-the-5-stars-part-1-55838468f429)).

The game trackers leave the category open. Backloggd's Discovery Engine, in open beta in mid-2025, "bubbles up highly rated games you haven't played" ([Backloggd on X](https://x.com/BackloggdApp/status/1937871015960359201)). SavePoint's paid Decision Engine "picks three games for you to play tonight given your time and mood" from the backlog ([SavePoint FAQ](https://savepoint.twoaveragegamers.com/faq)). The research found no "people who rank like you" filter and no taste match in any of the dedicated trackers. Letterboxd has no official recommendations either. The third-party tools that fill the gap report the expected failure: Sam Learner's SVD "tends to recommend very popular movies often, regardless of an individual user's taste" ([GitHub](https://github.com/sdl60660/letterboxd_recommendations)). Among the trackers found, Verso would be the first with a real similar-taste recommender and a first-party taste match.

## Taste similarity: count agreeing head-to-heads, shrink hard, and draw a chance line users can read

### Positional scores arrive normalized, with three distortions to correct

The notation below holds for the rest of the report. u and v are users. i, j, and x are items within one medium; similarities never mix media, which also follows Verso's rule that each medium is its own product. I_u is the set of items u has ranked, and n_u is its size. I_uv is the set both users have ranked (co-ranked), and n_uv is its size. s_ui is u's derived 0 to 10 score for i, and pos_ui is its 1-based position in u's whole list, with 1 the best. mu_u is u's mean score.

Positional scores are rank-normalized per user. That removes harsh-rater and generous-rater habits, which is Criticker's whole argument for percentiles. Verso's actual bands are loved 8.5 to 10, liked 6.5 to 8.4, fine 4 to 6.4, and did not like 0 to 3.9. The score is interpolated linearly within the band, and a one-item tier sits at the band midpoint (`packages/core/src/model.ts`, `scoreForPosition` in `packages/core/src/ranking.ts`).

The construction has three distortions. Small tiers are forced to span their band: a two-item loved tier reads 10.0 and 8.5 even if the user sees the two as equal. Equal spacing ignores "too close" answers. And one user's "fine" may be another user's "liked." Three cheap corrections handle them:

```
pct_ui  = (n_u - pos_ui + 0.5) / n_u                  overall mid-rank percentile, never exactly 0 or 1
q_ui    = (n_t - k + 0.5) / n_t                        within-tier percentile; k = 1-based position in tier t, n_t = tier size
s*_ui   = m_t + (s_ui - m_t) * n_t / (n_t + kappa)     shrink small tiers toward the band midpoint m_t; kappa about 2 to 3
```

The mid-rank form is preferred over Postgres's percent_rank(), which pins the ends of the list to exactly 0 and 1. The code already applies the kappa shrinkage in its extreme case, a single item at the midpoint; for larger tiers kappa is a guess to tune. For modeling, use either pct_ui or the pair (tier, q_ui), and keep s_ui for display.

### Rank agreement is the natural similarity for a head-to-head product

The math notes computed every candidate measure on the same example: two users who co-ranked eight games. The tier bands in this example are illustrative, not Verso's.

| item | s_A | tier A | s_B | tier B |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 9.6 | loved | 7.5 | liked |
| G2 | 8.9 | loved | 9.8 | loved |
| G3 | 7.8 | liked | 8.6 | loved |
| G4 | 7.1 | liked | 5.2 | fine |
| G5 | 6.4 | liked | 6.9 | liked |
| G6 | 5.5 | fine | 6.1 | liked |
| G7 | 4.2 | fine | 2.0 | disliked |
| G8 | 1.5 | disliked | 4.5 | fine |

```
pearson(u,v) = sum over i in I_uv of (s_ui - mu_u)(s_vi - mu_v)
               / ( sqrt(sum (s_ui - mu_u)^2) * sqrt(sum (s_vi - mu_v)^2) )     means taken over I_uv, as Postgres corr() does
tau(u,v)     = (n_c - n_d) / (n_uv * (n_uv - 1) / 2)                          n_c, n_d = concordant and discordant item pairs
agreement    = n_c / (n_c + n_d) = (1 + tau) / 2 when there are no ties
```

Pearson on the positional scores gives **r = 0.739**. Spearman gives 0.833. Kendall's tau gives **0.643**: of 28 pairs, 23 are concordant and 5 discordant (G1-G2, G1-G3, G4-G5, G4-G6, G7-G8). The **concordance rate is 82.1%**, which reads as "you would answer 82% of head-to-heads between these eight games the same way." That is why tau is the natural similarity for a head-to-head product. Coarser measures fail at this overlap. Exact tier agreement is 2 of 8, and the Jaccard overlap of the two loved sets is 1/3, so moving a single item one tier swings Jaccard by 0.33. Rank-biased overlap answers a different question, whether two top-k lists coincide, and belongs on each user's whole list rather than on the co-ranked set. The mean absolute score difference is 1.5 points, which is Criticker's TCI on a 0 to 10 scale ([Surprise docs](https://surprise.readthedocs.io/en/stable/similarities.html); [Kendall tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendall_rank_correlation_coefficient); [Webber et al. 2010](http://blog.mobile.codalism.com/research/papers/wmz10_tois.pdf)).

### At 5 to 15 shared items, every raw similarity is mostly noise

The 95% Fisher interval for r = 0.739 at n = 8 is **0.07 to 0.95**. Under independence, the standard deviation of tau is 0.41 at 5 shared items, 0.29 at 8, 0.25 at 10, 0.19 at 15, 0.13 at 30, and 0.10 at 50. Below about 10 overlaps a similarity is mostly noise, and below 5 it should not be computed at all. The fix is shrinkage toward zero by overlap:

```
sim'(u,v) = sim(u,v) * n_uv / (n_uv + beta)
```

Here beta is the overlap at which a similarity counts half. The published constants come from Netflix scale and do not transfer. Koren's typical shrinkage is 100 ([Koren 2010](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i290-dm/s11/SECURE/a1-koren.pdf)), Surprise's default is 100, and Herlocker's significance weighting uses min(n, 50)/50 ([survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.04584)). At n = 8, Koren's form multiplies r by 0.065 and Herlocker's by 0.16, which zeroes out every pair Verso will have. **beta = 10 gives a factor of 0.444, so r' = 0.33 and tau' = 0.29.** That starting value is an educated guess to be refit by leave-one-out, not a published number. Negative similarities are "less reliable than positive ones" and "provide no significant improvement in the prediction accuracy" in the experiments the survey reviews, so neighbor selection should keep positive similarities only ([survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.04584)).

### The displayed Match should put chance at 50%, show its overlap, and list agreements

There are three honest ways to turn a similarity into a displayed percentage, and all three are computable in SQL. Shrunk head-to-head agreement puts chance at 50%: match = 50 + 50 × tau_b × n_uv / (n_uv + beta). For the example that is 50 + 50 × 0.643 × 0.444 = **64%**, which reads as "you pick the same winner 64% of the time, adjusted for how few games you share," and it can never go negative. A chance-normalized distance puts chance at 0%: match = 100 × (1 - MAD_uv / MAD_chance) × shrink, where MAD_uv is the users' mean absolute score gap and MAD_chance is the gap between random pairs of users. MAD_chance is 3.33 for independent uniform 0 to 10 scores and should be measured from Verso's data. The example scores 55% before shrinkage, and reversed lists go to -50%. The third option is plain 100 × r'.

Beli's negative values are most plausibly a chance-at-zero scale on a correlation (**Inferred**). Verso should use the first form and hide the number below 5 shared items. It should show the overlap count beside the percentage. And it should list the items the two users agree and clash on, which is Criticker's transparency move and what the third-party Letterboxd compatibility tools display. The last guard against Beli's failure is level agreement. Two friends who both loved the same ten games should not read as strangers, so either blend in tier agreement (1 - mean absolute tier gap / 3 = 0.75 in the example) or use the "you both loved" list as the headline.

In SQL, Pearson is one aggregate: select the user pair, count(*), and corr(a.score, b.score) from a self-join of ranked items on item and medium, having count(*) >= 5. Kendall needs an item-pair self-join per user pair, which costs O(n_uv²) rows. That is trivial at hundreds of users. At 10,000 users, precompute tau for friend pairs and for top candidate neighbors only.

## Personal prediction: a shrunk baseline, a few trusted neighbors, and a score read off the user's own list

### A shrunk baseline is the floor every model has to beat

Build the baseline before any collaborative model and keep it as the fallback. Koren's Netflix results are the warning: the classic correlation-based neighborhood model "could not improve much upon a nonpersonalized scheme" on top-K ranking ([Koren 2010](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i290-dm/s11/SECURE/a1-koren.pdf)). In a reproducibility study, only 7 of 18 recent neural recommenders could be reproduced, and 6 of those 7 were often beaten by simple nearest-neighbor or graph heuristics ([Ferrari Dacrema et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902)).

```
m_x  = (n_x * avg_x + C * g) / (n_x + C)                            community prior for item x
b_ui = mu + b_u + b_i                                               baseline for user u on item i
b_i  = sum over raters u of i of (s_ui - mu) / (lambda2 + |R(i)|)
b_u  = sum over items i of u of (s_ui - mu - b_i) / (lambda3 + |R(u)|)
```

In the community prior, n_x is the number of Verso users who ranked x, avg_x is their mean score, g is the global mean, and C is a pseudo-count between 5 and 20. When n_x is small, fall back to external scores already mirrored in `external_scores` (IGDB, Steam, OpenCritic) or TMDB's, rescaled to 0 to 10 by a quantile map fitted on items that have both. This is the Bayesian average Flickchart adopted in 2010 and Backloggd in 2025. Flickchart's first version let a film "seen by 3 people" rank 7,000 places above The Phantom Menace, so C needs tuning ([Flickchart blog](https://web.archive.org/web/2016/http://www.flickchart.com/blog/the-global-rankings-2-rankings-reloaded/); [Backloggd, March 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://backloggd.medium.com/developer-update-march-2025-3e0601ac9efc)).

In the baseline, mu is the global mean, R(i) is the set of i's raters, and R(u) is u's items. Koren's Netflix values are lambda2 = 25 and lambda3 = 10. At Verso's scale, start near 5 and 3 and tune.

### User-user neighbors first, damped so one weak neighbor cannot swing the score

With dozens to hundreds of users, user-user beats item-item: item-item wins when users greatly outnumber items, and Verso will have fewer users than catalog items ([survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.04584)). Neither method needs retraining when a user ranks something new ([Koren 2010](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i290-dm/s11/SECURE/a1-koren.pdf)). The recommended form works on baseline residuals and damps the denominator:

```
pred_ux = b_ux + sum over v in N_x(u) of sim'(u,v) * (s_vx - b_vx)
                 / ( sum over v in N_x(u) of |sim'(u,v)| + lambda )
```

N_x(u) is the set of u's top neighbors, up to 30, with sim' > 0, who ranked x. The prediction requires at least 3 of them (Criticker's floor). lambda, between 0.5 and 2, is the prior's weight in similarity units. The damped denominator is a common practitioner form, but no primary source for it was located, so lambda is a tuning knob.

A worked micro-example uses the simplest baseline, b = mu_u. User A has mu_A = 6.375 and has not ranked X. Three neighbors ranked it:

| Neighbor | sim' | mu | score on X | deviation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B | 0.33 | 6.3 | 8.8 | +2.5 |
| C | 0.50 | 5.8 | 7.9 | +2.1 |
| D | 0.20 | 6.9 | 6.0 | -0.9 |

The numerator is 1.695 and the sum of |sim'| is 1.03. Undamped, the prediction is 6.375 + 1.646 = **8.02**. Damped with lambda = 1, it is 6.375 + 1.695/2.03 = **7.21**; lambda = 0.5 gives 7.48 and lambda = 2 gives 6.93. Confidence comes from the same numbers. The effective neighbor count is n_eff = (sum |sim'|)² / sum sim'² = 2.66. The similarity-weighted SD of the deviations is 1.26, so the standard error is 1.26 / sqrt(2.66) = **0.77**, or "8.0 ± 1.5" at about 95%.

### Content profiles carry cold start, and a precision-weighted blend hands over to neighbors

A content profile needs no other users and becomes usable after about 5 to 10 ranked items. That count is an inference and should be confirmed by the harness. The profile also writes the reason line.

```
idf(f)   = ln(N_items / n_f)                                          n_f = catalog items with feature f
w_ui     = s_ui - mu_u
P_u[f]   = sum over i in I_u of w_ui * x_i[f] / sum over i in I_u of |w_ui|
a_ux     = cosine(P_u, x_x)                                           in [-1, 1]
pred_ux  = (w0 * prior_ux + wc * content_ux + wk * knn_ux) / (w0 + wc + wk)
w0 = 1,  wc = n_u / (n_u + kc) * rel_c,  wk = sum over neighbors of |sim'|
```

x_i[f] is 1 when item i has feature f and 0 otherwise. Features include genre, developer or director, cast, tags, platform family, and decade, and a feature enters the profile only if it appears on at least two of the user's items. content_ux is a_ux mapped onto u's scale. kc is about 10. rel_c is the content model's leave-one-out reliability for this user.

With no neighbors, wk = 0 and the prediction is content plus prior. As neighbors accumulate, kNN takes over without any switch in code. This is an inverse-variance blend. Its weights can later be learned by linear regression on leave-one-out predictions, the small-team version of Netflix's two-model blend. LightFM's hybrid, which represents users and items as sums of feature embeddings, is the grown-up form: it "outperforms both collaborative and content-based models in cold-start or sparse interaction data scenarios" ([Kula 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.08439)).

### Map every prediction back through the user's own list, and sort conservatively

Criticker's key move is to predict a percentile and then read the score that percentile has in the user's own list: "Criticker is simply translating percentile back to rating" ([explain](https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://www.criticker.com/explain/)). In Verso terms, take the predicted percentile pct_hat, find the insertion position pos_hat = round((1 - pct_hat) × n_u) + 1, and return the score that position would receive. The number then means "if you played it, it would probably land about here, between X and Y." That gives the predicted tier for free, and it matches the design doc's rule to show neighbors rather than bare numbers (`docs/design/ranking-algorithm.md`).

Attach a confidence label, not a raw interval. "High" means SE < 0.75 with n_eff ≥ 5. "Medium" means SE between 0.75 and 1.5. "Needs more data" means n_eff < 3, SE > 1.5, or n_u < 10. Sort recommendation lists by pred - z × SE with z about 1, the recommendation analog of TrueSkill's mu - 3 sigma ([TrueSkill](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/trueskill-ranking-system/)). Shrinkage compresses predictions toward the mean. If leave-one-out shows that, fit a per-medium linear or isotonic recalibration and apply it before display.

### The comparison log refines the order but does not replace it, and the social layer does not need Option C

Bradley-Terry models the chance that i beats j as P_u(i beats j) = sigmoid(theta_ui - theta_uj), where theta is a latent strength and sigmoid(z) = 1/(1 + e^(-z)). Thurstone swaps in a normal curve. Elo and TrueSkill are online approximations of the same idea. Fit per user on Verso's log, these models are degenerate. Binary insertion asks only questions consistent with the current order, the top item never loses, and the bottom item never wins. A unique maximum-likelihood fit needs every item to both win and lose, so it does not exist here ([Bradley-Terry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradley%E2%80%93Terry_model)). With a prior the fit does exist, but it only reproduces a smoothed version of the positions, with spacing set by the insertion path rather than by strength of preference.

The log's real value lies elsewhere. "Too close" answers are genuine near-ties that equal spacing throws away; they can be used as ties in tau-b or through Davidson's tie term ([Davidson-Luce](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.07123)). "Can't compare" answers mark items that are not substitutes. Revisions over time flag unstable preferences. Most useful of all, the log is a free held-out test set of real binary judgments. Pooling across users also works: item strength plus a per-user low-rank offset, sigmoid((beta_i + p_u·q_i) - (beta_j + p_u·q_j)), is pairwise logistic matrix factorization, the BPR family ([Rendle et al. 2009](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)), and the pooled beta_i is a scale-free community strength.

This amends one expectation in `docs/design/ranking-algorithm.md`, which says to revisit Option C "when the social layer needs predicted scores and match percentages." The social layer does not need Option C. Predictions and Match come straight from positional percentiles. A latent model earns its place only as a pooled, cross-user model at V3 scale.

Two independent results support investing in the log anyway. Pairwise comparisons are "almost 20% more stable over time" than ratings ([Jones, Brun and Boyer 2011](https://doi.org/10.1109/wi-iat.2011.13)). And an online A/B test found that adding pairwise preferences to ratings "can generate better recommendations than a state of the art rating-only based solution" ([Kalloori, Ricci et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3240323.3240364)).

### Factor models wait for rankers per item, not for compute

At 10,000 users with about 100 items each (about 1 million rankings, 32 factors), one ALS half-sweep costs about 1.0e9 + 3.3e8 floating-point operations, which is seconds on one CPU core. BPR for 50 epochs is a few minutes in numpy ([Hu, Koren and Volinsky 2008](http://yifanhu.net/PUB/cf.pdf)), and no GPU is needed. Data is the binding constraint, not compute. A user with 100 ranked items supplies about 400 to 600 logged comparisons and 4,950 implied pairs, so per-user volume is ample. What is scarce is rankers per item: a 10 to 20 factor model needs well over that many rankers per item. No source gives a threshold at which factorization beats kNN, so "hundreds to low thousands of active users per medium" is an inference.

When the time comes, run training as a nightly Python job on a GitHub Actions schedule, which is how the IGDB mirror already runs, and write the factors back to Postgres. Edge Functions only serve dot products; they do not train.

### Evaluate with leave-one-out from day one

For each user and each ranked item, remove the item, recompute that user's scores and similarities, predict, and compare with the true score and tier. Report per-user MAE on the 0 to 10 scale, tier accuracy, pairwise accuracy on the logged comparisons, and coverage (the share of predictions that did not fall back). Also report NDCG@10 with loved items as relevant, ranked against all candidates rather than sampled negatives, because sampled metrics "do not persist relative statements, e.g., 'algorithm A is better than B'" ([Rendle](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02263)). Beat four baselines in order: the global mean, the user mean, the community prior mapped onto the user's scale, and b_ui. With 20 users, test the per-user differences with a paired bootstrap, 10,000 resamples of users. Only large effects, around 0.3 to 0.5 points of MAE, will be detectable.

## Cold start and cross-media transfer: borrow from content, then let each medium's rankings take over

The gates follow from the evidence above. Per user and per medium: show content-plus-prior scores from about 5 ranked items with a low-confidence label, and collaborative scores from 10, which is Criticker's start. Beli's 15 sits inside that range and is a reasonable single number for the "recommendations unlocked" UI gate. Per pair of users: compute a similarity only when n_uv ≥ 5 and n_uv is at least 10% of the smaller list. The 10% rule stops a 300-item user from matching a 6-item user on five blockbusters. Per prediction: require at least 3 contributing neighbors, otherwise fall back to content plus prior with "needs more data." Beli's city corresponds to Verso's medium. Ranked games should not count toward the film gate, and a sparse medium should visibly degrade to content and priors rather than switch off.

Onboarding is where the gate gets cleared quickly. MovieLens required 5 ratings at v0 and 15 at v3, with the v3 titles "selected for their popularity, excluding the most popular 50-150 movies." At v4 it dropped the requirement for a group-based elicitation flow ([Harper and Konstan 2015](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/harper-tiis2015.pdf)). In Rashid et al.'s live test with 351 new users, the old strategy "required over three times the effort of the best strategies, Popularity and Item-Item personalized" ([Rashid et al. 2002](https://cs.fit.edu/~pkc/apweb/related/rashid-iui02.pdf)). For Verso, the bulk entry in `docs/feature-ideas.md` (idea 1) is the onboarding. It should offer titles the user is likely to have played or watched, which means popular titles minus the very top, spread across genres, and it should switch to item-similar suggestions once a few are ranked. Head-to-head ranking needs at least two items per tier before the comparisons carry information.

Cross-domain signal reliably helps a user who is new to the target domain. Adding an Xbox movie and TV view lifted app-recommendation precision@1 for new users by 115% (0.142 to 0.306) and news by 101% ([Elkahky et al. 2015](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2015/documents/proceedings/proceedings/p278.pdf)). Using music listening to recommend podcasts to cold-start users raised consumption by up to 50% ([Spotify, SIGIR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13287)). The benefit shrinks as target-domain history grows. With equal data, cross-domain predictions "are less precise than those based on the same amount of preferences expressed in the target domain," though they add serendipity and diversity ([Winoto and Tang 2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00354-008-0041-0), as summarized in [Cantador et al.](http://arantxa.ii.uam.es/~cantador%20/doc/2012/ceri12a.pdf)). How related the two domains are decides whether transfer helps at all ([Sahebi and Brusilovsky 2015](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2792838.2800188)).

The one study with games shows asymmetric transfer. Video Games → Movies & TV raised hit@1 from 0.2269 to 0.3272 (+44%), while Movies & TV → Video Games lowered it from 0.2548 to 0.2344 (-8%). Pairs across distant subgroups were "detrimental" ([LLM4CDR, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07761)). The plausible reading is that game taste carries tone and theme a film can match, while film taste says nothing about mechanics such as roguelike versus open world. That reading rests on Amazon category data, not on any tracker. No study measures individual-level game-film correlation.

The bridges that fit one developer are content-level, because Verso has no cross-media co-consumption data yet. The first is a shared theme and mood vocabulary of 40 to 80 tags, including tone tags aligned with the five cross-media taste dimensions found in more than 3,000 people ("Communal, Aesthetic, Dark, Thrilling, and Cerebral," [Rentfrow et al. 2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-6494.2010.00662.x)). An offline LLM batch job maps IGDB themes, TMDB keywords, and book subjects into it and stores item_tags(item_id, tag, weight). The second is a table of creator, franchise, and adaptation links (item_links), which gives high-precision, explainable picks. The third is one text-embedding model over synopsis plus tags for every medium, stored in pgvector. The user vector is the mean of their top-tier items, and a nearest-neighbor query into the other medium returns candidates. The LLM then names the shared trait over candidates that came from these three bridges, which is LLM4CDR's "recommendation guidance" with grounding kept intact. A collaborative cross-medium signal ("people who loved game X loved film Y") comes last, once overlap exists. Simple aggregation is the baseline it has to beat ([Handbook, 2022](https://shlomo-berkovsky.github.io/files/pdf/Springer22c.pdf)).

Weight the cross-medium score by w_cross = k / (k + n_target), where n_target is the number of items ranked in the target medium and k is about 5 to 10, so it fades as the user ranks films. Present it as a labeled slot ("Because of your games"), so a weaker fit reads as a deliberate stretch. That fits rule 24's "same loop, own product": no medium's list or similarity is merged with another's. Cross-media products such as TasteDive and Qloo, Likewise, and the defunct Jinni publish no accuracy numbers for game-to-film transfer ([Qloo acquires TasteDive](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/qloo-the-leading-artificial-intelligence-platform-for-culture-and-taste-acquires-tastedive-300794951.html); [Jinni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinni_(search_engine))).

## Group picks: filter hard, shrink the guest, penalize disagreement, show everyone's number

Nobody in the comparable set does taste-aware group picks for films or games. Beli has not shipped one. Netflix answered the household problem in 2013 with per-person profiles ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/08/01/netflix-user-profiles/)). Letterboxd offers watchlist intersection with "Hide watched films" ([Letterboxd help](https://letterboxd.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/15179318694287-Can-I-compare-my-watchlist-with-another-member-s)), and Steam tools stop at "games you both own" ([Tildes](https://tildes.net/~games/1k9m/is_there_a_tool_method_to_find_games_you_have_in_common_with_someone_else)). Swipe apps such as Reelgood's Swipe With Friends (150+ services, "no account needed. Just share a url") and Matched aggregate by unanimous approval over a filtered catalog and use little personal history ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/2021/01/16/swipe-with-friends-is-like-tinder-for-finding-movies-and-shows-to-watch/); [Matched](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/matched-movie-app-for-couples/id1623287922)). Spotify's Blend is the best-documented two-person design. It chose "maximizing the joy," each person's most relevant tracks with equal representation, over "minimize the misery" ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2021/12/a-look-behind-blend-the-personalized-playlist-for-youand-you)). That works for a playlist, where each person gets many turns, and not for one film. The only large deployed movie-group study, PolyLens on MovieLens, found that **257 of 338 groups were pairs**, that 95% of surveyed users were satisfied, and that 77% found group recommendations more helpful than individual ones for choosing a movie ([PolyLens 2001](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/poly-camera-final.pdf)).

Every rule below scores five films neither member has seen. s(u,i) is member u's predicted 0 to 10 score for item i.

| Film | s(A) | s(B) | Average | Least misery | Most pleasure | Product | mean - 0.25·abs diff |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | 8.5 | 6.0 | 7.25 | 6.0 | 8.5 | 51.0 | 6.625 |
| F2 | 7.0 | 7.0 | 7.00 | **7.0** | 7.0 | 49.0 | **7.000** |
| F3 | 9.5 | 4.0 | 6.75 | 4.0 | **9.5** | 38.0 | 5.375 |
| F4 | 6.0 | 9.0 | **7.50** | 6.0 | 9.0 | **54.0** | 6.750 |
| F5 | 5.0 | 5.5 | 5.25 | 5.0 | 5.5 | 27.5 | 5.125 |

Average picks F4. Least misery picks F2. Most pleasure picks F3, the film B is predicted to dislike. The product rule, which is the geometric mean or Nash product, picks F4. The rank-based rules break down with two voters: Borda ties F1, F2, and F4 at 5 points each, Copeland reduces to counting Pareto dominance and ties the same three, and plurality splits 1 to 1. With two voters these rules are tie-prone and throw away intensity, so use them only as tie-breakers.

For a pair the score-based rules collapse into one family, and that collapse is the most useful fact for a two-person product. For two members, min(a,b) = mean - |a - b|/2 and max(a,b) = mean + |a - b|/2, so:

```
S_lambda(i) = mean(i) - lambda * |a - b|        lambda in [-0.5, 0.5]
```

Here mean(i) is the average of the members' scores for item i, and a and b are the two members' scores. lambda = 0 is Average, 0.5 is Least Misery, and -0.5 is Most Pleasure. Amer-Yahia's relevance-plus-disagreement consensus with weights w1 = 0.8 and w2 = 0.2 is lambda = 0.25 ([Amer-Yahia et al. 2009](http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol2/vldb09-858.pdf)). The product rule is the same idea with a squared penalty: a × b = mean² - (a - b)²/4. F2 overtakes F4 at lambda = 1/6. At **lambda = 0.2** the ranking is F2 7.00, F4 6.90, F1 6.75, F3 5.65, F5 5.15. For groups larger than two, use mean - lambda × stddev_pop. Least misery gets harsher as the group grows, because the chance that someone scores an item low rises with n.

The evidence on what people prefer points to the middle of this range. In Masthoff's experiments, people aggregating by hand used Average, Average Without Misery, and Least Misery, and cared about avoiding individual misery ([Masthoff 2004](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/B:USER.0000010138.79319.fd)). Search summaries of her later chapter report that the multiplicative rule best satisfied all individuals; that is a secondary source ([Masthoff 2015](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4899-7637-6_22)). In a preregistered study of **399 participants**, Most Pleasure received clearly worse ratings for fairness, consensus, and satisfaction, and the level of explanation had no effect ([Barile et al. 2021](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2955/paper11.pdf)). PolyLens used pure least misery, and "several users disliked the 'minimize misery' policy" ([PolyLens](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/poly-camera-final.pdf)). Amer-Yahia's study found "incorporating disagreements is critical." The feature-ideas sketch requires "a floor for every member rather than a maximum for one." That is Average Without Misery, and the research endorses it with one refinement: a moderate penalty (lambda about 0.2) plus the floor.

Aggregate predictions, not profiles. A merged pseudo-user "leads to recommendations biased toward users with more ratings," and its group score "may lie outside the range of any individual predictions" ([PolyLens](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/poly-camera-final.pdf)). For Verso's typical pair, a 200-film heavy user and a 5-pick guest, the merged profile would simply be the heavy user's. Aggregating predictions keeps the explanation and the fairness on each member's side. There is one plumbing consequence. If the personal recommender stores only each user's top-K, a disliked film has no row, and the misery rule cannot see the misery. Group queries therefore need every member's prediction for the whole eligible candidate set, computed on demand for the filtered candidates in an Edge Function or precomputed for a catalog subset.

Confidence should change the estimate, not the vote. The member on the couch counts as one person however much data Verso has on them. First normalize each member to their own scale, which answers PolyLens's complaint that "Mark's '5' is Dan's '3'." Then shrink uncertain predictions toward an item prior mu(i) and aggregate with equal weights:

```
s'(u,i) = mu(i) + c_u * (s(u,i) - mu(i)),   c_u = n_u / (n_u + k)
```

n_u is the number of items the member has ranked, or picked if they are a guest, and k is a pseudo-count of about 10. Worked on F1 with mu(F1) = 7.0 and k = 10: A has 200 items, so c_A = 0.952 and s'(A) = **8.43**. Guest B has 5 picks, so c_B = 0.333 and s'(B) = **6.67**. At lambda = 0.25 the pair score is 7.55 - 0.25 × 1.76 = **7.11**, against 6.625 raw. A guest's raw 9.5 would shrink to 7.83, so the guest's strong like counts but cannot dominate. The tempting alternative, weighting votes by confidence, gives (0.952 × 8.5 + 0.333 × 6.0) / 1.285 = **7.85**, which is essentially A deciding alone. No published study tests shrinkage against weighting for unequal-data members, so this is a principled inference.

For the misery veto, apply the floor to the shrunk scores. A default floor is the 30th percentile of each member's own scale, 3.0 in 0 to 10 percentile units. Show a high-risk film with an explicit "not sure Sam will like this" flag rather than dropping it silently. A lower-confidence-bound veto would give the uncertain guest more veto power, and the shrunk-mean veto gives them less. Which is right depends on logged outcomes. For the probability that a member is miserable, P_u = Phi((t - s)/sigma), where Phi is the normal cumulative distribution, t the misery threshold, and sigma the prediction's standard error. It fits in one Postgres expression through the logistic approximation 1/(1 + exp(-1.702x)). Example: B at s = 6.0 with sigma = 2.0 and t = 5.0 gives about 0.31.

The guest link in the sketch holds up with specifics. Show a grid of 24 to 40 recognizable titles spread across genres and eras, because pure entropy selection surfaces films people have not seen ([Rashid et al. 2002](https://cs.fit.edu/~pkc/apweb/related/rashid-iui02.pdf)). Collect 5 to 10 favorites and an optional row of "hard no" genres that become filters. Score the guest through item-item similarity to their picks, or by folding them into a factor model once one exists, with low confidence and a session-scoped guest id. User-user neighbors are too thin for 5 picks, since Criticker needs 3 shared titles just to compare. Invitations are worth building. PolyLens members sent 391 invitations to non-members and 95 were accepted. The invitees who joined made 872 recommendation requests in their first 30 days against 459 for a control group of new users, and 47% returned after the first visit against 32% ([PolyLens](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/poly-camera-final.pdf)).

Hard filters run before scoring, as SQL WHERE clauses. The first is "unseen by everyone present," which PolyLens and Amer-Yahia both require. Next comes availability on "a service one of us has," the union of members' providers in the session's region, read from Verso's `watch_availability` table (subscription, free, or ad-supported offers only, and not removed). An intersection-only toggle covers members watching from different homes. Runtime is filtered on `movie_details.runtime_minutes`, and genres are included or excluded. The session is defined by who is present, not by a persistent group. PolyLens users asked for temporary subgroups, and MovieLens replaced persistent groups with pairwise "buddies" because "group creation was too hard" ([Harper and Konstan](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/harper-tiis2015.pdf)).

The unseen filter's cost falls on quality, not quantity. Two members who have seen 50% and 30% of the eligible pool leave 35% of it if their viewing were independent, somewhat more in practice. But the remainder systematically lacks the canonical, high-prior titles, and it skews toward films where predictions are less confident. Two cheap additions compensate. The first is a labeled rewatch lane: a film one member ranked top-tier and nobody else has seen, scored by the unseen members' shrunk predictions and labeled like "Rafa's 9.4, you'd probably like it (7.8)." The second is a boost for titles on both members' backlogs, S'(i) = S(i) + b × (members with i in backlog)/n, or a dedicated "on both your watchlists" row shown first.

Games add feasibility constraints that films lack. The group needs a shared platform or crossplay, a player count that fits, and a co-op or versus mode. IGDB's multiplayer_modes endpoint exposes onlinecoop, onlinecoopmax, offlinecoop, offlinecoopmax, splitscreen, and related fields ([IGDB field list](https://tech.dukeyin.com/2023/06/14/igdb-api-documents/)); they need to be mirrored, and the feature-ideas sketch already notes this. Ownership should rank "everyone owns it" first and show the price for anyone who would have to buy. A member who played a game solo does not rule out a co-op run. Co-op justifies a larger lambda, because one bored player ruins the session for everyone. Two gaps remain open: IGDB does not obviously expose crossplay, and Verso stores no per-user platform access beyond owned platforms on entries.

Explanation is where the group feature earns trust. In PolyLens's composite view, which showed the group prediction beside each member's, **94%** preferred seeing each member's personal recommendations, 93% preferred letting others see theirs, and **97% (798 of 819)** actually shared ([PolyLens](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/poly-camera-final.pdf)). Explaining the aggregation rule itself did not move perceived fairness, consensus, or satisfaction in one controlled study ([Barile et al. 2021](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2955/paper11.pdf)), and did not improve understanding in another ([Waterschoot et al. 2025, n = 271](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04273)). A good card therefore shows each person's predicted score on their own scale ("you 8.4, Sam 7.1"), one anchor per person from their own top tier ("you loved Heat; Sam picked Collateral"), and any shared hook. For a guest it never exposes the host's low scores on the guest's picks. The rule must be computed in SQL, never delegated to a model. Three current LLMs executed the average and least-misery rules perfectly when told which to use, but produced different outcomes when the rule was left to them ([Du, 2026-08-25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23966)).

The result screen should show a shortlist of 3 to 5, chosen so each member has at least one of their personal top-quartile candidates on it, a light version of fairness-aware list building ([Kaya et al. 2020](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3383313.3412232)). A 10 to 20 card deck to swipe comes next, where the first mutual yes wins, and then a share card for chat apps. Jannach's observation that groups may prefer "to discuss options on existing communication platforms" argues for that share card ([arXiv 2507.00535](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.00535)).

A sketch of the query, using Verso's table names where they exist (`media_items`, `user_entries`, `movie_details`, `watch_availability`) and marking the new ones as proposed:

```sql
WITH members AS (SELECT unnest(:member_ids::uuid[]) AS user_id),
cand AS (
  SELECT m.id AS item_id
  FROM media_items m JOIN movie_details d ON d.media_item_id = m.id
  WHERE m.medium = 'movie'
    AND (:max_runtime IS NULL OR d.runtime_minutes <= :max_runtime)
    AND NOT (d.genres && :exclude_genres)
    AND NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM user_entries e                       -- unseen by everyone present
                    WHERE e.media_item_id = m.id AND e.status <> 'backlog'
                      AND e.user_id IN (SELECT user_id FROM members))
    AND EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM watch_availability a                     -- on a service one of us has
                WHERE a.media_item_id = m.id AND a.region = :region AND a.removed_at IS NULL
                  AND a.offer_type IN ('flatrate','free','ads') AND a.provider_id = ANY(:provider_ids))
),
p AS (                                                                  -- proposed: prediction, item_prior, user_rank_stats
  SELECT pr.item_id, pr.user_id, ip.mu + (pr.pct_score - ip.mu) * us.n / (us.n + :k) AS s
  FROM prediction pr JOIN cand c USING (item_id)
  JOIN item_prior ip USING (item_id) JOIN user_rank_stats us USING (user_id)
  WHERE pr.user_id IN (SELECT user_id FROM members)
)
SELECT item_id, avg(s) AS mean_s, min(s) AS min_s,
       avg(s) - :lambda * (max(s) - min(s)) AS group_score              -- use stddev_pop(s) for n > 2
FROM p GROUP BY item_id
HAVING count(*) = :n_members AND min(s) >= :misery_floor
ORDER BY group_score DESC LIMIT 50;
```

Starting defaults are lambda = 0.2, a misery floor of 3.0 in percentile units, k = 10, a shortlist of 3 to 5, and a deck of 10 to 20. They derive from the worked example and the literature's qualitative findings, not from any published tuning on couples.

Verso can evaluate this better than the literature does, which mostly simulates groups offline from individual ratings ([RecSys 2022 tutorial](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3523227.3547371)). Log every session. The primary label is whether the pair actually watched the film. After watching, each member places it in their own ranking, which gives realized percentiles per member: their mean, their minimum, a misery rate (the share of sessions where someone put the pick in their bottom tier), and calibration split by host versus guest. For a recurring pair, a deficit counter, the cumulative sum of each member's realized percentile minus the pair mean, should oscillate around zero. When it drifts, future sessions can tilt toward whoever keeps losing out. This applies Masthoff's fairness strategy across sessions rather than within one pick.

## The LLM earns its cost choosing among near-ties and explaining, not ranking

The research favors the sketch's split: SQL candidates first, then a model that picks three and explains them. In "LLMs are zero-shot rankers," GPT-4 reached NDCG@10 of **46.74 on MovieLens-1M and 62.88 on Amazon Games** when ranking 20 candidates, one true item among 19 random negatives ([Hou et al., ECIR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08845)). But in the realistic setting, where candidates came from seven retrieval models, a trained SASRec beat the LLM: 10.45 against 6.91 on MovieLens and 4.02 against 2.80 on Games. In re-ranking, GPT-3.5 beat four traditional models and raised the exposure of niche items ([Beyond Utility, WWW 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00331)). LLMs also have "limited capability to assimilate statistical information, such as popularity and collaborative filtering" ([BIGRec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08434)). The division of labor follows: SQL owns the score, and the LLM chooses among near-ties, adds diversity, and writes the reason.

The industry pattern points the same way. Netflix's LLM ranker, GenRec, gained +1.6% offline MRR but only +0.006% on its core online metric ([arXiv 2608.10257](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10257)). Spotify reported that users "were up to four times more likely to click on recommendations accompanied by explanations, especially for more niche content" ([Spotify Research, 2024](https://research.atspotify.com/2024/12/contextualized-recommendations-through-personalized-narratives-using-llms)). The explanation is probably worth more than the choice of three.

Grounding has to be structural. Open-catalog prompting invents 0.6 to 2.7% of titles on MovieLens-25M and **49.3 to 61.0% on Amazon Toys**, and the model's confidence does not predict which titles are invented ([arXiv 2608.10008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10008)). Given a candidate list, well-behaved models stray about 1% of the time, and some models far more ([Beyond Utility](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00331)). The fix is to label candidates c01 to c30 and declare pick1, pick2, and pick3 as `enum` fields in Claude's structured outputs, which use "constrained decoding" and "guarantee schema-compliant responses" ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). The enum values are identical for every request, so the compiled grammar stays cached, and three named fields sidestep the unsupported minItems. The model returns because_item_ids as enums too, and the server renders every title itself. Any validation failure falls back to the SQL top three with a template reason.

The prompt should carry a distilled profile, not raw history. LLMs "struggle to perceive the order" of a history and do best with shorter inputs ([Hou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08845)), so pass the resulting tiers plus two or three recent decisive comparisons, never the raw log. A cached taste summary of about 150 to 250 tokens per user per medium, rebuilt by a batch job when the list changes, follows practice at Spotify and in the research. Profiles built from reviews came close to matrix factorization without beating it (RMSE 0.941 to 0.944 against 0.925, [Ramos et al., ACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05810)), so expect notes to help the reason line more than the hit rate.

Reasons that cite the user's own history work, and generic ones do worse than nothing. In a 759-participant study, contextualized explanations scored 5.38 on transparency and trust against 4.94 with no explanation. Generic LLM explanations scored lower on consumption intent than no explanation at all (4.36 against 4.61) ([arXiv 2501.12152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12152)). So the reason line cites one or two of the user's own items, names one concrete shared trait, stays under about 25 words, and carries an honest confidence, with low-confidence picks labeled as "a stretch."

Cost is cents. A request with 30 candidate lines, a taste summary, and about ten loved items runs about 2,500 input tokens and 200 output tokens. Prices below were checked 2026-09-24, and newer Claude models are scaled ×1.3 for their tokenizer ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing); [Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)).

Provider prompt caching barely helps at this size, because Haiku 4.5's minimum cacheable prompt is 4,096 tokens (Sonnet 5's is 1,024) ([prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). Verso also has a label few products have: whether a recommended item is later ranked, and in which tier.

## A build order one developer can ship on Postgres, pg_cron, and Edge Functions

Each stage below ships only what beats the stage before it in the leave-one-out harness. The column "moves on when" is the data condition, not a date.

| Stage | Roadmap slot | What gets built | Runs where | Moves on when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Instruments | Now, before any recommender | A leave-one-out harness per medium. A `prediction_log` (user, item, predicted score, confidence, model version, shown at). The `suggestion_requests` meter. A percentile view over `ranking_entries`. | SQL views and a script | Always first; every later stage is judged by it |
| 1. Priors and content | V1 personal suggestion | Community prior m_x with external-score fallback; baseline b_ui; content profile mapped through the user's list; a SQL candidate function returning about 30 rows with a predicted score and a "why retrieved" field; an Edge Function that picks 3 with enum IDs and a grounded reason, on Haiku 4.5 or similar | pg_cron nightly for priors and idf; the candidate function on read; one Edge Function | Content beats b_ui on MAE and pairwise accuracy for users with 5 or more items |
| 2. Neighbors and Match | V2 social | Nightly similarity job (Pearson through corr(), or tau-b for friend pairs), shrunk with beta about 10 and n_uv ≥ 5; a top-30 neighbor table; kNN predictions blended by precision weights; the Friend score (unweighted average of friends); Match % = 50 + 50 × tau_b × shrink with agree and clash lists; a community-average toggle, never gated | pg_cron nightly; predictions on read for one item, nightly for lists | kNN beats the stage-1 blend in leave-one-out; coverage above a set share |
| 3. Group sessions | V2 group suggestions | Session object (members present, medium, region, services, runtime, genres; platform and couch-or-remote for games); guest grid link; group query over per-member predictions for the whole candidate set; shortlist, rewatch lane, watchlist row, deck, share card; session and "we watched it" logging; IGDB multiplayer_modes mirrored | Edge Function computing member predictions for the filtered set; SQL aggregation | Logged sessions accumulate enough to tune lambda and the floor per medium |
| 4. Items and bridges | When candidate items commonly have 10 to 20+ rankers | Item-item kNN for "because you loved X"; too_close answers as ties; stacked blend weights; theme and mood tag vocabulary through an LLM batch job; item_links; pgvector embeddings for cross-medium candidates | pg_cron nightly; Batch API jobs | Cross-medium slot beats the popularity baseline for new-to-medium users |
| 5. Factors | Hundreds to thousands of active users per medium | Pairwise or listwise factorization (BPR-style on implied pairs) with pooled Bradley-Terry item strengths, optionally LightFM with content features and backlog as implicit signal; linear blend with kNN; guest fold-in; exploration slot | Nightly Python job on a GitHub Actions schedule writing factors to Postgres; Edge Functions serve dot products | Beats the stage-4 blend on the harness and in interleaving |

Stage 1 is Beli's own starting point in miniature: a personal number before there are friends. Stage 2 is the part Beli gets wrong in public, so Verso's Match should ship with shrinkage, an overlap count, and an agreement list from its first day. Stage 3 is the part no comparable product has, which makes it the differentiator. It depends on stage 2's per-member predictions existing for any candidate, not just for each user's top list. Stages 1 through 4 need no training loop and no GPU.

## What only Verso's own data can settle

| Open question | Why the research cannot settle it | What settles it | Default until then |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whether kNN beats a tuned baseline at all at Verso's size | Classic kNN barely beat a movie average on Netflix top-K ([Koren](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i290-dm/s11/SECURE/a1-koren.pdf)) | Leave-one-out against b_ui | Ship content plus prior; add kNN only when it wins |
| Shrinkage beta, damping lambda, neighbor count, minimum overlap | Published constants are Netflix-scale (50 to 100) and zero out Verso pairs | Nested leave-one-out per medium | beta 10, lambda 1, k up to 30, n_uv ≥ 5 |
| Pearson versus Kendall at 5 to 15 overlaps | No recommender benchmark at this overlap; only sampling-variance arguments | Leave-one-out and pairwise accuracy on the log | tau-b for Match; Pearson or tau for neighbors |
| Gate thresholds (5, 10, 15 items) | Beli's are Single-source from 2023 and 2024; Criticker's 10 is untested elsewhere | Coverage-versus-error curves from the harness | 5 for content, 10 for collaborative, 15 for the UI unlock |
| Whether the comparison log improves prediction beyond the final order | No study measures it | Pairwise accuracy with and without too_close ties | Use the order; log everything |
| When factorization overtakes kNN | No published learning curve for explicit rankings | The harness as users grow | Stage 5 only at hundreds to thousands per medium |
| How users read a Match percentage (chance at 50% versus 0%) | Beli's negative values drew complaints; no study compares the scales | Feedback and a small test | Chance at 50%, hidden below 5 shared items |
| Group lambda, misery floor, and guest shrinkage | No published tuning on couples; shrinkage versus weighting is untested | Watched outcomes, realized percentiles, host-versus-guest calibration | lambda 0.2, floor 3.0, k 10 |
| How much "unseen by everyone" shrinks the pool for heavy pairs, and whether people want a rewatch lane | No empirical data found | Session logs | Rewatch lane shown as a separate labeled row |
| Whether game taste predicts film taste for Verso users | The only games evidence is Amazon categories, and it is asymmetric | Cross-medium slot outcomes for new-to-medium users | Fade with k / (k + n_target); one labeled slot |
| Whether the LLM's three beat SQL's top three, and whether a number beats a reason | Offline gains shrink online; no public A/B on numeric scores versus reasons | Interleaving; later-ranked tier of picks | Show the reason with a confidence label; the number maps to "lands between X and Y" |
| The right popularity weight | Users disagree; the MovieLens median was 0.021 with a wide spread | Per-user control or interleaving | A small fixed weight plus a separate "new and well-ranked" row |

Beli's actual formulas are also unknowable from outside, but nothing above depends on them. Every design choice here rests on published methods and on Beli's visible behavior, so Verso copies what Beli shows users rather than how Beli computes it.

## Conclusion

Verso's ranking loop has already done the work that makes recommendation hard for star-rating products. Every list is a strict order on a per-user scale, so similarity and prediction reduce to percentiles, a shrinkage factor, and a mapping back through each user's own list. That argument changes two plans. The latent model of Option C is not a prerequisite for the social layer; predicted scores and Match both come straight from positional percentiles. And the comparison log's highest-value use at first is evaluation, not modeling. The log is a growing set of real head-to-head judgments, and "recommended, then ranked in the top tier" is a stronger outcome label than any click. Beli's public misses map one to one onto design rules for Verso. Unexplained scores get called "extremely inaccurate." A correlation shown raw reads -11% between friends who love the same places. A stated constraint that is not a hard filter surfaces steakhouses for a vegetarian.

The strategic opening is the group feature. Beli has not shipped one, and neither has any game tracker, Letterboxd, or Netflix. The swipe apps that exist aggregate by unanimous approval over catalogs they know nothing about personally. Verso already knows each member's taste in the form a group rule needs: comparable, per-person scores, with confidence that can be shrunk rather than weighted. It can therefore turn the swipe into the final consensus step instead of the whole recommender. The one number to tune is lambda, and the watched-it outcome is the data that tunes it. Instrumenting sessions before optimizing them is the cheapest decision in this report, and the most consequential one.
