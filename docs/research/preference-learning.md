# Preference learning: conjoint analysis, embeddings beyond kNN, and one personal scorer for Verso

Research compiled 2026-10-29 for VER-310, the evening Rafa described the engine to a statistics professor after a lecture on Pandora and on film recommenders built from synopsis embeddings. The professor's read, as Rafa relayed it and as the addendum to [attribute-genome.md](attribute-genome.md) records it: the idea is good but broad, embeddings fit what Verso does and improve with more data, something better than kNN probably exists now, and marketing science's method for head-to-head comparisons is worth reading, inferred to be conjoint analysis (the method behind the feature valuations in Apple versus Samsung). Rafa's own line that evening, "the features are really coarse so we NEED knn", is treated here as a direction to test in the harness, not a decision. Two research threads fed the report. The first is conjoint analysis for head-to-head comparisons: choice-based conjoint and its logit, hierarchical Bayes, adaptive question design, the misfit between designed profiles and real items, conjoint's meeting with machine learning from 2023 to 2026, and what Apple's expert used it for; it was read from Sawtooth Software's documentation, Rossi and Allenby's review, the Toubia and Hauser papers and Train's textbook. The second is item embeddings beyond plain kNN from 2023 to 2026: personalized reward models on frozen embeddings, preference Gaussian processes, collaborative hybrids, sequential and generative recommenders, measured results for synopsis embeddings, and what any of it costs on Supabase. The report builds on [attribute-genome.md](attribute-genome.md) and does not repeat what that report settles: weighted Euclidean distance for graded attributes, scoring against Loved items rather than their average, personal weights learned from the ranked list and shrunk toward a population prior at about three to five ranked items per weight, the four-arm pilot, the data-terms gates and its 12 questions. It also builds on the engine as designed and built ([docs/design/recommendation-engine.md](../design/recommendation-engine.md)): the community prior, layer 1's cosine over idf-weighted binary features, the leave-one-out harness `recommendation_loo`, the prediction written at every insertion and never read during it, the blend, and Claude Haiku 5.5 picking three of thirty candidates and writing the reasons. Nothing in this report is decided. Numbers marked as the researchers' own are their arithmetic, derivations or estimates, not published results, and sources seen only through a search summary are marked as such.

## Summary

Conjoint analysis gives Verso no new model; it gives a better-run version of the one the genome report proposed. Choice-based conjoint with two options per question is exactly that report's pairwise logistic, P(a above b) = σ(w · (x_a − x_b)), and a whole ranked list is its exploded logit. What marketing adds is practice: hierarchical Bayes with a covariance of tastes, a mixture prior for camps such as cozy and soulslike players, sign constraints, and posterior draws for the exploration slot. Its misfits teach more than its machinery. Verso's items are real, self-chosen and correlated, so unobserved quality loads onto the weights unless the community prior enters as an offset, and a trait a person avoids never varies on their list. Choosing the next comparison buys little inside binary insertion, since consistent answers produce the same order whichever pivots are asked, and any rule that read the predicted score would break the rule that the guess never steers placement. A frozen embedding does not improve with data; what is learned on top of it does. For a dozen testers with 20 to 50 items each, the method past plain kNN is a per-person Bayesian Bradley-Terry head on 4 to 16 principal components of centered embeddings, shrunk toward everyone. A basis shared across users waits for about 20 people with 50 or more items, collaborative hybrids for hundreds whose lists overlap, and sequential or generative recommenders are off the roadmap. The threads join as one personal scorer, conjoint weights on embedding components and named attributes with the prior as an offset, beside max-similarity kNN for retrieval and Haiku 5.5 for the pick and the reason. "We NEED knn" survives as one term to test. The pilot becomes nested arms tested in a fixed order, and its first step costs nothing: embed the games already ranked with a local open model.

## The engine in six plain sentences

Written to be read aloud, to a professor or anyone outside the code; it describes the design this report proposes, not what is built today.

Each person ranks what they have played or watched by answering head-to-head questions, so Verso holds a full order of their taste rather than a pile of star ratings. Every game and film is described two ways, by a short list of named attributes (genres today, graded traits such as difficulty or coziness later) and by a text embedding of its synopsis. For each person, Verso fits a small conjoint model in which the chance they prefer one item to another depends on weighted differences in those descriptions, measured against how much everyone else likes the item, with each person's weights pulled toward the population's until their own list is long enough to stand alone. Beside that model, a nearest-neighbor search finds unranked items whose embeddings sit closest to any single item the person loved, so two separate tastes are never averaged into a middle that neither holds. The two scores are blended with weights fit by hiding each ranked item in turn and predicting where it would land, and a new part ships only if it beats the old engine for most testers. A language model then picks three of the top thirty candidates and writes the reason in terms of the person's own list, and it never computes a score.

## Conjoint is the genome report's pairwise logistic under its marketing name

Choice-based conjoint shows a respondent about ten choice tasks, each with three to six product profiles described by a handful of attributes, and asks which one they would buy ([Sawtooth, What is CBC analysis](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/hid_web_whatcbc.html), undated, read 2026-10-29). Each profile's utility is a sum of part-worths, one per attribute level, and the choices follow a multinomial logit. Written out by the researchers: for person h and choice task t, each alternative j in the set C_t has an attribute vector x_j (a dummy code per level for a categorical attribute, a number for a graded one); its utility is U_hj = β_h · x_j + ε_hj, where β_h is person h's vector of part-worths and ε_hj an extreme-value error; and the probability of choosing j is P_h(j | C_t) = exp(β_h · x_j) / Σ over k in C_t of exp(β_h · x_k). With two options a and b, this collapses to P_h(a over b) = σ(β_h · (x_a − x_b)), where σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)). That is Bradley-Terry with each item's log-strength linear in its attributes, and it is, term for term, the genome report's P(a above b) = σ(w · (x_a − x_b)) with w = β_h. A full ranking read as a sequence of choices, the first from all items, the second from the rest and so on, is the exploded logit ([Train, Discrete Choice Methods with Simulation, 2nd ed., 2009, ch. 7](https://eml.berkeley.edu/books/choice2nd/Ch07_p151-182.pdf)), which is the Plackett-Luce likelihood the genome report already proposes. Chapelle and Harchaoui made the same point from the machine-learning side in 2004: every choice is a constraint w · (x_chosen − x_other) ≥ 0, so estimating part-worths is classification on profile differences ([Chapelle and Harchaoui, NIPS 2004](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2004/hash/4bbdcc0e821637155ac4217bdab70d2e-Abstract.html)). Rafa's professor pointed him to a method Verso had already written down under another name.

The researchers' worked example shows what the model buys beyond a score. Take three standardized game attributes, difficulty, story weight and coziness, and a person whose part-worths are β = (0.8, −0.3, 0.5). Game A scores (1.5, −0.5, −1.0) and game B (−0.5, 1.0, 1.0). Then U_A = 1.2 + 0.15 − 0.5 = 0.85 and U_B = −0.4 − 0.3 + 0.5 = −0.2, so the gap is 1.05 and P(A over B) = σ(1.05) = 0.74. Split by attribute, difficulty contributes +1.6 to the gap, story weight +0.45 and coziness −1.0. That split is a reason in Verso's sense: this person put A above B for its difficulty, despite its being less cozy. Conjoint's standard summary of a person, each attribute's importance as the range of its part-worths divided by the sum of ranges ([MetricGate documentation, 2025-01-16](https://metricgate.com/docs/conjoint-analysis/)), is the same idea for a whole list, and it is the sentence the genome report wants ("you weight difficulty high and story low").

On raw count, a Verso list carries more evidence than a conjoint respondent. A Sawtooth respondent who answers 12 tasks of four concepts makes 12 choices; a 50-item list read as an exploded logit is 49 successive choices, from sets of 50, then 49, down to 2 (the researchers' arithmetic). Conjoint studies get away with less than one task per parameter, about 12 to 18 part-worths for six attributes at three or four levels estimated from 10 to 15 tasks, because hierarchical Bayes borrows strength across respondents and because the profiles are designed so that every attribute varies independently. Verso's items are not designed, which is why the section after next matters more than this one.

Conjoint also offers three shapes per attribute where the genome report used one. Green and Srinivasan's vector model is linear, so more of an attribute is always better or always worse; the ideal-point model is quadratic, w · (x − x₀)², with a peak at a favorite level x₀; the part-worth function model gives each level its own value at the cost of more parameters; and a mixed model lets each attribute take its own form ([Green and Srinivasan 1978, as described in a Springer chapter, read through a search summary](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-78213-3_2)). The genome report's s_i = w · x_i is the vector model on every attribute. For difficulty and pace a favorite middle level is more plausible than "more is better", and either an ideal-point term (one extra parameter) or the attribute coded as three levels (0 to 1, 2 to 3, 4 to 5, two parameters instead of one) can represent "likes medium difficulty" (the researchers' inference). Neither can represent the genome report's player who loves both cozy games and soulslikes, whose taste sits in two corners of several attributes at once. That takes a mixture or the max-similarity scorer, so part-worths belong in the personal-weights layer and do not replace the similarity layer.

Three of conjoint's variants map onto Verso. Adaptive conjoint analysis (ACA) asks graded paired comparisons, how much better one profile is than another, after people rate attribute importances directly, and hierarchical Bayes later replaced its ad hoc way of combining the two ([Sawtooth, ACA technical papers](https://events.sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/technical-papers/adaptive-conjoint-analysis-aca)); Verso's binary answer plus "too close" is already a three-point graded pair. Adaptive choice-based conjoint (ACBC) puts a screening stage before the choices, motivated by a Sawtooth study of nine attributes in which 85% of respondents' choices could be explained by attention to at most four attribute levels ([Sawtooth, Motivation for Adaptive CBC, read through a search summary](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/motivationforadaptivecbc.html)). That is the conjoint version of "I don't play horror": a filter, not a weight. A linear model will try to explain such a rule with a large negative weight, and only for people whose list happens to contain a horror game, so the rule belongs in onboarding exclusions or a "not for me" signal (the researchers' inference). MaxDiff, choosing the best and the worst of a set ([Louviere, Flynn and Marley, Best-Worst Scaling, Cambridge University Press, 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:cbooks:9781107043152)), is the efficient one: naming A best and D worst among four items settles five of the six pairs (A over B, C and D; B and C over D) in two taps, missing only B against C (the researchers' arithmetic). It would suit the onboarding deck, where people place many items at once, but it changes the ranking flow, which decision 1 (Option A) closes unless Rafa reopens it.

The class's case shows what conjoint is usually for. In the November 2013 damages retrial of Apple v. Samsung, Apple's expert John Hauser of MIT testified from choice-based conjoint surveys that consumers would pay about $100 more on a $199 smartphone, and about $90 more on a $499 tablet, for the features of three Apple patents; Apple sought $380 million and Samsung argued for about $52 million ([Macworld, 2013-11-15](https://www.macworld.com/article/222382/apple-experts-seek-to-justify-380-million-damages-claim-against-samsung.html)). A 2014 account of the second case describes two online studies of 507 phone and 459 tablet buyers, run in Sawtooth's software with hierarchical Bayes, that put the added willingness to pay at $32 to $102 a feature; Samsung's expert faulted the omission of brand, operating system and battery life, and Hauser agreed under cross-examination that these were stated preferences, not prices Apple could charge ([Quirk's, 2014-05-01](https://www.quirks.com/articles/when-apple-samsung-and-conjoint-came-together)); the article leaves unclear which trial the respondent counts belong to. The quantity conjoint sells is an exchange rate: willingness to pay is a part-worth difference divided by the price coefficient, so a feature worth 0.6 against a price coefficient of −0.012 a dollar is worth $50 (the researchers' illustration, not Hauser's numbers). Verso has no price, so its exchange rate would be in list positions or tiers, as in "for this person, high difficulty is worth about one tier". Samsung's main attack, the attributes the survey left out, is the problem Verso's real items raise in a sharper form.

## Hierarchical Bayes adds four things to "shrunk toward everyone's", and a dozen testers can afford two

Hierarchical Bayes is how conjoint gets a weight vector per person out of ten answers. Rossi and Allenby write it in three layers: the likelihood of each person's choices given their part-worths, p(y_i | β_i); a first-stage prior saying how part-worths are spread across the population, p(β_i | τ); and a second-stage prior on that spread, p(τ | h). Under a normal first stage, each person's estimate sits between their own data and the population mean, and outliers are pulled in hardest; learning the prior's tightness from the data is "adaptive shrinkage", which descends from Efron and Morris's empirical Bayes ([Rossi and Allenby, Marketing Science 22(3), 2003](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf)). The genome report's prior, w ~ Normal(m, τ²I) with m re-estimated over users, is this first stage with two simplifications, one spread τ shared by every attribute and a single normal, fit at the posterior mode rather than sampled. The mode loses nothing that matters at Verso's size. Chapelle and Harchaoui's posterior-mode version matched sampled hierarchical Bayes in accuracy, sometimes slightly beat it, and took about 1.8 seconds against about 19 minutes of Gibbs sampling ([NIPS 2004](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2004/hash/4bbdcc0e821637155ac4217bdab70d2e-Abstract.html)); Evgeniou, Pontil and Toubia's convex shrinkage generally beat standard hierarchical Bayes with diffuse priors on simulated and field data, because it set the amount of shrinkage better ([Marketing Science 26(6), 2007](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:26:y:2007:i:6:p:805-818)). No sampler is needed; a nightly Newton fit per person is the method.

What hierarchical Bayes adds is four refinements of the prior, in order of value to Verso. The first is a full covariance Σ across people in place of τ²I. If people who weight difficulty also weight combat intensity, one person's answers about difficulty inform their weight on combat, and the population learns how weights on correlated attributes usually move together, which is the hierarchical answer to the genome report's warning that correlated attributes trade weight between themselves. The second is a mixture of normals. Rossi and Allenby warn that a single normal prior can shrink individual estimates so hard that it hides a bimodal population, and offer the mixture as the fix ([2003](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/feinf/Bayes/Rossi_and_Allenby_-_Bayesian_Statistics_and_Marketing.pdf)); the R package bayesm fits exactly that, a hierarchical logit whose population of tastes is a mixture of normals, with per-coefficient sign constraints in newer versions and person-level characteristics in the prior mean ([bayesm, rhierMnlRwMixture, read through a search summary](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/bayesm/html/rhierMnlRwMixture.html)). Verso's population is the textbook case: one normal prior pulls cozy players and soulslike players toward a middle that neither camp holds. The third is sign or order constraints where the answer is known in advance, which Allenby, Arora and Ginter brought into conjoint with priors such as "a lower price is never worse" ([Journal of Marketing Research 32, May 1995](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/doc/u406BA8DL7)); Verso has at least one such fact, that a person's weight on the community prior should never be negative. The fourth is posterior draws rather than only the mode. Thompson sampling for the exploration slot, which the genome report proposes after [Chapelle and Li (NIPS 2011)](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2011/hash/e53a0a2978c28872a4505bdb51db06dc-Abstract.html), needs draws, and a Laplace approximation at the mode, a Gaussian whose covariance is the inverse of the fit's Hessian, supplies them for the price of one small matrix inverse per person.

A fifth refinement sits in both the marketing and the machine-learning literature: a prior mean that depends on what the person has said about themselves. bayesm's person-level characteristics are the linear version, and TasteNet-MNL is the neural one, a network that maps individual characteristics to the taste parameters of a hand-specified logit and that predicted better than plain and mixed logit benchmarks on the Swissmetro travel data ([Han, Zegras, Pereira and Ben-Akiva, arXiv 2002.00922, 2020, revised 2022, read through a search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.00922)). For Verso the characteristics are the onboarding answers of decision 25, where a person plays or watches and the three titles they love, so the person's prior mean becomes m + Γ c_h, where c_h codes those answers as numbers and Γ is learned over users. It matters most at cold start, when the prior is nearly the whole estimate (the researchers' inference). The same population layer also fills in what a person's list cannot show: Lenk, DeSarbo, Green and Young used it to impute part-worths for attributes a respondent never saw ([Marketing Science 15(2), 1996](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.15.2.173)), which is the remedy for self-selection in the next section.

How fast a person's answers overtake the prior depends as much on which pairs they compare as on how many. The researchers' own Gaussian approximation: the posterior precision on a weight is the prior precision plus the Fisher information from the answers; one logistic answer with predicted probability p and attribute gap Δx_k carries p(1 − p) · Δx_k² about weight k; and the share of the estimate driven by the person's own data is I / (I + 1/τ²), where I is the summed information. With τ = 0.5 (prior precision 4) and balanced answers (p near 0.5), random pairs of standardized items, whose squared gap averages 2, carry 0.5 each, so the data share is 0.56 after 10 comparisons, 0.83 after 40 and 0.94 after 120. Pairs of similar items, whose squared gap averages 0.5, carry 0.125 each, giving 0.24, 0.56 and 0.79. The figures treat one weight as if the others were known and the answers as independent, so they flatter the real share, but the direction is clear, and it runs against Option A's genre-preferring pivot, which asks about similar items on purpose. The section on choosing the next comparison takes this up.

Not all four refinements are affordable at a dozen testers, a point the notes leave implicit and this report draws as its own inference. A full covariance on 8 personal dimensions is 36 numbers to estimate from 12 people; a two-component mixture is two means and two sets of spreads from perhaps six people each; and Γ needs people who answered onboarding differently. What 12 people support is a population mean and one spread per dimension, a diagonal prior, re-estimated nightly by empirical Bayes. The mixture's job of keeping camps of taste apart falls in the meantime to the max-similarity term, which separates camps without estimating anything. The sign constraint and the posterior draws cost nothing extra and belong in the first fit; the covariance, the mixture and the onboarding covariates switch on at a few dozen active users, and only if the harness shows they help.

Conjoint's validation habits carry over too. The holdout hit rate on a two-option task is the genome report's pairwise accuracy, with chance at 0.5. Sawtooth's usual band of 55% to 75% is for tasks with three or four options, where chance is 25% to 33% ([Sawtooth glossary, read through a search summary](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/knowledge-base/general-issues/glossary-of-terms)), so it is no benchmark for the genome simulation's 0.59 to 0.67, and conjoint's holdouts are designed, same-session and fully described profiles, an easier target than a real held-out game whose appeal lies partly outside its attributes. The habit worth copying is the double benchmark: report each person's hit rate against chance and against the aggregate model. Moore found hierarchical Bayes matched or beat latent-segment and aggregate models on hit rates ([Moore, International Journal of Research in Marketing, 2004, read through a search summary](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/a-cross-validity-comparison-of-rating-based-and-choice-based-conjoint-analysis-models/812339339060051970-7548)), but one Sawtooth analysis found aggregate logit predicted individuals' holdouts better than hierarchical Bayes in four of nine partial-profile datasets until the priors were adjusted ([Sawtooth, read through a search summary](https://content.sawtoothsoftware.com/assets/d320d51d-ad42-476b-b781-726df2e2828e)). The harness already carries the second column, population weights only, and every personal arm should report against it. On volume, Sawtooth asks for about 10 or more choice tasks per person for robust individual predictions, 8 to 15 being typical ([Sawtooth, CBC questionnaires and design strategy](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/hid_web_cbc_designs_1.html), read 2026-10-29), and rarely more than about 10 attributes ([Sawtooth, number of attributes, levels and tasks](https://legacy.sawtoothsoftware.com/help/lighthouse-studio/manual/hid_web_cbc_designs_4.html)); in Toubia, Hauser and Simester's simulations, estimates after 16 questions were about 25% more accurate than after 8, and after 24 about 12% more accurate than after 16 ([Journal of Marketing Research 41, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.1.116.25082)).

Two newer threads do not earn a place. Neural choice models such as ConjointNet report beating traditional conjoint estimation "by over 5%" on two preference datasets, with no peer-reviewed venue listed ([Zhang et al., arXiv 2503.11710, 2025-03-12](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11710)), a gain that needs more data per person than Verso has. Language models as synthetic respondents are the busiest thread and the least reliable: Brand, Israeli and Ngwe's estimates from GPT-3.5 looked realistic in their 2025 working paper ([MSI working paper](https://www.msi.org/working-paper/using-llms-for-market-research-2/)), while the revised abstract calls them often inaccurate and sometimes wrong-signed ([SSRN 4395751, read through a search summary](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4395751)); in a national choice experiment of 18,144 observations, even the best prompting strategy reversed the human ranking of attribute importance ([Hu and Gao, NeurIPS 2026 EconML workshop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/169654)); and a model simulating a respondent shifts its beliefs about unstated attributes when one stated attribute changes ([Gui and Toubia, arXiv 2312.15524, revised 2025, read through a search summary](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524)). Verso has each person's real comparisons, so a model's guess at their weights could at most be a weak cold-start prior, scored in the harness like any other arm.

## Real items break conjoint's design, so the community prior enters as an offset

Conjoint's guarantees come from design: attributes varied independently, every level shown to every respondent, and hypothetical profiles with nothing in them beyond the listed attributes. Verso's pairs are the opposite on every count. They are real games and films the person chose to play or watch, their attributes move together, they carry qualities no attribute names, and attributes the person avoids never appear at all. Train names the general problem for data from real choices: it is limited to alternatives that exist, it may lack variation in the factors of interest, and the factors that matter most to consumers often vary least, because markets equalize them ([Train, 2009, ch. 7](https://eml.berkeley.edu/books/choice2nd/Ch07_p151-182.pdf)). The researchers found no study that estimates part-worths from a person's own ranked list of real, experienced products; the nearest are ranking-based conjoint on designed profiles and demand estimation from market shares. Five misfits follow, and the first is the one that changes the model.

Unobserved quality is the first. If acclaimed games also tend to be hard and story-heavy, a person who simply likes good games will show positive weights on difficulty and story that are really weights on quality. Demand estimation's standard answer is a term for each item, and the cheap version for Verso, which is the researchers' inference rather than a cited result, is to put the community prior into each person's utility as an offset with its own weight: s_hi = α_h · p_i + w_h · x_i, where p_i is layer 0's prior score for item i, α_h the person's weight on consensus, and w_h their weights on the attributes x_i. The personal weights then explain where the person departs from consensus, which is also what a reason should say ("you rank harder games higher than most people do"). Two details keep it honest. The prior must leave out the person's own ranking of the item, or it leaks the answer; the engine already stores `baseline_score` in `item_prior` for exactly that purpose in the harness. And α_h ≥ 0 is the natural sign constraint from the previous section. Embeddings attack the same problem from the other side. Compiani, Morozov and Seiler took principal components of pretrained text and image embeddings, gave them random coefficients in a logit like observed attributes, and beat attribute-based models at predicting second choices; across 40 Amazon categories, text found close substitutes better than images ([Compiani, Morozov and Seiler, arXiv 2503.20711, 2025, read through a search summary](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20711v3)). Embedding components soak up traits the named attributes miss, which shrinks the same omitted-quality bias. The researchers did not read Berry, Levinsohn and Pakes, the standard treatment of unobserved product traits, and the harness can test the offset as one more column at no data cost. Gui and Toubia's confounding result adds a warning for the genome itself: a model that scores "difficulty" while knowing a title is acclaimed may let quality into the score at the measurement step, one more reason the genome report keeps reception fields out of the scoring prompt.

Correlated attributes are the second. Real items move together, difficulty with combat intensity and darkness of tone with horror, the conjoint textbook's horsepower and fuel economy, and in a non-orthogonal set a regression assigns part of one attribute's effect to another ([Marketing Engineering technical note on conjoint, read through a search summary](https://www.decisionpro.biz/business-users/training/technical-notes/send/5-technical-notes/13-tn09-conjoint-analysis-technical-note)). By the researchers' arithmetic, two standardized attributes correlated at r inflate each weight's variance by 1 / (1 − r²): 1.33 at r = 0.5 (standard errors 1.15 times larger), 1.96 at 0.7 (1.40), 2.78 at 0.8 (1.67) and 5.26 at 0.9 (2.29). That supports the genome report's grouping of attributes before the personal fit, and the full covariance of the previous section is the alternative once there are enough people to estimate it.

Self-selection is the third, Train's paradox at the scale of one person. A list holds what its owner chose, so an attribute they strongly avoid, horror or extreme difficulty, may barely vary on it, and the weight that matters most to them is the one their list cannot reveal. Three remedies follow, all inferences: let the population prior carry the weight for ranges a person never shows, Lenk's bridging; collect stated evidence outside ranking, onboarding exclusions or a "not for me" on a recommendation, read as screening rules in ACBC's sense rather than as weights; and treat Disliked and Dropped items as the genuine low end they are.

The fourth misfit favors Verso. Conjoint's chief weakness, the one Samsung pressed against Hauser, is that choices among hypothetical profiles carry no consequence. Verso's comparisons are judgments of real items after playing or watching them, which is the very quantity Verso predicts, where an item will land once experienced, and that is closer to a revealed preference than conjoint ever gets. The gap runs the other way only for questions asked before experience. A "which would you rather play next?" answer measures anticipated liking, and the discrete-choice literature combines stated and revealed data only with a separate scale factor and constants for each source (Ben-Akiva and Morikawa 1990; Swait and Louviere 1993); Adamowicz, Louviere and Williams found the two looked like different preferences when estimated apart and similar once a relative scale was allowed ([Train, 2009, ch. 7](https://eml.berkeley.edu/books/choice2nd/Ch07_p151-182.pdf)). Any optional question that places nothing would need its own scale in the likelihood.

The fifth is the escape hatches. "Too close" is indifference, a tie in a paired comparison, which the genome report already models with Rao-Kupper's threshold ([Encyclopedia of Mathematics, paired comparison model](https://encyclopediaofmath.org/wiki/Paired_comparison_model)); it is the midpoint of ACA's graded pair and not conjoint's "none" option, which says every shown option falls below something outside the set, closer in Verso to "neither is worth my time", a statement about tiers. "Can't compare" resembles "none" in what it records, a refusal to rank the shown options, but not in why: Brazell and colleagues found choosing "none" tied more to how attractive the set was than to how hard the choice was ([Brazell et al., Marketing Letters 17(4), 2006](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:mktlet:v:17:y:2006:i:4:p:255-268)), while the sources behind the genome report tie incomparability to trade-off difficulty. The same Brazell paper offers a tool. Dual response asks for a forced choice first and then whether the person would really take it; it showed little systematic violation of the logit's assumptions and added power without biasing the estimates, and a 2025 paper reports about 30% better parameter efficiency than a single response ([Hung, Liu, Brazell and Allenby, Marketing Letters, 2025, read through a search summary](https://eccles.utah.edu/news/document/dual-response-in-conjoint-analysis)). By analogy, a soft "if you had to pick?" after "can't compare" would yield a usable answer with its own scale factor (the researchers' inference). It would also change the ranking flow's escape hatch, so it is Rafa's call; the default stays the genome report's treatment, with "can't compare" left out of the likelihood, used as a cannot-link constraint between prototypes, and checked as a free test of whether such pairs sit far apart in attribute space.

## Choosing the next comparison buys little inside binary insertion, and must never read the guess

Adaptive conjoint chooses each question to shrink what is still unknown about a person's part-worths. Toubia, Simester, Hauser and Dahan's polyhedral method picks the question that cuts the set of part-worths consistent with past answers fastest; it was best or tied with few questions, fixed efficient designs were better with many, and a web field test with 330 respondents supported it ([Marketing Science 22(3), 2003](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:22:y:2003:i:3:p:273-303)). The choice-based version designs questions on four criteria, among them choice balance, meaning the options "should be approximately equally likely" ([Toubia, Hauser and Simester, Journal of Marketing Research 41, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.1.116.25082)). The probabilistic version of 2007 added response error and informative priors, was supported by a field experiment with more than 2,200 wine buyers in three countries, and notes that for logit questions the information is greatest "close to, but not perfectly, choice balanced" ([Toubia, Hauser and Garcia, Marketing Science 26(5), 2007](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1060.0257)). Later methods replace the polyhedron with a Gaussian posterior ([Sauré and Vielma, Operations Research 67(2), 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:oropre:v:67:y:2019:i:2:p:315-338)), start each person from collaboratively filtered part-worths and pick questions by active learning over 70 to 100 attribute levels ([Huang and Luo, Marketing Science 35(3), 2016](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:35:y:2016:i:3:p:445-464)), or choose pairwise queries by expected value of information under a Gaussian belief ([Guo and Sanner, AISTATS 2010](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/guo10b.html)). The payoff in designed studies is real: raising utility balance cut the respondents needed for a given error by 10% to 50% across five designs ([Huber and Zwerina, Journal of Marketing Research 33(3), 1996](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1485813)).

In Verso the payoff is far smaller, because the pivot changes what is asked, not what the order teaches. When answers are consistent, binary insertion reaches the same slot whichever eligible pivot it asks about, so the finished list, and the n(n − 1)/2 pairs it implies, does not depend on the pivots (the researchers' analysis). For a 50-item list that is 1,225 implied pairs against at most about 210 logged answers, the base-2 logarithm of 50 factorial, and fewer in practice because questions stay inside a tier (this report's arithmetic). Verso has already measured the property. The batch session's estimate-first search, which starts each insertion from the engine's guess, landed the true order in every one of a thousand trials whether the guesses were right, random or reversed; only the number of questions changed, 9 with right guesses against 17 with reversed ones ([ranking-algorithm.md, measured in PR #72, 2026-10-07](../design/ranking-algorithm.md#estimate-first-then-ask-2026-10-06-later)). Adaptive design, the heart of polyhedral conjoint, therefore buys little for a model fit on the order. It matters in five narrower places: if a model is ever fit on the comparison log rather than the order; when answers are noisy, so that asked pairs are attested and the rest inferred through transitivity; on "too close", which ties the new item to whichever pivot was on screen and so decides both which tie is learned and where the item lands; on a second "can't compare", which places the item mid-range; and in the batch session, where stopping leaves unanswered games in the guessed order.

Binary insertion is also choice-balanced already. The pivot sits at the median of the remaining range, so each answer is close to a coin flip and carries about one bit about position, which is the balance Toubia and Huber obtain from part-worth estimates, obtained here from the person's own order without reading any prediction (the researchers' inference). What binary insertion does not control is contrast: two neighbors in a list may differ on many attributes, which teaches trade-offs, or on none. The researchers' information arithmetic puts a number on the difference. Under a Gaussian posterior with covariance Σ = 0.25 · I on three standardized weights, one logistic answer multiplies the determinant of the posterior precision by 1 + p(1 − p) · Δxᵀ Σ Δx, where Δx is the attribute gap between the two items, and the expected information gained is half the log of that factor. A same-genre pivot with Δx = (0.3, 0.2, −0.1) gives Δxᵀ Σ Δx = 0.035 and gains 0.0044 nats at p = 0.5; a contrasting pivot with Δx = (1.5, −1.0, 0.8) gives 0.97 and gains 0.109 nats, or 0.088 at p = 0.73. The contrasting pivot teaches about 25 times more about the weights, and pays for it in apples-to-oranges questions, which the genome report's sources associate with more "can't compare". Leaving the midpoint costs little if the pivot stays near it: if the new item's final slot is uniform over the eligible range, a pivot splitting the range at fraction q yields H(q) bits per answer, H being binary entropy, so the expected number of questions scales by 1 / H(q), which is 3% more at q = 0.4, 7% at 0.35, 13.5% at 0.3, 23% at 0.25 and 39% at 0.2 (the researchers' arithmetic). Picking the most informative pivot inside the middle 40% to 60% of the range costs about one extra question in thirty.

Whether such a pivot is allowed depends on what it reads. The engine's rule of 2026-10-15 is that the prediction "is written at insertion and never read during it", because a guess that chose the pivot or the starting tier would make the placement lean toward the guess ([recommendation-engine.md](../design/recommendation-engine.md#every-insertion-is-a-prediction-rafa-2026-10-15)). Classic utility balance reads the new item's predicted utility, choosing the pivot the model thinks is closest to the new item, and expected value of information computed from predicted win probabilities does the same; both read the guess, both would also steer a "too close" landing toward it, and both are ruled out in the log flow. A rule that maximizes Δxᵀ Σ_post Δx over the middle band, where Σ_post is the current uncertainty about the person's weights, reads only the attributes and that uncertainty and never the new item's predicted score, while binary insertion supplies the balance. It sits in the same class as the genre-preferring pivot Option A already allows: it influences which question is asked, not where the item may land, except on the "too close" and double "can't compare" branches, where any pivot rule, genre included, already does (the researchers' analysis). The batch session is the one place the guess is read by design, to cut questions; its rows are logged with `components.source` `batch` and reported apart for that reason.

The information pivot pulls directly against the genre pivot: one minimizes contrast and the other maximizes it. Verso would have to choose, or alternate, asking early steps for information and the last step or two apples to apples. That is a ranking-flow decision for Rafa, and this report's default is to leave Option A as it is. The gain is a fraction of a nat per answer on a model that does not yet exist, against slightly longer insertions and harder questions; it is worth revisiting only if the personal scorer earns weight in the blend and its posterior shows weights the lists leave poorly identified.

Adaptive conjoint fits whole in one place: optional questions that place nothing. "Which of these two would you rather play next?" between two backlog items, or a short designed deck at onboarding, can be chosen freely by any of the methods above, or by the BALD rule of collaborative preference Gaussian processes ([Houlsby, Hernández-Lobato, Huszár and Ghahramani, NIPS 2012](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2012/hash/afdec7005cc9f14302cd0474fd0f3c96-Abstract.html)), and their answers go only to the weight model, which respects the decided rule that the backlog never touches ranking. Huang and Luo's design, a population or collaborative estimate first and then questions adapted to the person, is the closest precedent. A conversational version exists too: PEBOL updates Bayesian beliefs over items from a user's utterances, picks what to ask by Thompson sampling or an upper confidence bound, and lets the language model only phrase the question, reaching an MRR@10 of up to 0.27 against 0.17 for the best monolithic model after ten turns, on simulated users ([Austin, Korikov, Toroghi and Sanner, RecSys 2024, arXiv 2405.00981](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00981v2)). Such answers are stated and made before experience, so they enter with their own scale factor, and each is a screen, so it goes through Figma; neither is part of the pilot.

## Coarse features make the case for kNN; embed rich text and keep the tags beside it

One framing point first, the researchers' own: a pretrained text embedding does not improve with more data, because it is frozen. What improves is whatever is learned on top of it, a per-person head, a projection shared across people, or a collaborative model aligned to it. The professor's "improves with more data" is right about that layer, and every method in the next section is a choice of what to learn on top of fixed vectors and how much data the learning needs.

Rafa's premise is right about layer 1. Twenty-three IGDB genres, a developer, a decade and a platform family cannot tell a brutal action RPG from a gentle one, as the genome report says, and a nearest-neighbor search over an embedding of the item's text can. The measured gains are real for films and thin for games. On MovieLens 10M, with item text made of title, description, genres, actors and directors, sentence-transformer embeddings reached a hit rate at 10 of 0.0378 in a metadata-only setting, against 0.0329 for TF-IDF and 0.0071 for an item-kNN baseline; on a collaborative backbone, bag-of-words reached 0.1094, sentence-transformer embeddings 0.1385, embeddings plus posters 0.1401, and embeddings plus MovieLens users' tags 0.2194 ([Monteil et al., MARec, arXiv 2404.13298 v3, 2024-12-10](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13298v3)). By the researchers' arithmetic, embeddings beat bag-of-words by 27% there, crowd tags added 58% on top of embeddings, and images 1%. Language-model-written synopses of 100 to 150 words moved recall and NDCG by about −1.5% to +2.6% for most models on a MovieLens subset ([Nazary et al., ViLLA-MMBench, arXiv 2508.04206, 2025-08-06](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04206)), and one late-2025 study found a model's descriptions mostly restated title and genre ([EmerFlow, arXiv 2512.10370, read through a search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.10370)). For games, the one controlled result used Steam titles plus tags: language-model embeddings gave a "steady but marginal" gain, which the authors put down to titles that say little and tags such as "action" shared by many games; pure embedding similarity trailed on Steam, and its popularity hybrid trailed even the popularity baseline ([Boz et al., arXiv 2402.01339 v2, 2025-01-11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01339)). No study embedded full Steam descriptions or IGDB summaries.

Three conclusions follow, each the researchers' inference. What gets embedded matters more than which model embeds it: titles and generic tags carry little, while IGDB's summary (mirrored today as `game_details.summary`) plus its themes and keywords in one string carries more, and the same goes for TMDB overviews once TMDB answers the terms question. Embeddings complement tags rather than replace them: MARec's jump from tags on top of embeddings and the genome report's two-term design point the same way, so the embedding enters as its own term in the blend, beside layer 1's cosine on idf tags, with its weight fit by the harness. And MovieLens tags are a crowd vocabulary closer to a tag genome than to IGDB's curated keywords, so their 58% does not transfer automatically.

How the kNN term scores matters as much as what it embeds. The genome report's rule stands: score a candidate by its highest similarity to the person's Loved items, or the mean of the top two or three weighted by position, never by similarity to their average. Two preprocessing steps from the embedding literature apply. Pretrained item embeddings are anisotropic, with a secondary summary reporting average cosines above 0.8 between items, and whitening them significantly improved sequential recommendation, though full whitening can erase similarity between items with similar text, so the authors ensemble full and relaxed whitening ([Zhang, Zhou, Zeng and Shen, WhitenRec, ICDE 2024, arXiv 2402.10602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10602)). And recommending by embedding similarity was always best after dimensionality reduction across Amazon Beauty, Delivery Hero and Steam, with PCA always the best configuration and 512 target dimensions beating 128 ([Boz et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01339)). Subtracting the catalog mean and reducing by PCA is the embedding version of the genome report's centered cosine. One result looks like a challenge and is not: Kusano, Abe and Takeoka scored users by the average of cosine similarities to each history item and found max-sum variants generally did not help ([arXiv 2512.13001, 2025-12-15](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13001v1)), but at one to three history items there are no camps of taste to keep apart, and at 20 to 50 there usually are.

The verdict on "we NEED knn": the premise holds, the conclusion is plausible, and the evidence is strongest for films and weakest for games. That makes it a pilot arm, the first one that uses embeddings, and the next section's question is what beats it.

## Five ways past plain kNN, ranked for a dozen testers growing to thousands

The professor's guess that something better than kNN exists is right, and the 2024 to 2026 literature on personalized reward models converges on its form: a Bradley-Terry head on frozen embeddings, with a basis shared across users and each person fitting only a few coefficients. Which version fits Verso depends on how many people have ranked how much. The ranking below is the researchers' synthesis; each step ships only if it beats the one before it in `recommendation_loo` and the paired insertion rows.

| Rank | Method | Learned per person | Data it needs | When for Verso |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bayesian Bradley-Terry head on 4 to 16 principal components of centered embeddings, shrunk toward everyone | 4 to 16 weights | Each person's order; the catalog's text | Now, as a pilot arm |
| 2 | A basis learned across users (LoRe, PReF) in place of PCA | A few coefficients on shared directions | About 20 people with 50 or more ranked items each | A few dozen active users, if step 1 earns weight |
| 3 | Preference Gaussian process on the same reduced features | A utility with calibrated uncertainty | The same as step 1 | Whenever the stretch slot is built |
| 4 | Collaborative hybrids: EASE plus metadata, BPR or a two-tower model with frozen item vectors | User factors from co-occurrence | Hundreds of users whose lists overlap | Under decision 24's existing gate |
| 5 | Sequential and generative recommenders; a language model as the main ranker | Next-item behavior | Hundreds of thousands to billions of sequences | Not on the roadmap |

### First, now: a shrunk per-person head on reduced embeddings

A personal head cannot sit on the raw embedding. By the genome report's simulation, an unshrunk per-person fit needs two to three ranked items per weight before it beats population weights, which at 384 to 1,536 dimensions means about 770 to 4,600 ranked items; a shrunk fit recovers about half the gap to the oracle at three to five items per weight, so a list of 20 to 50 items supports about 4 to 16 personal dimensions (the researchers' arithmetic). The head therefore sits on a projection: P(a above b) = σ(v_h · P(e_a − e_b)), where e_a is item a's embedding, P a k × d matrix of the catalog's top k principal components after centering, and v_h ~ Normal(m, τ²I) the person's k weights. It is the genome report's attribute model with embedding components in place of attributes, and it inherits that report's fixes: implied pairs weighted so the list counts as about n observations, or Plackett-Luce over the whole order; "too close" as Rao-Kupper ties; "can't compare" left out. It is also Compiani and colleagues' demand model at the scale of one person. Evidence that heads on fixed embeddings are cheap and hold up with little data comes from reward modeling: a three-layer network on precomputed embeddings trained in about 28 seconds on one CPU against about 4,755 seconds for a LoRA-tuned Gemma 2B on a V100, and beat it when annotations were few or noisy ([Sun, Shen, Ton and van der Schaar, arXiv 2502.04357, 2025-02-04](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04357v1)). The caveat is the researchers': PCA keeps the directions along which items vary most, which need not be the directions along which taste varies, and only the harness can say whether the top components carry taste. No study fits per-person Bradley-Terry heads on frozen text embeddings from ranked lists of games or films, so the evidence that this beats kNN is indirect.

The head and max-similarity answer different questions, and the researchers argue they belong together. Max-similarity finds items near any one of a person's camps and cannot cancel them; a linear head finds a direction of taste, darker or slower or harder, and can rank items far from every loved item. The first handles the cozy-and-soulslike player; the second generalizes beyond the items already loved. They are two terms in the blend, not rivals.

### Second, at a few dozen users: a basis learned across people

Once enough people have ranked enough, the projection can be learned from everyone's pairs instead of from the catalog's variance. LoRe models each user's reward as a weighted mix of B shared basis rewards, each a linear map of frozen embeddings, with B chosen by cross-validation; for a new user only the mixing weights are fit. On unseen users of the PRISM dataset (643 seen users, about 3.9 examples per new user) it reached 71.0% accuracy against 61.0% for one shared Bradley-Terry model; on Reddit TLDR, with only 20 seen and 20 unseen workers and 50 examples per new user, 66.2% to 66.7% against 60.0% to 64.3% ([Bose et al., LoRe, arXiv 2504.14439, 2025-04](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14439v1)). The paper reports no per-user-only baseline. PReF factors each user's reward over shared base functions, beat a shared reward model with five samples from a new synthetic user, and with about ten responses per user reached a 67% win rate over default GPT-4o responses in a human evaluation ([Shenfeld, Faltings, Agrawal and Pacchiano, ICML 2025, arXiv 2503.06358](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06358v1)). PAL is the camps idea in embedding form, an ideal-point model with a mixture of prototype preference points in a shared space, which generalizes to unseen users from a few examples ([Chen, Chen, Rege and Vinayak, ICLR 2025, arXiv 2406.08469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08469)). A two-tower recommender with a frozen item tower and a user tower pooling the person's ranked items is the same object seen from the recommender side. LoRe's smallest setting, 20 seen users with 100 to 150 examples each, anchors the threshold: about 20 people with 50 or more ranked items, and only if step 1's head earns weight. The cost is a cross-validated rank and a nightly fit over all users' pairs on a CPU.

### Third, for exploration: a preference Gaussian process

A preference Gaussian process puts a GP prior on a person's utility over item features and learns it from pairwise answers; BoTorch's PairwiseGP implements Chu and Ghahramani's 2005 model with a probit or logit likelihood, a Laplace approximation and an RBF kernel by default ([BoTorch, pairwise_gp](https://botorch.readthedocs.io/en/stable/_modules/botorch/models/pairwise_gp.html)). With a linear kernel its posterior mean is the Bayesian Bradley-Terry head of step 1, so it adds value only through a nonlinear kernel, which can represent several camps, and through calibrated uncertainty, which is what the stretch slot's Thompson sampling needs. At Verso's size it is trivial: the Laplace step factors a matrix over the items a person has compared, about 50³ or 125,000 operations at 50 items, milliseconds in a nightly job (the researchers' arithmetic). The kernel belongs on the same centered, reduced features, since an RBF kernel on raw 1,024-dimension vectors suffers the distance concentration the genome report documents. Active learning is where the GP literature shines, by BALD in collaborative GPs or by ordering error bounds in recent work on ordering items in and out of sample ([Bergström, Carlsson, Dubhashi and Johansson, NeurIPS 2024](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/95312)), but inside insertion it would reopen Option A; its two legitimate uses are the stretch slot and, as a proposal, the order of the onboarding deck.

### Fourth, at hundreds of overlapping users: collaborative hybrids

Every collaborative method that uses embeddings learns its mapping from a warm matrix in which many users co-rate many items. BPR's loss is a logistic on score differences, the Bradley-Terry form again; a 2024 replicability study found open-source BPR implementations that lose up to 50% of performance against the paper, and a tuned BPR near the state of the art, 10% ahead of Mult-VAE on NDCG@100 on the Million Song Dataset ([Milogradskii et al., RecSys 2024, arXiv 2409.14217](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14217v2)). Tuned simple baselines are hard to beat in general: six of seven neural recommenders examined in 2019 were often outperformed by tuned nearest-neighbor or graph methods ([Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach, RecSys 2019](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/4581acd8-b5d1-4b3d-8bc8-5982f25b751b/are-we-really-making-much-progress-a-worrying-analysis-of-recent-neural-recommendation-approaches.pdf)). MARec aligns item metadata to a closed-form collaborative backbone (EASE), beats the best cold-start baseline by 8.4% to 53.8%, including Amazon Video Games at a hit rate at 10 of 0.1568 against 0.1231, and trains in 34 seconds on MovieLens 10M and 92 seconds on the video games ([MARec, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13298v3)). AlphaRec maps language-model embeddings of item text linearly into a collaborative model and transfers zero-shot to entirely new datasets ([Sheng et al., ICLR 2025, arXiv 2407.05441](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.05441v4)), which suggests the one collaborative idea that needs no Verso users: learn a text-to-collaborative mapping on public interaction data and test it in the harness as a similarity whose closeness reflects co-consumption rather than wording. That is an offline experiment, and only if the datasets' licenses allow commercial use; the genome report found MovieLens bars it without permission. The data condition rules out the rest for now. With 12 testers each ranking 20 to 50 of about 94,000 items, almost every item pair is co-ranked by zero, one or two people, and a learned mapping would fit noise (the researchers' inference); a Netflix Prize study was titled "even a few ratings are more valuable than metadata" ([Pilászy and Tikk, RecSys 2009](https://bibtex.github.io/RecSys-2009-PilaszyT.html)), but the few ratings have to exist. These methods become plausible at hundreds of users with overlapping lists, under decision 24's gate that neighbors ship only when they beat the baseline.

### Not on the roadmap: sequence models, generative retrieval, and a language model as ranker

Sequential recommenders (SASRec, BERT4Rec), generative retrieval by semantic IDs (TIGER) and Meta's HSTU model what a person consumes next. HSTU's generative recommenders reach 1.5 trillion parameters on a platform with tens of billions of user actions a day ([Zhai et al., ICML 2024, arXiv 2402.17152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17152)); BERT4Rec's published results replicated only with up to 30 times longer training ([Petrov and Macdonald, RecSys 2022, arXiv 2207.07483](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.07483)); and holding TIGER's backbone fixed, no single semantic-ID design was best across datasets ([arXiv 2609.24430, 2026-09-21, read through a search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.24430)). Verso's signal is an order of preference, not a timeline, and its insertion order records when people added items, not when they played them, so a next-item model would learn onboarding habits; the data needs are four to eight orders of magnitude beyond Verso in any case (the researchers' inference). A language model as the main ranker fares no better. In the most direct 2025 comparison, on MovieLens 1M, a jobs dataset and Amazon music, movies and books, text-embedding models beat language-model rerankers cold and warm: in narrow cold start, Qwen3-Embedding-8B reached a recall at 10 of 0.392 against 0.311 for gpt-4.1 and 0.315 for BM25 ([Kusano, Abe and Takeoka, arXiv 2512.13001, 2025-12-15](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13001v1)); Boz and colleagues' language-model re-ranker did worse than the embedding candidates it re-ranked; and zero-shot language-model rankers show position and popularity bias, reduced by ranking shuffled candidates repeatedly ([Hou et al., ECIR 2024, arXiv 2305.08845](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.08845v2)). The engine's split, SQL scores and the model picks three of about thirty and explains them (decision 24), matches the evidence, with the candidates shuffled before they reach Haiku. Language models are competitive near cold start when preferences arrive as language ([Sanner et al., RecSys 2023, arXiv 2307.14225](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14225v1)), which supports the engine's "ask in plain language" surface, not model scoring.

## One personal scorer: conjoint on embedding components and named attributes, beside kNN and Haiku

The two threads describe one object. Conjoint supplies the model and its prior, the embedding literature supplies the features, and Compiani and colleagues' demand model, embedding components with random coefficients in a logit, is the published precedent for joining them. The design below is this report's synthesis; nothing in it is decided, and every term ships only through the harness.

The personal scorer gives person h's utility for item i as s_hi = α_h · p_i + w_h · a_i + v_h · z_i, where p_i is the item's community prior with the person's own ranking removed and standardized; a_i is a handful of named attribute groups, standardized (in the pilot, groups built from IGDB's binary families such as social play, perspective and a few theme clusters; later the genome's graded groups, as ideal-point terms where a middle level can be the favorite); z_i is the first k principal components of the item's centered text embedding; and β_h = (α_h, w_h, v_h) are the person's weights. The prior is β_h ~ Normal(m + Γ c_h, Σ), with c_h the person's coded onboarding answers; at a dozen testers Γ is zero and Σ diagonal, and the full covariance, the mixture of two to four normals and the covariates follow at a few dozen users if the harness rewards them. α_h is constrained to be non-negative. The likelihood is Plackett-Luce over the person's list, or the implied pairs weighted so the list counts as about n observations, with "too close" as Rao-Kupper ties under one threshold per medium and "can't compare" left out. The number of personal dimensions, the length of a_i plus k, follows the genome report's rule of three to five ranked items per weight: about 4 to 7 for a 20-item list and 10 to 16 at 50 (for instance the offset, five attribute groups and eight components, 14 in all), with dimensions beyond that held at the population's weights. The fit runs nightly at the posterior mode by Newton's method, which is convex and takes milliseconds per person, and the Laplace covariance at the mode supplies the draws.

The scorer's output maps onto Verso's scale the way the engine already reads predictions back. The expected number of the person's ranked items that a candidate would beat is r̂_hi = Σ over ranked items j of σ(s_hi − s_hj); divided by the list's length it is a percentile, and the percentile read back through the person's own list gives the two neighbors ("would land between Outer Wilds and Celeste"), the predicted tier and the score (this report's inference). That keeps the scorer on the same footing as content-v1 in the harness, absolute error and tier hit, and gives it pairwise accuracy natively, since σ(s_hi − s_hj) is its probability for each pair.

The kNN term sits beside the scorer, not inside it: the highest cosine between a candidate's centered, reduced embedding and the person's Loved items, or the mean of the top two or three weighted by position, minus γ times the highest similarity to a Disliked or Dropped item, the genome report's local penalty with γ starting at 0.3 to 0.5. It answers whether a candidate is close to any one thing the person loved, which a linear scorer cannot, and it carries the camps of taste until there are enough people to fit a mixture. It also does a second job, retrieval. `suggestion_candidates` scores the 500 best items by prior after its filters, which is exactly where a niche match to one Loved game is lost before any personal term sees it; scoring the union of the prior's top 500 and the kNN's top 200 under the same filters lets the long tail in, which is where the genome report found Pandora's attributes earned their keep (this report's inference).

| Term | The question it answers | Learned from | The reason it can write | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Community prior (layer 0) | How much does everyone like it? | Other users' rankings, external scores | "widely loved" | Built |
| Content profile (layer 1, content-v1) | Does it share this person's genres, developers, decades? | The person's tiers over idf-weighted tags | "three of your Loved games are roguelikes" | Built |
| kNN, max-similarity to Loved | Is it close to any one thing they loved? | Nothing per person; fixed embeddings | "closest to Hollow Knight, which you put in Loved" | Proposed, pilot arm C |
| Personal scorer, prior as offset | Which way does their taste lean, beyond consensus? | The person's order, shrunk toward everyone | "you rank harder games higher than most people do" | Proposed, pilot arm D |
| Genome term | How much of each quality does it have? | Model-scored graded attributes | "slower and cozier than your Loved" | Proposed, pilot arm E |

The blend combines the terms with weights fit by the regression on leave-one-out and insertion rows that the engine's blend already anticipates, so a term that does not help earns a weight near zero without a separate decision. Each new term is written at every insertion into the row's `components`, say `knn_v1` and `pref_v1` beside `content_v1`, computed from the list as it stood and never read during the placement. In leave-one-out, the person's weights are refit without the held-out item and the prior excludes their own ranking; the principal components are fit on catalog embeddings, which contain no rankings, so they leak nothing.

Haiku 5.5 keeps its job unchanged: pick three of about thirty candidates by enum ids, write each reason in the person's own terms, and never compute a score. What changes is the evidence in each candidate's `why` field. The kNN term contributes the nearest Loved item, and the personal scorer contributes its largest named-attribute contributions, the conjoint decomposition of the worked example above, where difficulty supplied +1.6 of a 1.05 gap. Embedding components never appear in a reason, because a component has no name. The exploration slot draws one β_h from the Laplace posterior, scores, and takes the best candidate not already picked, labeled "a stretch" and logged, so the tier it later lands in measures how uncertain the weights were.

The pieces run where the engine already runs things. A nightly Python job on a GitHub Actions schedule, the pattern the IGDB mirror and the engine's stage-5 plan use, fits β_h for every person and medium and writes one row of weights each; the PCA loadings and reduced vectors are written once per embedding run. Scoring stays in SQL over the filtered pool: the personal score is a dot product over about 14 numbers per item, and the expected-rank mapping is 25,000 to 35,000 sigmoids for 500 to 700 candidates against a 50-item list. One scorer per medium, with its own embeddings, components and weights, keeps rule 24: similarities and predictions never cross media.

## What this changes in the pilot

The genome report planned three arms run side by side, content-v1, content-v1 plus IGDB's taxonomies, and content-v1 plus the genome term, with synopsis embeddings proposed as a fourth. Two findings change that plan. The power is thin: by the genome report's arithmetic, a user-level paired test with 12 testers detects only a standardized per-user effect of about 0.9, so a new term must win for nearly every tester to show at all, and five parallel comparisons spend that same dozen five times. And the arms are not independent ideas but layers, each of which only makes sense on top of the one before. The change this report proposes, as its own inference, is a chain of nested arms in a fixed order, each adding one term to its predecessor and each tested paired per user against that predecessor alone. The chain stops at the first arm that fails; later arms can still run and be reported, but as exploration, not as a basis for building. The order puts the arms that need no money, no terms question and none of Rafa's time first.

| Arm | Adds to the arm before | The claim it tests | What it needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Nothing: content-v1 as built, with the prior and population columns the harness already prints | The baseline | Nothing |
| B | IGDB themes, player perspectives, game modes and keywords as binary features in layer 1 | The genome report's cheaper rival: richer free tags close the gap | An IGDB fetch for the ranked games only |
| C | Max-similarity to Loved items on centered, PCA-reduced embeddings of summary plus themes and keywords, with the local penalty near Disliked and Dropped | Rafa's "we NEED knn" | A local embedding run over the ranked games |
| D | The personal scorer: shrunk Bradley-Terry on B's attribute groups and C's components, with the prior as offset | The professor's "something better than kNN" | One Python fit per person, milliseconds each |
| E | The genome's graded groups, in the scorer and as their own similarity term | That graded attributes add accuracy, and reasons people find true | A rubric, model scoring, and Rafa's golden set of about 50 games |

Each arm is scored on the genome report's metrics, per-user pairwise accuracy of each held-out item against the rest of the list first, absolute error and tier hit second, tested at the user level by a randomization test or a bootstrap over users. Arm D also reports against its own population-weights-only version, conjoint's double benchmark, so a win can be traced to personal weights rather than to the components themselves. The genome report's two leakage rules carry over, personal weights refit without the held-out item and the population prior over weights fit on the other testers only, and two are added: the community prior offset computed without the person's own ranking, and the blend weights for each arm fit leaving out the whole tester, so no tester's own list sets the weight used to score them (this report's inference). The genome arm moves to the end because its accuracy case runs mostly through personal weights, which arm D tests first and more cheaply, and because its distinct value, graded reasons and filters such as "short and relaxing tonight", deserves to be judged on its own even at equal accuracy. Rafa's 90-minute golden set therefore waits until arm D reports, a change to the timing of the genome report's question 6. Films stay out of every arm until TMDB answers: the genome report reads embedding TMDB overviews as a machine-learning use under its clause 1.C, and running the model locally does not change the use (this report's reading, not legal advice).

The lecture's transcript, read the same night, sharpens three of these arms; the genome report's [last addendum](attribute-genome.md#addendum-2026-10-29-night-what-the-lecture-adds) has the detail. Arm C runs in two forms, max-similarity to Loved items and a similarity-weighted kNN regressor over the whole list, both falling back toward the prior when the nearest ranked item is far. Arms D and E code graded attributes by level (conjoint's part-worths) or by an ideal point, since a linear weight cannot hold a sweet spot. And a probe set of pairs that share a setting but differ in tone tests whether the genome separates what synopsis embeddings conflate.

The cheapest first step is arms B to D on the games already ranked. Export the testers' ranked games, a few hundred, with their mirrored IGDB summaries; fetch themes and keywords for those games only; embed one string per game locally with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B; and run one Python script that computes the leave-one-out scores of arms B, C and D, reads content-v1's numbers from `recommendation_loo`, and prints the paired per-user differences. A few hundred items at about 300 tokens each is about 100,000 tokens, a minute or two of compute under the researchers' throughput assumption (this report's arithmetic). There is no migration, no money, no text leaving the machine and nothing shown to users, and the engineering is about a day (this report's estimate). The open question is the terms: an open model run on Verso's own machine sends nothing to a third party, but whether stored embeddings count as "derived insights" under the Twitch agreement is the question the IGDB email already asks, and whether to run this offline step before the reply is Rafa's call.

If arm C or D wins, the build needs the whole catalog embedded, and the numbers are small.

The embedding itself costs almost nothing. At 150 to 300 tokens per item, about 94,000 games and films come to 14 to 28 million tokens, which is $0.28 to $0.57 at $0.02 per million tokens with OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small ([OpenAI model page](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/text-embedding-3-small), read 2026-10-29) or Voyage's voyage-4-lite, whose first 200 million tokens are free ([Voyage pricing, updated 2026-09-29](https://docs.voyageai.com/docs/pricing)) (the researchers' arithmetic). The local default is Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B: Apache-2.0, 0.6 billion parameters, up to 1,024 dimensions with any output size from 32, a 32,000-token context, and an MTEB English v2 mean of 70.70 against 75.22 for its 8B sibling ([Hugging Face model card, paper dated 2025-06-05](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B)). The researchers estimate, without measuring, 4 to 9 hours for the whole catalog on the M1 Pro, an overnight run, and under an hour for a nightly increment of about 1,000 items on a CPU-only Actions runner. EmbeddingGemma, 308 million parameters with 768 dimensions truncatable to 128, would take about half as long, and its license was not checked ([Google, arXiv 2509.20354, 2025-09](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20354v3)). An API model matters only if the harness shows a gain worth sending catalog text out.

Storage is the tight constraint. pgvector stores a vector in 4 × dimensions + 8 bytes and a halfvec in 2 × dimensions + 8 ([pgvector README](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)). For about 94,000 items that is 387 MB at 1,024 dimensions, 194 MB as halfvec, 97 MB at 256 dimensions, about 49 MB at 256 dimensions as halfvec, and about 7 MB for the 16 components the scorer reads (the researchers' arithmetic and this report's). Verso's hosted database is on Supabase's Free plan, capped at 500 MB, which by the TMDB sizing note already holds about 70,000 games and 25,000 films with little room to spare ([tmdb-setup.md](../design/tmdb-setup.md); [Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing), read 2026-10-29). The layout that fits is a 256-dimension halfvec for kNN plus the scorer's components in Postgres, with full vectors kept off the database; the Pro plan's 8 GB disk would remove the constraint. Since Boz and colleagues found 512 dimensions beat 128 after PCA, the harness should compare 128, 256 and 512 before the size is fixed, and the `pg_database_size` line the nightly catalog run prints is the number to check first.

Search stays exact, with no index, as the genome report concluded for the genome: max-similarity of 20 to 50 Loved items against a filtered pool of 500 to 700 candidates is 10,000 to 35,000 dot products, a few milliseconds in pgvector, and an exact scan of all 70,000 games at 1,024 dimensions should take a few hundred milliseconds, extrapolated from that report's 26 to 27 ms at 48 dimensions (the researchers' extrapolation). pgvector is not yet enabled in Verso's database; the local image offers 0.8.2 and the latest release is 0.8.7. HNSW indexes vectors up to 2,000 dimensions and halfvecs up to 4,000, but nothing here needs one, and a per-person score cannot use a shared index anyway.

## Inferences to flag

Calls this report makes that Rafa did not, each reversible:

- (a) Conjoint is treated as the same model as the genome report's pairwise logistic, so what it adds is practice (the prior, constraints, validation), not new math.
- (b) The community prior enters the personal scorer as a non-negative offset, computed without the person's own ranking, so the personal weights explain departures from consensus.
- (c) At a dozen testers the population layer is a mean and one spread per dimension; the full covariance, the mixture prior and the onboarding covariates wait for a few dozen active users, and the max-similarity term carries camps of taste until then.
- (d) Onboarding answers (decision 25) as covariates of each person's prior mean, later.
- (e) Attributes with a likely favorite middle level, such as difficulty and pace, as ideal-point terms or three coded levels.
- (f) Avoidance rules such as "I don't play horror" handled as filters from stated evidence, not as learned weights.
- (g) No information-seeking pivot for now: Option A's genre pivot stays, and any pivot rule that reads the predicted score is ruled out in the log flow.
- (h) Optional "which would you rather play next?" pairs, if ever built, feed only the weights, with their own scale factor.
- (i) No soft "if you had to pick?" after "can't compare" by default.
- (j) The personal head on 4 to 16 principal components of centered catalog embeddings, and the thresholds for a shared basis (about 20 people with 50 or more ranked items) and for collaborative hybrids (hundreds of users whose lists overlap).
- (k) One embedded string per game: IGDB summary, themes and keywords.
- (l) The expected-rank mapping of the personal score onto the person's list, tiers and 0 to 10 scale.
- (m) Widening the candidate pool with the kNN term's top 200 beside the prior's top 500.
- (n) The pilot as nested arms in a fixed order, stopping the confirmatory chain at the first that fails, with blend weights fit leaving out the whole tester.
- (o) The genome arm last, and Rafa's golden set after arm D reports.
- (p) Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B run locally as the default embedder, and the offline first step before IGDB replies as acceptable if Rafa agrees.
- (q) The storage layout on the Free plan: a 256-dimension halfvec and the scorer's components in Postgres, full vectors off the database.
- (r) The engineering estimate of about a day for the first step.
- (s) Neural choice models and language-model synthetic respondents left out.
- (t) The component names `knn_v1` and `pref_v1`.
- (u) That running an embedding model locally does not take TMDB overviews outside clause 1.C.
