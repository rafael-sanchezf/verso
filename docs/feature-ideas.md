## 1. Bulk entry

**What Rafa described.** Beli's flow is "I just ate at this restaurant, let me rank it." Verso's first use is different: a new user arrives with years of games behind them and wants to rank their top 30 in one sitting. Today that is thirty trips through Search, the add-to sheet, the game page, the tier sheet, the comparisons, and the confirm step. He wants three taps per game at most: search, tap the game, pick the platform, rank. He also flagged that we have never said how ranking works when there is nothing to compare against.

**Where it sits.** V1, first item; a candidate for building before the three-friends test, because the test starts from an empty ranking and this is the first thing every real user will do.

**What exists.** More than it looks:

- The engine already answers the cold-start question. `startInsertion` in [packages/core/src/ranking.ts](../packages/core/src/ranking.ts) places the first game of a tier with no comparison at all, the second costs one, and the k-th costs about log2(k). There is no "nothing to compare to" state to design; the first game of each tier just lands.
- The cost that remains is the comparisons themselves, and they are close to optimal already: thirty games spread over the four tiers (say 10, 10, 7, 3) cost about 67 comparisons whichever order they are entered in, plus thirty tier picks. Binary insertion is within a constant of the information-theoretic minimum, so no algorithm halves that; what bulk entry removes is the overhead around each game (sheets, navigation, the per-game confirm step), not the questions. Ranking is the product, so the questions stay.
- An entry can already be Played with no ranking row: `user_entries` and `ranking_entries` are separate tables. "Played but not ranked" is a valid state today, and it is exactly what the JSON import produces (import writes entries only, item 9) and what the Steam library's candidates become when accepted (item 24).

**Sketch.**

1. A multi-select mode in Search ("Add many"): tapping a result adds it as Played at once, with no sheet; a counter chip shows "12 added"; the platform is asked only when IGDB lists more than one and the Steam link has not already answered it (ownership is a separate fact and can be skipped entirely, rule 19). This is the three taps.
2. One list behind it: "played, not ranked yet", a `packages/api` read over `user_entries` where status is Played and no `ranking_entries` row exists for the item. It shows on Ranking as a banner ("14 played games to rank") and on Home after a Steam import or a JSON import, so bulk entry, import, and the Steam candidates all drain through the same door.
3. A ranking session over that list: tier sheet, comparisons, next game, with no confirm step per game. `finishInsertion` stays the one write per game, so a session interrupted halfway loses nothing. At the end, one review screen shows the tier list with drag to fix, which is the confirm step's job done once instead of thirty times.
4. Two orderings are possible inside the session and only Rafa can pick: per game (tier, then its comparisons, then the next game, which keeps the game you just tapped in mind) or tiers first (swipe every game into a tier, one tap each, then all the comparisons in one run). Per game is the literal reading of "three taps then rank"; tiers first batches the easy decisions.

**Cost.** No migration, no engine change. One read in `packages/api`, a Search mode, a session wrapper around the existing log flow, and the review screen. It is a new flow, so it goes through Figma first (the add-many state of Search, the banner, the session's progress indicator, the review). Roughly one screen agent's PR plus a Figma pass.

**Open.** Per game or tiers first; whether to ship it before the three-friends test; whether the banner belongs on Ranking, Home, or both.

**Second pass (Rafa, 2026-09-25): the deck.** Rafa described the first-run version in full and asked for pushback before any Figma work. His description is decoded here against the code and the earlier decisions; nothing below is decided until he answers questions 8 to 12 at the end, and question 2 is read as answered by it.

*What Rafa described.* A guided flow rather than a search. Ask which consoles you own. Ask what kind of games you like, answered by naming a couple of games rather than picking genres. Then suggest a long list of the most popular games on those consoles, weighted toward the kinds you named but not limited to them: a Switch owner who never said "platformer" should still be asked about Mario Odyssey, because it sold enough that they probably played it. Console is a hard constraint, taste a soft one. Ranking each game should be as light as it can be: say a tier, get a score, move on, compare with what came before, until there is a good sample. The flow stays open after first run, remembers the platforms, and lets you add one. Account links (Steam, PlayStation, Nintendo, Xbox, the app stores) would fill the list automatically if that is possible at all.

*What stands from the first sketch.* The "played, not ranked yet" list, the ranking session over it with no confirm step per game, and the one review screen at the end. The multi-select Search mode stays as the second door, for people who know exactly what they played.

*The shape, decoded, with the pushback in place.*

1. **Where have you played, not what do you own.** Decision 19 (2026-09-22) says a ranked game was played, not necessarily owned; someone who sold their PS4 still has thirty PS4 games to rank. So the first question is "Where do you play, now or before?", and its answer is a fact on the profile (a `platforms` column on `profiles`, new), not on an entry. It pre-fills the entry's `played_platforms` (PR #59) when a game exists on only one of them, and it is what the Ranking platform filter should list (phone entry 4). Inferred from decision 19; flagged.
2. **Families, with backward compatibility.** The picker offers families (PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo, PC, mobile) with the generations under them, and picking PS5 pulls in PS4 titles, Xbox Series pulls in One and most 360, Switch 2 pulls in Switch, PC covers every store. Without this a PS5 owner is never asked about Bloodborne. A static map over IGDB's platform names, which `game_details.platforms` already holds.
3. **The seed games are the first ranked games.** "Name three games you love", through search, adds them as Played and puts them first in the deck; there is no genre picker. They give the soft preference (their genres and developers) and they are the first items of the recommendation engine's content profile, which starts working at about five ranked items. Asking for taste and then asking about the same games again would be the survey twice.
4. **The deck.** Candidates: catalog games on the chosen platforms with no entry for the user, ordered by popularity with a boost for the seed games' genres. One card at a time: cover, title, year, platforms; three answers: **Played it**, **Want to play**, **Skip**. Played opens the tier sheet in place, the comparisons follow on the same surface, then the next card. Want to play adds to the backlog and moves on; the deck never ranks anything unasked and the backlog stays the user's own list (rule 35's spirit, rule 11's order). Skip is remembered for the session only. This is Beli's "been / want to go" onboarding with the platform in place of the city, and the second answer is what Rafa's description left out: the popular games you have not played are the best backlog the app could offer, and without it the Next tab is empty when the deck ends.
5. **A goal instead of "a good sample size".** Ten ranked games is where the engine's confidence label leaves "needs more data" ([design/recommendation-engine.md](design/recommendation-engine.md), five for the content profile, medium confidence up to fifteen). The session shows progress toward ten with "unlocks your taste profile" and keeps going past it with no cap. Inferred; flagged.
6. **No new ranking mode.** "Say a tier, get a score, compare with the one before" is what `startInsertion` already does for a small tier: the first game lands without a question, the second costs one, the k-th about log2(k), and the score is read off the position. A chain of comparisons against the previous game only would not produce an order, so the engine stays as it is; thirty games cost about 67 answers, roughly two per game, plus a tier tap each. What the deck removes is everything around the questions, which is where the thirty trips through the sheets went.
7. **Editions and series collapsed.** A popularity list from IGDB shows a remaster beside its original and eight entries of one yearly series. The deck shows one card per game (IGDB's `parent_game` and `version_parent` fold editions in; the mirror does not keep them yet) and one card per series with "which ones?" expanding to the entries. A mirror change; small.
8. **Popularity per platform.** `popular_games` orders by IGDB's `total_rating_count`, which skews to PC and indie titles; filtered by platform it is probably right for the top hundred on each console and costs one SQL function (`deck_candidates(p_platforms, p_genres, p_limit)`, excluding the caller's entries). IGDB's PopScore primitives (visits, want to play, playing, played: four native types since June 2024, refreshed daily, every platform; Steam-based types announced, not confirmed shipped) are the truer console signal and are a follow-up in the mirror if the Switch deck looks wrong.
9. **Two doors, one drain.** First run leads with the deck (first-run B's two dead cards go, which the ui-brief already leaned against); Ranking's empty state and Search carry the permanent doors, "Rank popular games" and "I know what I played" side by side. Everything drains through "played, not ranked yet". Reopened later, the deck skips what already has an entry and shows the profile's platforms with "Add a platform".
10. **A Steam-linked deck.** For a PC player the first card offers "Link Steam"; once linked, the deck is the Steam library ordered by playtime, a game over about two hours presented as "you played this for 40 h" with only the tier to answer, and the rest as candidates. The backend exists (item 24); the link button and the privacy checklist are the screens still owed, coded from conventions by decision 16.

One inference from "it should let you go past ten, maybe after a couple of games": progress toward ten shows from the first card, at ten the app says the taste profile is unlocked and offers to stop or keep going, and it does not ask again after that; a way out sits on every card. *The compatibility map, exact.* A generation folds in an older one only where the console plays that whole library by default, and the chip says so ("PS5 · with PS4 games") so it can be unticked. Folded in: PS5 with PS4; Xbox Series with Xbox One; Switch 2 with Switch; Wii U with Wii; 3DS with DS; PS2 with PS1. Never folded in, because the compatibility is partial or depends on the model, so the older console is its own pick: PS3 on PS4 or PS5 (PS4 has no backward compatibility at all, and a streaming subscription does not count as playing on the console); PS2 on PS3 (early models only); Xbox 360 and the original Xbox on One and Series (a curated list); GameCube on Wii (early models only). PC means Windows, with Mac and Linux as their own picks and Steam Deck counted as PC; mobile is iOS and Android; every family ends with an "older" pick for the rest. Classics played through Nintendo Switch Online or the PlayStation classics catalog are catalogued by IGDB under their original platform, so a Switch pick does not surface Ocarina of Time and the older pick does. The map is one static table in `packages/core` with a test per row.

*Popularity, revised (Rafa, 2026-09-28).* Sales first where a publisher prints them, then IGDB. Confirmed the same day ([research/game-data-sources.md](research/game-data-sources.md), section 14): Nintendo's investor site prints life-to-date units for its top six to ten titles per platform every quarter, plain HTML; Wikipedia keeps a best-selling list per platform (Switch, Switch 2, PS5, PS4, PC, Xbox One; none for Series X|S) of about fifty rows each, compiled from publisher announcements and readable through the MediaWiki API; Wikidata's units-sold property is patchy and only a supplement; Sony and Microsoft print nothing usable, and Circana is a rank without units. So the deck orders by units where known, which covers the head of every console's list, and by IGDB's signals below that, and the row keeps its source. The ingest is one `title_sales` table fed quarterly from Nintendo and monthly from Wikipedia, matched to the catalog by title and year. The Figma round does not wait on it.

*Account links, honestly.* Checked 2026-09-25 against [research/game-data-sources.md](research/game-data-sources.md), sections 4 to 7 and the addendum of that date for the stores the research had not covered:

- **Steam**: official (OpenID login, the Web API); the backend exists (item 24), the link button and privacy checklist are owed. The one accelerator, and it goes into the deck (point 10).
- **PlayStation**: no official API. The unofficial mobile-app route needs an NPSSO cookie that its own maintainers call equivalent to a password, with a stated ban risk; Backloggr ships it on the App Store without saying how. Deferred; if built, the read-only "your PSN ID, public profile" mode first, as the research recommends.
- **Xbox**: OpenXBL, a paid third-party proxy (free at 150 requests an hour, 5 USD a month for 500); whether a user can sign in through it rather than the app holding one key is unconfirmed. Deferred.
- **Nintendo**: nothing usable; the parental-controls API gives per-day minutes from the day a console is linked. The deck is the substitute, which is the reason the deck matters more than any link.
- **Epic, GOG**: no library API for third parties; Heroic and Legendary use reverse-engineered endpoints (Epic's with hardcoded launcher credentials). No.
- **Apple App Store, Game Center, Google Play**: nothing across developers. An iOS app cannot enumerate installed apps; StoreKit and Game Center are scoped to the calling app; Screen Time needs a parental-control entitlement and returns opaque tokens; Play Games Services is per app. No.
- **Battle.net, EA, Ubisoft**: per-game profile scopes at most, no library. No.
- **The PC catch-all**: Playnite exports a JSON of every PC store through a community extension, and `importEntries` already reads a file of our own format, so accepting Playnite's file is a small mapping. Later, if a PC player asks.

So the deck is the universal path, Steam is the only link worth wiring in now, and for every other platform the app says so plainly rather than promising a sync it cannot do.

*The session's arithmetic, in short.* Ordering n games inside one tier needs at least ceil(log2 n!) comparisons (4 games: 5; 6: 10; 10: 22), and binary insertion, which the engine already does, is within a few of that (4: 5; 6: 11; 10: 25). Ten played games spread as 3 Loved beside the 3 seeds, 4 Liked, 2 Fine, 1 Didn't like cost about 8 + 5 + 1 + 0 = 14 comparisons however they are batched, roughly 1.4 per game, and nothing across tiers is ever asked because the tier tap already answered it. Five comparisons therefore cannot order ten games (five answers distinguish 32 orders; one tier of four needs five on its own); what five buy is a pass that pairs every game with one other and settles the top of the biggest tier. So the session asks in value order with a soft target of about one comparison per game, lets the user stop whenever they like, and fills what is left from the community prior as provisional positions the review and later comparisons correct. What the batch removes against today's loop: per game, a sheet to open and a tier to tap become one chip tap, the confirm step becomes one review, and the navigation between games disappears; about seven taps a game become about two and a half.

## 2. Time to finish

**What Rafa described.** Fourteen hours into a game whose average time to beat is twenty-five: "about 11 h to finish." Better still for film and TV ("3 h 20 left in this season") and books (pages or chapters left).

**Where it sits.** V1 for games, cheap; V3 and V4 for the other media as part of their progress pointers.

**What exists.** Every input is already stored. `game_details` carries the time-to-beat triple (hastily, normally, completely; item 6) and `user_entries` carries `playtime_minutes` with its source (manual, or Steam through the link). The media brief already gives every medium one time figure (its decision 7: "~25 h" for games, episodes times runtime for TV, pages times 1.1 minutes for books) and draws a "done by Oct 3" pace variant for books (Home B).

**Sketch.** A pure function in `packages/core`: remaining = max(0, time to beat normally minus playtime), rounded to the half hour, null when either side is missing so the UI says nothing rather than something wrong (rule 21, data honesty). Shown as "~11 h left" on the Playing hero and small cards, on the game page beside "~25 h", and as a backlog sort key beside time to beat. Past the average, the label flips to "past the average, 3 h over" rather than a negative number. Steam's two-week playtime, already imported, gives a pace, so the pace-led variant the books brief drew ("~40 pages a day, done by Oct 3") works for games with no new data: hours per day over the last fourteen days, remaining divided by that. For TV, episodes left times runtime once the episode pointer exists; for books, pages left times 1.1 minutes.

**Cost.** Tiny: one function with tests, a line on three screens. Worth pairing with a Figma touch on the Playing card only if the label does not fit the drawn hero.

**Open.** Whether "completely" replaces "normally" when the user has marked a completionist intent. Not needed for the first version.

## 4. Progress tracker

**What Rafa described.** "I am on season six, episode nine of How I Met Your Mother." Ideally pulled from Netflix or Hulu automatically; entered by hand if not.

**Where it sits.** V3; the manual half is already designed.

**What exists.** The media structure round (2026-09-16) designed the manual half: TV is ranked as a show and tracked by episode, a season-and-episode pointer on the entry, an `episode_progress` table under it, "mark season watched" and "watched up to here" writing the rows, a seasons screen with a check per episode, and "up to date" derived ([design/media-brief.md](design/media-brief.md), its decision 5). Books get a page pointer the same way. For games the equivalent already runs: Steam playtime arrives through the link and `started_at` is stamped by a trigger.

**The automatic half, honestly.** No streaming service exposes viewing history to third parties. The research ([research/film-tv-data-sources.md](research/film-tv-data-sources.md)) found Netflix's export is a title-and-date CSV with no ids and deferred it; Hulu, Disney+, Max, and Prime Video publish nothing; Trakt is the only service with per-episode history and an API, and since August 2026 an app on it needs a Trakt VIP account; TV Time shut down in July 2026 and deleted its users' history. Trakt itself only fills automatically from Plex, Jellyfin, Kodi, Infuse, or desktop browser extensions, which is to say from people who run their own media servers. So "updates automatically" is, for the audience Verso wants, not buildable from the services' side, and the honest product is the manual pointer made very cheap plus imports for the few who have them.

**Sketch for making manual cheap.** The seasons screen's one-tap check is the baseline. Cheaper still: an iOS Shortcut and Siri phrase through App Intents ("I watched an episode of Severance") that advances the pointer by one; a Home widget with a "watched the next one" button (a widget is already under Later); the "New episodes" checklist the TV Home B variant draws, which is the same tap surfaced where the user already is. Imports: Trakt OAuth (disclosed as needing VIP) for history, Netflix CSV later if users ask, both already ordered in the V3 list.

**Cost.** Nothing new beyond V3's plan except the Shortcut, which is a small native addition once the pointer write exists.

## 5. Episode and aspect ranking

**What Rafa described.** One level deeper than ranking a show: rank its episodes. For games, rank the story, the gameplay, the art, separately. Compare your own episode ranking with the IMDb ranking people post online. Far out and undecided.

**Where it sits.** Episodes: late V3. Aspects: Later.

**What exists.** The engine is generic: `startInsertion` takes any list of `RankableItem` and a tier, so episodes of one show rank with the code that ranks games. Episodes are not rows yet; the film and TV catalog design fetches them on demand and that pass is still to come. IMDb's per-episode ratings are the famous ones but are licensed for personal use only, so they are not shown anywhere in Verso (media brief decision 1, research); TMDB carries a per-episode vote average, sparse but usable and already the labeled community figure for the rest of V3.

**Sketch, episodes.** Rankings keyed by medium today; an episode ranking adds a scope, the parent show, so `ranking_entries` and `comparisons` gain a nullable `scope_media_item_id`. Rank within a season, not the whole show: a season is at most about twenty-four items, so a full season costs about 60 comparisons and a single episode costs four, on the seasons screen right after the check. The community figure beside it is TMDB's episode vote, labeled "TMDB," and the IMDb episode page is a link out through `external_ids.imdb_id`. Sharing a season heatmap (your order against TMDB's) is the shareable image, the way the collection wall is for games.

**Sketch, aspects.** Full per-aspect rankings multiply the comparison burden by the number of aspects, so they must be opt-in per game and the score badge stays the overall one. The schema change is an `aspect` column on `ranking_entries` and `comparisons` defaulting to `overall`, and the prompt is already a parameter ("Which story did you enjoy more?"). The cheaper first step, and the recommendation: highlights, one-tap tags on the note step of idea 3 ("Story," "Gameplay," "Art," "Music," "Multiplayer"), which say what stood out without another ranking, show on the row, and feed the suggestions of idea 9 with the same signal at a fraction of the taps.

**Cost.** Episodes: a migration with policy tests, the on-demand episode fetch, and a seasons-screen flow, medium. Highlights: small. Full aspect rankings: medium, and only if people ask.

**Open.** Highlights first, or full aspect rankings straight away.

## 9. AI suggestions, personal and group

**What Rafa described.** The end state has two layers. On the surface, a catalog of everything you like across media. Underneath, AI insights: "you know my taste, what should I play, what should I play in this genre," and for groups, "my girlfriend and I want an action movie tonight, what fits both of us," "we want a multiplayer game for four." He calls this the backbone of the value and the thing a paid tier would meter.

**Where it sits.** Personal suggestions in V1 (the existing bullet, reframed); group suggestions in V2, because they need friends' lists.

**What exists.** The signal is the moat and it is already stored. Every comparison is an event (the decision that most protects the future, per the data model), tiers and positions give a full order, notes say why, playtime and Dropped say what did not hold, and genres come with every catalog row. No other tracker has pairwise preferences; star ratings are a much weaker taste model. The V1 bullet was written as a query, not a model, and that first half stays.

**Sketch.** Two stages, so the model never sees the whole catalog and the cheap part does most of the work:

1. Candidates in SQL. Items the user has no entry for, filtered by the request (medium, genre, time available against time to beat or runtime, platform owned, on the user's streaming services, multiplayer modes for a group), scored by overlap with the user's Loved and Liked tiers on genre and developer and by what the user's comparisons preferred. A `security invoker` function returning thirty rows. For games this needs IGDB's `game_modes` and `multiplayer_modes` mirrored; the mirror keeps genres and platforms today and drops the rest.
2. The model picks and explains. An Edge Function sends the candidates with the user's top and bottom tiers, recent notes and highlights, and the request, and asks for three picks with a one-line reason grounded in the user's own list ("you put Hades in Loved and dropped two open-world games at ten hours, so..."). The Claude API key lives in Supabase secrets; the app never calls the model directly. Model choice and prompt design happen when it is built, with the current pricing in hand rather than from memory.
3. Group. The same two stages over several users' lists: candidates none of them has an entry for, scored so every member's predicted enjoyment clears a floor rather than maximizing one person's, and the reason names each member ("she loved Everything Everywhere, you loved Edge of Tomorrow"). Members are friends from V2, whose lists are already visible to followers, so no new exposure; for a partner who is not on Verso, a one-off session link where they pick five favorites from a grid gives the model enough to work with and doubles as an invitation.
4. A meter from day one: `suggestion_requests` (user, kind, model, tokens, created at), which is the rate limit, the cost report, and, when the time comes, the paid tier's counter, so the gate of idea 10 is a config value and not a feature.

**Cost.** Stage 1 is a function with pgTAP and a mirror change, medium. Stage 2 is an Edge Function with Deno tests and the first secret for a model key, medium. The screens ("Suggest," the request sheet with genre, time, and group, the three-pick result) go through Figma. Group adds the session link, small once friends exist.

**Open.** Nothing that blocks V1's personal version; the group version waits for friends.

**Thinking, 2026-09-24.** The research behind this sketch is in [research/recommendation-engine.md](research/recommendation-engine.md) and the design that follows from it, with worked numbers, in [design/recommendation-engine.md](design/recommendation-engine.md); nine questions there wait for Rafa. The sketch stands, refined in three places. The personal number is a neighbor prediction on rank percentiles read back through the user's own list ("would land between Outer Wilds and Celeste"), which is Criticker's published method plus shrinkage for small overlaps, a confidence label, and a content profile for the first months, rather than a query over genre overlap alone. The group rule is the floor kept here plus a penalty on disagreement (average minus 0.2 times the gap for a pair), a guest with five picks is shrunk toward the item's average rather than down-weighted (weighting hands the choice to the host), the query needs every member's prediction for the whole filtered candidate set, and the rule runs in SQL, never in the model. The model's job is to pick three among about thirty SQL candidates by fixed candidate ids, so it cannot name a title outside the list, and to write a reason that cites the user's own items. A prediction log and a leave-one-out harness come first, because every constant is a guess until there is data. Where the language model fits, since the engine is mostly arithmetic (Rafa's question the same day): ask in plain language, the reason line, your taste in a paragraph, the group card, the catalog read into mood tags and embeddings, notes into highlights, a model as judge; each is placed at a stage in [design/recommendation-engine.md](design/recommendation-engine.md#where-the-ai-is), none scheduled.

**Decided 2026-09-30:** the engine is the product (decision 21 in [ROADMAP.md](../ROADMAP.md#decisions)) and the beta ships the finder from the user's own list (decision 24).
